## Verified facts

### Shape of the test

| Fact | Status |
|---|---|
| 4 parts, 10 questions per part, **40 questions** total | VERIFIED [T1: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Listening test format] |
| **1 mark per correct answer**, no weighting between parts or question types | VERIFIED [T1: ielts.org — Listening test format] |
| Approximately **30 minutes** of audio | VERIFIED [T1: ielts.org — Listening test format; T1: ielts.org — Teacher's guide to IELTS, p.6] |
| Raw score converted to a band in **whole or half bands** (e.g. 5.5, 7.0) | VERIFIED [T1: ielts.org — Listening test format] |
| The Listening paper is **identical for Academic and General Training** | VERIFIED [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs, p.3] |
| Each recording is heard **ONCE only** | VERIFIED [T1: ielts.org — Listening test format; T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs p.3] |

The prior-work hypothesis "4 parts × 10 questions = 40; ~30 min" is **confirmed**.

### The four parts

| Part | Context | Speakers | Official wording |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Everyday / social | **Two** — a conversation | "a conversation between two people set in an everyday social context" [T1: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test format] |
| 2 | Everyday / social | **One** — a monologue | "only one person speaks", e.g. a speech about local facilities [T1: ielts.org — Listening test format] |
| 3 | Educational / training | **Two to four** — a conversation | ielts.org says "a conversation between two main speakers"; British Council says "a conversation between **up to four people** set in an educational or training context" |
| 4 | Educational / training | **One** — a monologue | "a monologue on an academic subject, e.g. a university lecture" [T1: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test format] |

The prior-work hypothesis "Parts 1–2 everyday/social, Parts 3–4
educational/training" is **confirmed** [T1: ielts.org — Listening test format;
T1: ielts.org — Teacher's guide to IELTS, p.6].

**Nuance the book must carry:** the two official sites give *different* speaker
counts for Part 3. ielts.org says two main speakers; British Council says up to
four. They are not contradictory — a tutorial can have two dominant voices plus
others — but the reader should train for **up to four voices in Part 3**, i.e.
the harder case. Official sample Matching Task 1 in the sample-tasks PDF is a
two-speaker Part 3 (Jack + Dr Ray); Cambridge practice material commonly runs
three (tutor + two students).

### Pauses, reading time and what you hear before each part

All from [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs, p.3]:

- **Before each part** you hear a spoken description of the situation — who is
  speaking, where, and the general topic. **This is not printed on the question
  paper.** If you tune out during it you lose free orientation.
- There is a **pause between each part**.
- There is **one break inside Parts 1, 2 and 3** so you can read the next set of
  questions.
- **Part 4 is the exception.** The official FAQ says: "In Part 4 there is a short
  pause in the middle of the recording to allow you time to refocus if required,
  but this is **not mentioned on the recording**. The time for reading **all**
  Part 4 questions is given **at the start** of the part."
- "Will I have time to check my answers at the end of each section? **Yes**."

**Correction to prior work.** `knowledge/tricks.md` says "Part 4 is usually a
single academic lecture with **no break**." That is *nearly* right and is the
right thing to train for, but it is not literally what the official FAQ says.
The accurate statement is:

> Part 4 gives you **all** your reading time up front, for all ten questions, and
> gives you **no announced break** in the middle. There is a brief unannounced
> pause mid-recording, but it is a moment to refocus, not reading time — do not
> plan to use it.

Tier 2/3 sources converge on the practical version: "there is no break in the
middle of the audio, and test takers will not get time in the middle of the
recording to read through the next set of questions" [T3-discovery, multiple
prep sites]. Promoted under rule (a): it is consistent with the Tier 1 fact that
all Part 4 reading time is given at the start, and the Tier 1 fact that the pause
is unannounced.

### Answers come in order — settled, and stronger than expected

> "Are the questions in the same order as the information in the recording?
> **Yes. This is true for all question types in IELTS Listening.**"
> [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs, p.3]

Independently: "Questions follow the chronological order of information
presented" [T1: ielts.org — Academic test format in detail]. And officially
listed as a debunked myth: "The audio conversation will always follow the order
of questions in the test" [T1: ielts.idp.com — 9 myths about the IELTS Listening
test].

The prior-work hypothesis is **confirmed, for every question type including
matching and map labelling.** This is a stronger claim than most prep books make
and it is worth stating loudly, because it licenses the single most valuable
recovery tactic in the test (see Strategies).

**Critical qualification the book must not omit** — the *questions* are in order;
the *answer options* are not. Within a multiple-choice item, options A, B and C
are not mentioned in A-B-C order in the audio [T2: IELTS Liz — Listening
multiple choice essential tips]. The official Multiple Choice sample confirms
this: for Q9 the speaker lists "Premium … Standard … Economy" — i.e. **C, B, A**
— and the answer is C. Likewise in the official Matching 2 (hotels) task the
five hotels are described in the order Carlton House / Imperial / Royal Oak /
Bridge / Majestic, while questions 1–4 answer E, B, C, A. [DERIVED from T1:
ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, pp. 8–9, 22–25]

### Accents

| Source | Accent list |
|---|---|
| [T1: ielts.org — Listening test format] | "British, Australian, New Zealand and North American" |
| [T1: ielts.org — Teacher's guide to IELTS, p.26] | "a mix of accents from **Australia, Canada, New Zealand, the UK, and the USA**" |
| [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.4] | "Australian, British, New Zealand and North American speakers" |
| [T1: ielts.idp.com — 9 myths] | "British, Australian, New Zealand, American and Canadian accents" |

**All four agree.** "North American" resolves to US **and Canadian**. The prior
hypothesis is confirmed and can be sharpened: the book should name **five**
national varieties — UK, Australia, New Zealand, USA, Canada — because a reader
who has only trained on RP and General American is under-prepared for the
Australian and New Zealand vowel shifts that carry the most Part 2/Part 4 load.

Note the older British Council Test format page describes the speakers as "native
English speakers"; the FAQ says "native-speaker accents". Nothing official
promises a non-native accent will appear, so train on the five above.

### Scoring and negative marking

- **There is no negative marking.** "Try to answer all questions; **you will not
  lose points for incorrect answers**." [T1: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test
  day advice]. Reinforced by "Try to answer all the questions, even if you don't
  feel sure about an answer — you may have understood more than you think."
  [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.2]
- The prior-work hypothesis "Never leave a blank / no negative marking" is
  **confirmed**.
- Approximate marks needed (already verified in `knowledge/sources.md` and
  re-confirmed as an official planning figure, not a guarantee): **30/40 → band
  7**, 23/40 → band 6, 35/40 → band 8. Conversion varies slightly by test
  version.

**Band-7 arithmetic the reader needs in front of them:** 30 of 40 means you can
afford **10 losses across the whole paper**. Part 1 should cost you 0–1. That
budget framing is the most useful single number in the chapter.

### One rule that quietly destroys scores

> "Don't write more than one answer when only one is required. **Even if one of
> your answers is correct, you will not receive a mark.**"
> [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs, p.3, Listening Don'ts]

This is repeated verbatim for Reading on p.5. It is not on any of the HTML format
pages. It kills the very common candidate habit of writing `4.15/4.30` or
`library/town hall` in one gap when unsure. **Hedging in a gap scores zero.**

### Paper vs computer — verified precisely

| | **Paper** | **Computer** |
|---|---|---|
| Audio | ~30 min | ~30 min |
| After the audio | **10 minutes** to transfer answers to the answer sheet | **No transfer time**; **2 minutes** to check answers |
| How you answer | Write on the question paper during the audio, then transfer in **pencil** | **Type directly** into the on-screen boxes as you listen |
| Where marks come from | **Only the answer sheet is marked.** "Anything that you write on your question paper will not be marked" | What is in the box |
| Legibility | "If an answer isn't clear on your answer sheet, you will lose the mark" | Not a risk |
| Audio delivery | Some centres use **room speakers**, not headsets | Own computer, own headphones, **volume control on screen** |
| Notes | Pen and paper provided for notes | Pen and paper provided for notes; plus on-screen notes tool |

Sources: [T1: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test day advice] (10 min, pencil,
question paper not marked); [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.2] (10 min "which
is plenty of time"; write clearly; don't copy words from around the gap);
[T1: ielts.idp.com — How IELTS on computer works] (**no extra transfer time; only
2 minutes at the end to review**; volume control top right; navigation to all 40
questions; forward/back arrows; a **Review button** that flags a question with a
circle for later checking); [T1: ielts.idp.com — IELTS on computer vs IELTS on
paper] (answers typed into boxes; own headphones); [T1: ielts.idp.com — 9 myths]
(myth 4: **not every centre gives headsets** — some use speakers).

The prior-work hypothesis "paper gives 10 min transfer, computer gives NO
transfer and only 2 min to check" is **confirmed**.

**Two official inconsistencies to be aware of (do not repeat them in the book):**

1. IDP's "9 myths" page says the only computer/paper difference is *how you input
   answers*. That is contradicted by IDP's own "How IELTS on computer works"
   page (10 min vs 2 min). Follow the specific page over the marketing page.
2. IDP's vs-paper page says computer order is Listening→Reading→Writing and paper
   is Writing→Reading→Listening. No other official source corroborates a
   Writing-first paper order, and it conflicts with the standard L-R-W sequence.
   **Flagged for verifiers — do not put a paper section order in the book on this
   evidence alone.**

**Computer-specific on-screen tools** [T1: ielts.idp.com — How IELTS on computer
works; T1: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS on computer]: adjustable font
size and background colour, volume control, on-screen timer, note-taking tool,
highlight/underline, free navigation between questions, Help button, word count
(Writing only), and a stopwatch that flashes at 10 minutes and 5 minutes
remaining (Reading/Writing).

### Recent changes

- **From mid-2026 IELTS stops being offered on paper**; all tests move to
  computer delivery, with timelines varying by market. Listening and Reading are
  taken on computer even where the new **"Writing on Paper"** option exists.
  [T1: ielts.org — Updates to IELTS test delivery; T1: ielts.idp.com — IELTS test
  delivery updates for 2026; T1: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Updates to how
  IELTS tests are delivered]
- **The Listening test itself is unchanged.** The updates "do not change the
  IELTS skills assessed, the test construct, or the way results should be
  interpreted." No change to parts, question types, timing or marking was found
  in any official source.
- **Practical consequence for this reader:** train exclusively for the
  computer-delivered Listening — typing as you hear, no transfer window, 2-minute
  check. The 10-minute transfer routine is a legacy skill and training it is
  now close to wasted time.

## Question-type inventory

Officially there are **six** named question types [T1: ielts.org — Listening test
format; T1: ielts.idp.com — Question types in the IELTS Listening test]. Type 4
is a family of five layouts sharing one rule set, so the book should present
**ten practical variants**. Every task below is drawn from the official sample
tasks PDF, so the "what the official key accepts" lines are evidence, not
inference.

### 1. Form completion

**Appearance.** A pre-printed form with numbered gaps — the layout you meet in
Part 1. Official example: PACKHAM'S SHIPPING AGENCY customer quotation form —
Name / Address to be collected from / Town / Postcode / Size of container
(Length, Width, Height) / Contents / Total estimated value. Instruction: *Complete
the form below. Write NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER for each answer.*
An **Example** is worked for you at the top ("Country of destination: Kenya") —
that information is then **skipped** in the audio, or given before the numbered
answers start. [T1: ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, p.3]

**Rules.** Word limit as printed. Spelling must be correct. Numbers, postcodes
and measurements are all fair game. Where an answer can legitimately be expressed
several ways, the key lists alternatives.

**What the official key actually accepts** (p.7) — this is the most instructive
answer key in the whole official corpus:

| Q | Accepted |
|---|---|
| 1 | `Mkere` (spelled out in the audio: M-K-E-R-E) |
| 2 | `Westall` (spelled out: W-E-S-T-A-L-L) |
| 3 | `BS8 9PU` |
| 4 | `0.75 m/metre(s)/meter(s) (wide)` / `three(-)quarter(s) (of) (a) metre/meter (wide)` / `¾ m (wide)` / `75 cm(s) (wide)` |
| 5 | `0.5 m/metre(s)/meter(s) (high/deep)` / `(a) half (a) metre/meter` / `½ m` / `50 cm(s)` |
| 6 & 7 | **in either order**: `(some) books` · `(some) toys` |
| 8 | `1,700` |

Read that carefully. **`meter` — the American spelling — is explicitly in the
official key.** Units may be abbreviated or spelled out. Equivalent conversions
(75 cm for 0.75 m) are accepted. Bracketed words are *optional but correct*.
Where two gaps take two items from a list, **either order is accepted**.

**The trap.** Numbers arrive in a run, attached to labels you must keep straight:
"it's 1.5m long … 0.75m wide … 0.5m high or deep." Three numbers, three labels,
five seconds. If you are writing 1.5 you will miss 0.75. Then Q8: "the clothes
and the books – they'll be about **£1500** but then the toys are about another
**two hundred** – so I'd put down **£1700**." Three numbers; the answer is the
**sum**, stated last.

**Technique.** Before the audio, annotate each gap with the *category* it can
take: `1 = surname (will be spelled)`, `3 = postcode (letters+digits)`,
`4/5 = number + unit`, `8 = £ figure`. Then you are matching a slot, not
transcribing a conversation. For runs of numbers, write the digits only and add
the unit in the 2-minute check.

**Band-6 mistake.** Writing `£1700` when `£` is already printed on the form. The
gap reads `Total estimated value: £………`. Adding the symbol you were given is a
copying error of exactly the kind the official Don't list warns about: "Don't
copy any words before or after the gaps" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.2].
On computer this is easier to get wrong, not harder — the currency symbol sits
outside the input box and it is tempting to retype it.

### 2. Note completion

**Appearance.** Headed notes with indented sub-points and gaps — the standard
Part 4 layout, and common in Part 2. Official example: THE NATIONAL ARTS CENTRE —
*Well known for / Complex consists of / Historical background / Managed by /
Open*. Instruction: *Complete the notes below. Write NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS
AND/OR A NUMBER for each answer.* [T1: ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, p.29]

**Rules.** Same word-limit and spelling rules. The heading structure is your map:
answers arrive in the order of the headings, and each heading is signposted in
the audio.

**The trap — three of them, all visible in one official tapescript.**

1. **A list where one item is the answer.** The notes pre-print *concert rooms,
   theatres, cinemas, art galleries, public library, restaurants* and leave gap
   12. The audio: "it houses concert rooms, theatres, cinemas, art galleries and
   a wonderful public library, as well as service facilities including 3
   restaurants and **a book shop**." You must notice the one item on the list you
   were *not* given. Key: `(a) bookshop / (a) bookstore` — again, the American
   variant is accepted, and *bookshop* as one word is accepted even though the
   speaker says "book shop".
2. **Three dates, three verbs, one gap each.** "it was **planned in the 60s**,
   **built in the 70s** and eventually **opened to the public in 1983**." Q13 is
   `1960s – Centre was ………` → **planned**. Write *built* and you have taken the
   adjacent fact. Q14 is `In ……… – opened to public` → `1983 / (the) 1980s`.
3. **A negated lead-in.** Q15: "It's **not** privately owned, like many arts
   centres, but is still in public hands: it's run by **the City Council**."
   The wrong answer (*privately owned*) is spoken first and is grammatically
   fluent in the gap.

**Technique.** For Part 4 note completion, read **all ten** gaps in the opening
reading window — you get no second window. Mark the two or three gaps whose
headings are furthest apart; those are your re-entry points if you lose the
thread. Then track the **signposting** ("Historical background" will be
introduced by a date or "the area was…"), not the individual words.

**Band-6 mistake.** Freezing on a missed gap. The official Don't is explicit:
"Don't panic if you miss one question. Look ahead and concentrate on the next
one" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.2]. Because answers are guaranteed in
order, one missed gap costs one mark; hunting for it costs three.

### 3. Table completion

**Appearance.** A grid with a numbered gap in some cells; column headers tell you
the category of each gap. Official example: Day / Time / Event / Venue / Ticket
price for the National Arts Centre. Instruction: *Complete the table below. Write
NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER for each answer.* [T1: ielts.org —
Listening Sample Tasks, p.30]

**Rules.** Identical to note completion. The column header is a hard constraint on
the answer type — a gap under "Ticket price" can only be money.

**What the official key accepts** (p.33): `17 (the) Garden Hall` · `18 Three
Lives` · `19 £4.50` · `20 Faces of China`. Note the titles are capitalised in the
key but capitalisation is not required (see Answer-rule reference).

**The trap — the correction-by-contrast number.** Q19: "tickets cost just
**£4.50**, which is a reduction on the usual price of **£5.50**." Two prices,
half a second apart, the *second* one wrong. This is the mirror image of the
self-correction trap: here the answer comes **first** and the distractor
**second**. A reader trained only on "the answer comes after the correction"
will get this wrong. The distinguishing signal is the framing — *usual price*,
*normally*, *was*, *used to be*, *before the discount* mark the distractor;
*just*, *only*, *now*, *this week* mark the answer.

**Technique.** Read the table **by row**, not by column — a row is one event and
the audio delivers it as one continuous chunk. Convert each header into a
listening target before the audio: row 2 = "Wednesday, 8pm, Canadian film →
I need a TITLE and a PRICE."

**Band-6 mistake.** Missing that gap 18 and gap 20 sit inside quotation marks
(`18 '………'`) — they are **titles**, so you are listening for a name, not a
description. `Three Lives`, not *a Canadian film*.

### 4. Flow-chart completion

**Appearance.** Boxes joined by arrows, showing stages of a process, with gaps in
some boxes. Officially grouped with form/note/table/summary completion and used
where the audio describes "a process which has different stages" [T1:
ielts.idp.com — Question types in the IELTS Listening test].

**Rules.** Identical word-limit and spelling rules to the rest of the family.

**The trap.** The arrows impose a sequence, and the speaker may describe the
stages **out of** narrative order ("before you do that, you'll need to…",
"but first…", "once that's done, going back a step…"). The *questions* are still
in order — the official order rule holds — but the *process* may be narrated with
backtracks.

**Technique.** Before the audio, say each arrow out loud as a sequence word:
"first → then → after that → finally." Then listen for those connectives rather
than for content. Flow-charts are the one completion type where sequencing
language ("subsequently", "prior to", "at this point") carries more information
than topic vocabulary.

**Band-6 mistake.** Writing a whole clause into a box. Boxes are short by design;
if your answer needs a verb *and* an object *and* a preposition you have almost
certainly heard a paraphrase rather than the words to write down.

### 5. Summary completion

**Appearance.** A continuous prose paragraph summarising part of the recording,
with numbered gaps. Sometimes with a **box of options** to choose from instead of
free writing [T1: ielts.org — Listening test format: "you may have to select your
answers from a list on the question paper"].

**Rules.** If there is a word bank, the word-limit instruction disappears and is
replaced by letters — and you must transfer the **letter**, not the word (see
Answer-rule reference). If there is no word bank, the word limit applies.

**The trap.** Because it is continuous prose, the grammar of the surrounding
sentence constrains the answer far more tightly than in notes — and the audio
will not hand you a word in the right grammatical form. You hear "they decided to
*expand*"; the summary reads "the *…………* of the programme" and needs
**expansion**. But you may only write what fits the gap grammatically *and* is
within the limit *and* is a word from the recording — official guidance says
"Don't try to rephrase what you hear. Try to write down the words you hear which
fit the question" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.2]. When those two pull
apart, the gap is usually one where the exact word *is* spoken; you missed it.

**Technique.** Read the summary once for **meaning** before predicting word
forms. Unlike notes, a summary is readable prose and gives you the whole argument
of the passage in 40 seconds — that context is worth more than the individual
predictions.

**Band-6 mistake.** For French/Arabic L1 specifically: producing a word that is
the right meaning but a French-derived form the speaker never said
(*augmentation* for *increase*, *formation* for *training*). The recording's word
is the only safe answer.

### 6. Sentence completion

**Appearance.** A short list of stand-alone sentences, each with one gap, each
summarising one point. Official example (Part 3, Open University): *Studying with
the Open University demanded a great deal of 27 ………. · Studying and working at
the same time improved Rachel's 28 ………. skills. · It was helpful that the course
was structured in 29 ………. · She enjoyed meeting other students at 30 ……….*
Instruction here: *Write **NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS** for each answer* — note: **no
"AND/OR A NUMBER" clause**. [T1: ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, p.14]

**Rules.** Word limit as printed, and **the limits genuinely vary between tasks
in the same paper.** Officially this type tests "the ability to identify the
important information" and relationships "such as cause and effect"
[T1: ielts.org — GT Listening test format].

**What the official key accepts** (p.17): `27 motivation` · `28
time(-)management` · `29 modules` · `30 summer school(s)`.

Three enormous lessons in four answers:

- **`time(-)management`** — the hyphen is **optional**. With or without, it is
  within a TWO WORDS limit, because hyphenated words count as one.
- **`summer school(s)`** — the **plural is optional here.** The audio says "from
  time to time there are summer schools", but both singular and plural score.
  So "wrong plurals score zero" is **too strong as a blanket rule**. See the
  Answer-rule reference for the precise version.
- **`modules`** — here the plural *is* required by the key, because the audio is
  "the degree is made up of modules" and the singular would be ungrammatical in
  the gap.

**The trap.** The sentence's grammar is doing the work and the audio does not
match it. Q28 reads "improved Rachel's ………. **skills**", so the gap needs a
modifier; the audio says "I got very good at **time-management**". You must
convert a predicate into a pre-modifier. Q29 reads "structured **in** ……….";
the audio says "the degree **is made up of** modules".

**Technique.** For every gap, decide the **part of speech** and whether the gap is
before or after the head noun, *before* the audio. Then a heard phrase can be
tested against the slot in a fraction of a second.

**Band-6 mistake.** Answering Q30 with `summer schools they usually last a week`
— true, heard, and over the limit, therefore zero. Under a TWO WORDS instruction
the discipline is: **stop writing at two words.**

### 7. Multiple choice — single answer

**Appearance.** A question or sentence-stem with three options A, B, C.
Instruction: *Choose the correct letter, A, B or C.* Official example: *9 Type of
insurance chosen — A Economy / B Standard / C Premium.* *10 Customer wants goods
delivered to — A port / B home / C depot.* [T1: ielts.org — Listening Sample
Tasks, p.8]

**Rules.** One letter only. Writing two letters scores zero even if one is right
[T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.3].

**The trap — and the official tapescript shows it perfectly.** For Q9 the speaker
names **all three** options in a single sentence: "there's the highest
comprehensive cover which is **Premium** rate, then there's **Standard** rate and
then there's **Economy** rate." The customer then answers **without naming any of
them**: "I've been stung before with Economy insurance so **I'll go for the
highest**." Answer: **C, Premium.**

Three separate mechanics stacked in one item:

- All options mentioned, none is the answer by virtue of being mentioned.
- The only option the customer *names* — Economy — is the one being **rejected**.
- The answer is delivered by a **back-reference** ("the highest") that only works
  if you retained the definition given eight seconds earlier.

Q10 does the same more simply: "would you want **home delivery** or to a **local
depot** or would you want to pick it up at the **nearest port**?" → "The **port**'d
be fine." All three in one breath; the answer is the last-mentioned, which is a
coincidence, not a rule.

**Technique.** Read the **stem** first and turn it into a question you are asking
the audio ("which insurance did he pick?"). Then read the three options and mark
what *distinguishes* them — Economy/Standard/Premium differ only in level, so
listen for a **comparative** (*highest*, *cheapest*, *basic*), not for the names.
Tick a provisional answer but keep listening [T2: IELTS Liz — Listening MCQ
essential tips].

**Band-6 mistake.** Selecting the option whose word you heard most clearly.
Officially: "Just because it is mentioned, doesn't mean it is right"
[T2: E2 — Understanding distractors in IELTS Listening].

### 8. Multiple choice — multiple answer

**Appearance.** A longer option list (A–G or A–H) with an instruction to choose
**two or three**. Officially: "you may be given a longer list of possible answers
and told that you have to choose more than one" [T1: ielts.org — Listening test
format]; "sometimes choosing multiple answers requiring careful reading"
[T1: ielts.org — GT Listening test format].

**Rules.** Choose exactly the number asked for. Under-choosing loses marks;
over-choosing loses marks. Each correct letter is normally worth one mark, so a
"choose TWO" item occupies **two** question numbers.

**The trap.** The instruction is the trap. Candidates who skim the rubric select
one letter for a two-mark item. Official Do: "Read the instructions for each task
carefully" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.2]; "Read questions carefully to
determine how many answers are required" [T1: ielts.idp.com — Question types].
Secondly, on a two-from-seven item the audio touches **most** of the seven
options, and typically rejects them in a rapid list.

**Technique.** Circle the number word in the instruction (TWO / THREE) before you
read the options. On computer, the interface enforces the count — you physically
cannot tick a fourth box — which makes this type *easier* on screen. Use the two
question numbers as a checklist: two numbers, two letters.

**Band-6 mistake.** Treating the option list as ordered. In this type especially,
the audio jumps around the list; the *question numbers* are in order, but a
"choose two" item is a single point in the audio.

### 9. Matching

**Appearance.** Two forms, both officially exemplified.

**Matching (a) — repeated criteria.** *What does Jack tell his tutor about each of
the following course options? A He'll definitely do it. B He may or may not do
it. C He won't do it. Write the correct letter, A, B or C, next to questions
21–25.* **"You may choose any letter more than once."** [T1: ielts.org —
Listening Sample Tasks, p.18]

**Matching (b) — one-to-one from a box.** *Which hotel matches each description?
Choose your answers from the box and write the correct letter, A–E, next to
questions 1–4.* Box lists five hotels alphabetically; four questions. [T1:
ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, p.22]

**Rules.** Write the **letter**, not the word. Read whether letters may repeat —
in (a) they may, in (b) each is used once and one is a spare. Questions are in
order [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.3].

**The trap — the official Matching 1 tapescript is a masterclass.** Look at what
Jack actually says versus the answer:

| Item | Jack says | Key |
|---|---|---|
| Media Studies | "that was **interesting**, but I'd rather do something completely new" | **C — won't do it** |
| Women and Power | "I'm not sure I do actually" … then "**I'll sign up for that, then**" | **A — definitely** |
| Culture and Society | "**I'll think about** that one" | **B — may or may not** |
| Identity and Popular Culture | "That sounds **interesting**" … "I might **wait until then to decide**" | **B — may or may not** |
| Intro to Cultural Theory | "I'm quite **interested** in that too – … she said it was **really useful**" … "Oh, I'll **forget about** that one, then" | **C — won't do it** |

Every single item contains **positive enthusiasm language that is then
overturned**. The word *interesting* appears against a C, a B and a C. The last
item stacks two positive endorsements — Jack's own interest and a second-year
student's recommendation — before the tutor's objection flips it in four words.

For Matching 2 (hotels), the mechanics are different and equally instructive:

- **Q1 "is in a rural area" → E, Royal Oak.** The audio never says *rural*: "out
  in the country, about ten kilometres away, very peaceful." Meanwhile Bridge and
  Majestic are "in town but not in the centre, out on the airport road" — which
  *sounds* peripheral but is not rural.
- **Q2 "only opened recently" → B, Carlton House.** "they took their first guests
  just a few months ago." The distractor is the Imperial, "a much more **modern**
  building" — modern architecture is not recent opening. A pure
  false-equivalence trap.
- **Q4 "has an indoor swimming pool" → A, Bridge Hotel.** Three pools discussed:
  Royal Oak has an **outdoor** pool; Majestic "is **planning to build** a
  swimming pool … but it's not finished yet"; only the Bridge has an indoor one.
  Adjective trap + planned-not-actual trap in one breath.

**Technique.** For type (a), write the criteria as three shorthand symbols
(✓ / ? / ✗) and mark the symbol, not the letter, while listening; convert to
letters afterwards. For type (b), the box items are your listening anchors —
they are proper nouns and survive paraphrase; the *descriptions* are what get
paraphrased.

**Band-6 mistake.** Committing on first evidence. In official matching tasks the
verdict is delivered at the **end** of each item's discussion, after the
positive framing. Wait for the final clause.

### 10. Plan / map / diagram labelling

**Appearance.** A visual — a building plan, a town map, a piece of equipment —
with numbered positions. Two variants: choose letters from a box, or write words
within a limit. Official example: *Label the plan below. Choose FIVE answers from
the box and write the correct letters, A–I, next to questions 11–15.* — a town
library plan with nine lettered spaces and five to fill. [T1: ielts.org —
Listening Sample Tasks, p.26]

**Rules.** When lettered, write letters. When free-written, the word limit
applies. Officially this type tests "understanding descriptions of places and
relating them to visual representations" and following directional language —
"straight on / turn left / opposite" [T1: ielts.idp.com — Question types].

**The trap — orientation, not vocabulary.** The official tapescript is one long
chain of spatial relations anchored to a moving reference point:

> "my desk is just **on your right as you go in**, and **opposite this** the first
> room **on your left** … **Just beyond** the librarian's desk **on the right** …
> If you **carry straight on** … fiction in the shelves **on the left**,
> non-fiction **on your right**, and on the shelves **on the far wall** … **Through
> the far door** … **just past** the fiction shelves … **next door to that** …
> a large room **to the right of** the library area … the Library Office, **on the
> left of** the librarian's desk."

Note "**on your** right" — the description is from the walker's point of view,
which **rotates** as the tour turns. A reader who fixes left/right to the page
will invert half the plan.

Note also the discarded fact: the multimedia room "was originally the art
collection but that's been moved to another building." A former label, given and
withdrawn.

**Technique.**

1. In the reading window, find the **entrance / "you are here" / compass** and
   put your finger (or cursor) on it. Every description starts there.
2. Read the **already-labelled** features aloud — they are the fixed points the
   speaker will navigate from.
3. Number the lettered options you have *not yet used*; in a five-from-nine task,
   crossing off used letters halves the search space by Q14.
4. Track the speaker's body, not the page. When you hear "turn", rotate.

**Band-6 mistake.** For this reader specifically: **French/Arabic spatial
prepositions do not map one-to-one.** *En face de* covers both *opposite* and
*facing*; *à côté de* covers *next to*, *beside* and *adjacent to*; Arabic
`أمام` covers *in front of* and *opposite*. Drill the English set as a closed
list — opposite / next to / adjacent to / beyond / past / behind / in front of /
at the far end / in the corner of / across from / bear left — because in a map
task these words *are* the answer.

### 11. Short-answer questions

**Appearance.** Direct questions with a word limit, often in pairs or triples
that share one prompt. Official example: *Answer the questions below. Write NO
MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER for each answer.* — *What TWO factors can
make social contact in a foreign country difficult?* (11, 12); *Which types of
community group does the speaker give examples of?* (13, 14, with "theatre"
given); *In which TWO places can information about community activities be
found?* (15, 16). [T1: ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, p.11]

**Rules.** Word limit applies. Officially tests listening "for facts, such as
places, prices or times" [T1: ielts.org — GT Listening test format].

**What the official key accepts** (p.13): `11 & 12 **in either order**: language ·
customs` · `13 & 14 **in either order**: music (groups) · local history
(groups)` · `15 & 16 **in either order**: (the) (public) library/libraries ·
(the) town hall`.

Look at Q15/16: `(the) (public) library/**libraries**` — **singular and plural
both accepted**, and both optional determiners accepted. Again: the blanket claim
that a wrong plural always scores zero is not what the official keys show.

**The trap.** When one prompt covers two numbered gaps, the two answers may be
**several sentences apart** and separated by material that answers neither. In
the official tapescript the two difficulties — "not just because of the
**language**, but because **customs** may be different" — are adjacent, but the
two places (library, town hall) come at the very end after a long list of group
types.

**Technique.** Because *either order* is accepted, do not try to assign the first
thing you hear to the first gap. Collect both, then place them. This is the one
question type where you can safely listen ahead of your pen.

**Band-6 mistake.** Answering in a full sentence. "You can find it in the public
library" is 6 words, over a THREE WORDS limit, and scores zero for an answer that
is completely correct.

## Answer-rule reference

This is the highest-value section for a 6.5→7 reader, because every rule here
converts a *heard-correctly* answer into a mark or into a zero.

### The word limit

**What the instruction says.** The limit is printed on every completion, sentence
and short-answer task, and **it varies between tasks in the same paper.** Verified
official wordings:

| Wording | Where seen |
|---|---|
| `NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER` | Form completion, short-answer, note completion, table completion [T1: ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, pp. 3, 11, 29, 30] |
| `NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS` | Sentence completion [T1: ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, p.14] |
| `NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER` | Given as the canonical example [T1: ielts.org — Listening test format; T1: ielts.idp.com — Question types] |
| `ONE WORD ONLY` | Standard variant; not in the 2023 sample PDF but consistent with the pattern [UNVERIFIED as an exact official Listening string — flagged for verifiers] |

**The penalty is absolute and total.** "You will lose the mark for writing more
than the word limit" [T1: ielts.org — Listening test format]. "Answers which are
over the word limit will be marked as incorrect" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org —
FAQs, p.3]. Officially listed as a myth to kill: thinking a correct answer
survives an instruction breach [T1: ielts.idp.com — 9 myths, myth 9].

**Official illustration:** if the limit is two words and the answer is *leather
coat*, then *coat made of leather* is **wrong** [T1: takeielts.britishcouncil.org
— Test day advice].

The prior-work hypothesis "word limits are absolute; three words scores zero
under a two-word limit" is **confirmed**.

### What counts as a word

| Item | Counts as | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Hyphenated word** (`check-in`, `time-management`, `up-to-date`) | **ONE word** | [T1: ielts.org — Listening test format: "Hyphenated words such as 'check-in' count as single words"] |
| **Article** (`a`, `an`, `the`) | **ONE word — it counts** | [DERIVED, see below] + [T2: IELTS Liz — How words are counted: "Small words such as 'a' or 'an' are counted"] |
| **Preposition** (`in`, `at`, `of`) | **ONE word — it counts** | [T2: IELTS Liz — How words are counted] |
| **A number written in digits** (`55`, `1,700`, `363`, `9.30`) | **ONE number** | [DERIVED from official keys: `1,700`, `363`, `£4.50` all score as single answers] |
| **A currency or percent symbol** (`£`, `%`) | **Not counted** | [T2: IELTS Liz — How words are counted] |
| **Compound written as one word** (`bookshop`, `blackboard`) | **ONE word** | [DERIVED: official key accepts `(a) bookshop` where the audio said "book shop"] |
| **Compound written as two words** (`town hall`, `summer school`) | **TWO words** | [DERIVED from official key `(the) town hall`] |
| **A date like `12th July`** | one number + one word | [T2: IELTS Liz — How words are counted] |
| **Contraction** (`it's`) | one word — **but not tested** | see below |

**The derivation on articles, because a lot of bad advice circulates here.**
Some prep sites claim articles "don't count toward the limit". That is wrong, and
the official keys show why. The key for the note-completion task gives
`15 (the) City Council` under a **THREE WORDS** limit. The brackets are defined by
the official document itself: "**Words in brackets are optional — they are
correct, but not necessary.**" So *the City Council* is an accepted answer, and it
is three words. It fits because the limit was three. Under a **TWO WORDS** limit
that same answer would breach. Therefore articles are words and they consume
budget. [DERIVED from T1: ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, pp. 7, 13, 33]

**The safe operating rule for the book:** *never write an article you were not
forced to write.* It can only cost you; it can never gain you a mark. The keys
consistently bracket articles as optional, which means dropping them is always
safe and including them is only sometimes safe.

### Spelling

- **Spelling must be correct.** "You will lose marks for incorrect spelling and
  grammar" [T1: ielts.org — Listening test format]. "Do answers in IELTS
  Listening have to be correctly spelled? **Yes.**" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org —
  FAQs, p.4]
- **Both UK and US spellings are accepted.** "**Both UK and US spellings are
  accepted**" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.4]. "IELTS recognises both
  British and American English in terms of **spelling, grammar, and choice of
  words**" [T1: ielts.org — Teacher's guide to IELTS, p.26]. "IELTS is an
  international English test that covers all major varieties of English
  (American, Australian, British)" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.1]. And
  the sample answer key literally prints `metre(s)/**meter**(s)` as accepted
  [T1: ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, p.7].
- **Reassurance that matters:** "words which you have to write will usually be
  **common** words" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.4].
- **Proper nouns are spelled out for you.** "When you hear the name of a person,
  place, company, etc., in the recording, **it may be spelled out**"
  [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.4]. Both official Part 1 names — *Mkere*,
  *Westall* — are spelled letter by letter in the tapescript.

The prior-work hypothesis "spelling errors score zero" is **confirmed**. The
prior work did **not** record that American spellings are accepted — that is a
material addition, and for a French-L1 reader it matters, because the French
`-re` ending (*centre*, *metre*, *theatre*) is the **British** form and is safe,
while *program/programme* and *labor/labour* are both safe. The one thing that is
never safe is a French form that is not English in either variety
(*adresse*, *exemple*, *développement*, *responsable*).

### Plurals

**This needs stating more carefully than the prior work does.**

The prior work says flatly: "Plurals cost marks. If you hear 'two tickets' and
write *ticket*, it is wrong." The first half is right; the blanket rule is too
strong. Official keys show **three** different treatments:

| Key | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `29 modules` | plural **required** — the singular is ungrammatical in the gap |
| `30 summer school(s)` | **either** accepted |
| `15 (the) (public) library/libraries` | **either** accepted |
| `4 0.75 m/metre(s)/meter(s)` | **either** accepted |

[T1: ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, pp. 17, 13, 7]

**The accurate rule.** Number is marked **when the gap's grammar or the audio's
content makes it determinate**, and tolerated when it does not. You cannot tell
which case you are in from the outside. Therefore the operating rule is
unchanged in practice — **match what you hear, and check the gap's grammar** —
but the *reason* is different, and the book should not tell the reader that every
plural slip is fatal, because that produces panic-driven over-correction (adding
`-s` to uncountables, which for this reader is a live L1 risk: *informations*,
*researches*, *equipments*, *advices*).

**L1 warning to carry into the Listening chapter.** French pluralises nouns that
English does not. In a Listening gap, writing *informations*, *researches*,
*advices*, *equipments*, *furnitures*, *softwares*, *evidences* or *feedbacks* is
a **spelling/grammar error and scores zero**, and it is a mistake this reader is
statistically likely to make under time pressure. Drill the uncountable list as
Listening vocabulary, not just as Writing grammar.

### Capitalisation

- **It does not matter.** "Your answers may be written in **either capital
  letters or lower case**" [T1: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test day advice].
- The official keys print `Faces of China`, `Three Lives`, `(the) City Council`,
  `Mkere` with capitals, but nothing in any official source requires them.
- **Paper-only nuance:** writing in capitals is a *legibility* tactic, not a
  scoring one, and it exists because "if an answer isn't clear on your answer
  sheet, you will lose the mark" [T1: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test day
  advice]. On computer this reason evaporates entirely.

**Recommendation for this reader (computer-delivered): type normally.** ALL CAPS
costs keystrokes and shift-key errors and buys nothing. See MYTHS.

### Contractions

"Contracted words such as 'they're' **will not be tested**" [T1: ielts.org —
Listening test format]. Prior-work hypothesis **confirmed**. Practical meaning:
if the only way you can fit an answer into the limit is by contracting it, you
have the wrong answer.

### Numbers, money, dates, units

From the official keys [T1: ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, pp. 7, 33]:

- `1,700` — comma accepted; the `£` was pre-printed and must **not** be retyped.
- `£4.50` — where the symbol is *not* pre-printed, include it.
- `363` — bare digits.
- `BS8 9PU` — alphanumeric postcode, space included.
- `0.75 m` ≡ `75 cm` ≡ `three-quarters of a metre` — equivalent expressions all
  score; units may be abbreviated, spelled out, singular or plural, British or
  American.
- `1983` ≡ `(the) 1980s` — where the audio supports both precisions.

**Do not write out numbers in words unless it is faster.** Digits are always
accepted and are one "number" for word-limit purposes.

### One answer per gap

"Don't write more than one answer when only one is required. **Even if one of
your answers is correct, you will not receive a mark**" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org
— FAQs, p.3]. This deserves a boxed callout in the chapter. It is the rule most
likely to be broken by a candidate who is *nearly* good enough for band 7 and is
trying to protect themselves.

### Paper-only transfer rules

Include for completeness, flagged as legacy given the mid-2026 move to computer:

- 10 minutes, in pencil, onto the answer sheet
  [T1: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test day advice].
- "Anything that you write on your question paper **will not be marked**" [ibid].
- "Write clearly … If an answer isn't clear on your answer sheet, you will lose
  the mark" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.2].
- "**Don't copy any words before or after the gaps** on the question paper when
  you transfer your answers" [ibid] — i.e. transfer the answer, not the printed
  frame around it.
- Officially, 10 minutes is "plenty of time" [ibid] — so on paper, transfer
  slowly and check spelling as you go.

## Distractor mechanics

Every mechanic below is evidenced from an **official tapescript** in
`ielts-listening-sample-tasks-2023.pdf`, with Tier 2 corroboration where the
mechanic has a name in the literature. This is what "find concrete mechanics, not
vague advice" looks like.

### M1 — Option mentioned, then rejected

The commonest mechanic; appears in MCQ, matching and completion alike.

> "there's the highest comprehensive cover which is Premium rate, then there's
> Standard rate and then there's Economy rate … **I've been stung before with
> Economy insurance** so I'll go for the highest." → answer **Premium**
> [T1: Sample Tasks p.9]

**Rejection signals to drill:** *I've been stung before with…* · *I'd rather do
something completely new* · *I'll forget about that one* · *that might be a bit
far out actually* · *I'm not sure I do actually* · *but* · *however*.

Corroborated as a named mechanic: "Just because it is mentioned, doesn't mean it
is right" [T2: E2 — Understanding distractors]; "Watch out for answers that are
given and then taken away again by the speaker" [T2: IELTS Liz — Listening MCQ
essential tips]. **Two independent Tier 2 sources + official evidence → promoted.**

### M2 — Positive language then reversal (the matching killer)

Distinct from M1 because the *reversal is verbal and late*, and the positive
language is emphatic enough to feel like an answer.

> "I'm quite **interested** in that too – I was talking to one of the second year
> students, and she said **it was really useful**, it made a lot of things fall
> into place." … *[tutor objects]* … "Oh, **I'll forget about that one**, then."
> → **C, won't do it** [T1: Sample Tasks p.20]

Two endorsements — the speaker's own and a third party's — then four words flip
it. In the same task *interesting* appears against two different answer keys.
**The rule: in matching, the verdict is the last clause of the item, never the
first.**

### M3 — Self-correction (answer comes AFTER)

The speaker states a value and revises it.

**Signals:** *sorry* · *actually* · *I mean* · *no wait* · *let me check that* ·
*or rather* · *in fact* · *hang on*.

Corroborated: "Speakers often correct themselves. For example, 'The train leaves
at 4:30… oh wait, no, it's 4:15.' If you write 4:30, you lose the mark"
[T2: E2/IELTS Advantage — listening mistakes]. Consistent with the official
Do "Check that what you write makes sense in the context"
[T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.2].

### M4 — Correction by contrast (answer comes FIRST)

**The mirror of M3, and the one the prior work misses entirely.** The correct
value is stated, then a *different, wrong* value is stated immediately after as
background.

> "tickets cost just **£4.50**, which is a reduction on the usual price of
> **£5.50**." → answer **£4.50** [T1: Sample Tasks p.32, key p.33]

If the reader has only learned "the answer comes after the correction", this item
takes the mark off them. **Discriminator:** M3 revises the *same* fact
(*sorry, I mean…*); M4 contrasts *two different* facts (*usual price*, *normally*,
*was*, *used to be*, *before the discount*, *last year*). Teach them as a pair.

### M5 — Arithmetic / running total

> "the clothes and the books – they'll be about **£1500** but then the toys are
> about another **two hundred** – so I'd put down **£1700**." → answer **1,700**
> [T1: Sample Tasks p.6, key p.7]

Three numbers; the answer is the sum and is stated last. Related mechanic: the
gap label ("Total estimated value") tells you in advance that a total is coming.
**Read gap labels for the word *total*, *altogether*, *each*, *per* — they warn
you that arithmetic is in play.**

### M6 — Number run against labels

> "it's **1.5m** long … **0.75m** wide … and it's **0.5m** high or deep"
> [T1: Sample Tasks p.5]

Three numbers in five seconds, each bound to a different label, in the same order
as the gaps. The failure mode is not mishearing — it is falling one label behind
and writing every answer in the wrong box, which turns one slip into three zeros.

### M7 — Negation carrying the answer

> "It's **not** privately owned, like many arts centres, but is still in public
> hands: it's run by **the City Council**." → answer *City Council*
> [T1: Sample Tasks p.31]

The wrong answer (*privately owned*) is spoken first, is fluent in the gap, and is
negated by a single word. Corroborated as a category: "hidden negation using
words like avoid, never, or prefixes (un-, dis-, in-, ir-, mis-, de-)"; and the
inverse case where "it's not just X but also Y" makes **both** X and Y correct
[T2: E2 — Understanding distractors].

### M8 — Planned / proposed but not actual

> "The Majestic is **planning to build** a swimming pool and a fitness centre,
> **but it's not finished yet**." → not the answer for "has an indoor swimming
> pool" [T1: Sample Tasks p.24]

Also: "We're **hoping to add** a section on local tourist attractions too, later
in the year" [T1: Sample Tasks p.27] — a section that does not exist on the plan.

**Tense is the discriminator.** Present perfect and simple present = real;
*planning to*, *hoping to*, *due to*, *will be*, *is being considered* = not yet.

### M9 — Superseded fact

> "It **was originally** the art collection but **that's been moved** to another
> building." [T1: Sample Tasks p.27]

A label that used to be true. Signals: *originally* · *used to be* · *formerly* ·
*until last year* · *before the refurbishment*.

### M10 — Near-miss category (false equivalence)

> "only opened recently" → **Carlton House**, "they took their first guests just a
> few months ago". Distractor: the Imperial, "a much more **modern** building."
> [T1: Sample Tasks p.23]

Modern ≠ recently opened. Similarly *outdoor pool* ≠ *indoor pool*; *out on the
airport road* ≠ *rural*. The distractor is in the right semantic field and the
wrong category.

### M11 — Paraphrase, never the printed word

> Question: "is in a rural area." Audio: "**out in the country**, about ten
> kilometres away, very peaceful." [T1: Sample Tasks p.23]
> Question note: "Well known for ………". Audio: "It's **famous throughout the
> world as one of the major venues for** classical music." [T1: Sample Tasks p.31]

Corroborated: "A common mistake is to read a certain word and try to listen for
that word, but then not hear it. That is because the recording used a synonym"
[T2: IELTS Advantage / E2]. "Think of some synonyms for keywords … it is normal
for you to read one word but hear another word with the same meaning"
[T2: Magoosh; T2: IELTS Liz]. **Two independent Tier 2 → promoted.**

**Crucial asymmetry the book must state:** the **question stem** is paraphrased;
the **answer you write** is not. Officially: "Don't try to rephrase what you hear.
Try to write down the words you hear which fit the question"
[T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.2].

### M12 — Similar-sounding words

*Austria / Australia*; *thirteen / thirty*, *fourteen / forty*, *fifteen /
fifty*; *-teen* carries final stress (thir-**TEEN**), *-ty* carries initial stress
(**THIR**-ty) and often a flapped /t/ in North American speech. [T2: E2 —
Understanding distractors, "Similar Words"; T2: IELTS Advantage — common
mistakes]. **Two independent Tier 2 → promoted**, and it follows from the Tier 1
fact that five national accents are used, since the *-teen/-ty* contrast is
realised differently across them.

### M13 — The item you were NOT given

> Notes pre-print *concert rooms, theatres, cinemas, art galleries, public
> library, restaurants*; the audio lists all of those **plus** "a book shop".
> → answer *bookshop* [T1: Sample Tasks pp. 29, 31]

In a gapped list, the audio reads out the whole list including the items already
printed. Your job is subtraction, not comprehension. **Technique: tick the printed
items off as you hear them; the untickable one is your answer.**

### M14 — Adjacent-fact substitution

> "it was **planned in the 60s**, **built in the 70s** and eventually **opened to
> the public in 1983**." Gap: `1960s – Centre was ………` → **planned**
> [T1: Sample Tasks p.31]

Three verbs, three decades, delivered in one sentence, all of them plausible in
the gap. The gap's own label (*1960s*) is the discriminator. **Technique: the
pre-printed part of the gap is a filter — read it, then only accept audio that
matches it.**

## Strategies found

### S1 — Use the reading window to predict answer type and word class
**Tier 2, multiple sources + official support.**
"Predict the answer type you need to listen for and underline any key words"
[T2: IELTS Liz — Predicting answers]. "Think about grammar: noun, verb,
adjective, adverb… Consider the subject: phone number, address, date, business
name" [T2: Magoosh — Listening tips & strategies / APT method]. "You will have
some time before the recording starts to read over the question"
[T1: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — computer Listening practice].
**Official backing:** "Tell learners to read each question and think about the
context before they listen to help them identify **the type of information they
will need to listen for**" [T1: ielts.org — Teacher's guide to IELTS, p.22]; and
the official Do "Use the time at the beginning of each part (and in the middle of
Parts 1–3) to look through the questions and think about the topic"
[T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.2]. **Promoted under (a) and (b).**

**Helps-not-hurts:** yes. It converts open listening into slot-matching, which is
exactly what raises accuracy in the 6.5–7.5 band where comprehension is adequate
but capture rate is not.

### S2 — Generate synonyms for question keywords before the audio
**Tier 2, two independent sources.** [T2: Magoosh — Listening tips; T2: IELTS Liz
— Predicting answers]. Follows from mechanic M11, which is evidenced officially.
**Promoted under (b) and (c).**

### S3 — Do not use the mid-part break to check previous answers
**Tier 2.** "Don't check your answers from the previous section — it's crucial
that you use this time to study the upcoming questions instead" [T2: Magoosh].
Consistent with the Tier 1 fact that Parts 1–3 have exactly one mid-part break
whose stated purpose is "to allow you time to look at the following questions"
[T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.3]. **Promoted under (a).**

### S4 — Abandon a missed answer immediately; the order rule guarantees recovery
**Tier 1 directly.** "Don't panic if you miss one question. **Look ahead and
concentrate on the next one**" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.2]. Combined
with "questions are in the same order as the information… true for all question
types" [ibid, p.3], this yields the strongest single tactic in the chapter: if
you hear content that answers Q19 while hunting for Q17, **Q17 and Q18 are gone**
— mark a guess and lock onto 19. Prior work already states this; **confirmed and
now officially sourced.**

### S5 — Tick provisionally, confirm before committing (MCQ)
**Tier 2.** "If you think an answer is correct, put a tick but keep listening"
[T2: IELTS Liz — MCQ tips]. Consistent with mechanics M1/M2. On computer, the
equivalent is: select the option, then use the **Review flag** to revisit in the
2-minute check [T1: ielts.idp.com — How IELTS on computer works]. **Promoted.**

### S6 — Read tables by row, maps from the entrance
**Tier 2 + derived from official tapescripts** (see question-type inventory).
Both follow from how the official audio is organised: a table row is one
narrative chunk; a plan description is a walk from a fixed origin.

### S7 — Train on five accents, not two
**Derived from Tier 1.** Since the audio uses UK, Australian, NZ, US and Canadian
voices [T1: ielts.org — Teacher's guide, p.26], and since Parts 2 and 4 are
monologues carrying 20 of the 40 marks, a single unfamiliar accent in Part 4 can
cost 10 marks — a full band. Concrete plan: a fixed weekly rotation of one
Australian and one New Zealand source alongside British and North American.

### S8 — Listen to the spoken introduction to each part
**Tier 1.** "At the beginning of each part you will hear a short description of
the situation… **This description is not written on the question paper**, so it
is important to listen carefully" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.3]. Most
candidates use those seconds to keep reading. That is a real, cheap loss.

### S9 — Type as you hear; treat the 2 minutes as spelling-check only
**Derived from Tier 1.** With no transfer window and 2 minutes for 40 answers
(3 seconds each), the check is only long enough to fix flagged items and obvious
spelling. Prior work's framing — "you must be accurate *as you type*, not tidy up
afterwards" — is **confirmed and correct**.

### S10 — Budget the losses: Part 1 costs zero
**Derived.** Band 7 ≈ 30/40 = 10 losses available. Part 1 is form completion of
spelled-out names, phone numbers and prices, and is the only part where near-100%
is realistic. Every mark conceded in Part 1 is borrowed from Part 4, where it is
far harder to earn back.

## MYTHS

Popular Listening advice that is wrong, unsupported, or actively score-lowering.

### MYTH 1 — "Write both answers if you're not sure; one of them might be right."
**FALSE, and it scores zero.** "Don't write more than one answer when only one is
required. **Even if one of your answers is correct, you will not receive a mark**"
[T1: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs, p.3]. A candidate who writes
`4.15/4.30` has converted a 50% chance of a mark into a 0% chance.
**Why it's believed:** it feels like hedging. **Correct behaviour:** commit to one.

### MYTH 2 — "Always write your answers in CAPITAL LETTERS — it's safer."
**Half-true for paper, pointless on computer, and now mostly obsolete.** Official:
"Your answers may be written in **either** capital letters or lower case"
[T1: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test day advice]. The only real reason it was
ever advised is handwriting legibility on the paper answer sheet, where "if an
answer isn't clear… you will lose the mark" [ibid]. On computer there is no
legibility risk, ALL CAPS costs extra keystrokes, and shift-key errors introduce
typos into words you heard correctly. **Score impact of following it on computer:
neutral-to-negative.**

### MYTH 3 — "Articles don't count toward the word limit."
**FALSE.** This circulates on several prep blogs [T3-discovery]. All words count,
including *a*, *an*, *the* and prepositions [T2: IELTS Liz — How words are
counted], and the official keys confirm it: `(the) City Council` is offered as an
answer under a **three**-word limit, and would breach a two-word limit
[DERIVED from T1: ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, p.33].
**Why the myth persists:** answer keys bracket articles as optional, which people
misread as "not counted". Optional ≠ free.
**Correct behaviour:** never write an article you weren't forced to write.

### MYTH 4 — "You must use British spelling."
**FALSE.** "Both UK and US spellings are accepted" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org —
FAQs, p.4]; "IELTS recognises both British and American English in terms of
spelling, grammar, and choice of words" [T1: ielts.org — Teacher's guide, p.26];
and the official key prints `meter(s)` as correct [T1: ielts.org — Listening
Sample Tasks, p.7]. **Why it matters here:** a reader who believes this wastes
study time memorising *-our/-ise* pairs instead of drilling the words that
actually recur and actually get misspelled.

### MYTH 5 — "Paraphrase the answer into your own words to show range."
**FALSE and score-destroying.** Listening is not assessed on vocabulary range —
it is 1 mark per correct answer. Official: "**Don't try to rephrase what you
hear.** Try to write down the words you hear which fit the question"
[T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.2].
**Why it's believed:** carry-over from Writing and Speaking, where paraphrase is
rewarded. **Helps-not-hurts test:** it cannot raise a Listening band and it can
push you over a word limit or introduce a spelling error. Straight into MYTHS.

### MYTH 6 — "Matching and map questions are NOT in order — you have to scan around."
**FALSE.** "Are the questions in the same order as the information in the
recording? Yes. **This is true for all question types in IELTS Listening**"
[T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.3]. Also debunked officially at
[T1: ielts.idp.com — 9 myths, myth 2].
**Why it's believed:** confusion with IELTS *Reading*, where some types (Matching
Headings, Matching Information) genuinely are not in text order. **Cost of
believing it:** you scan the whole task on every clue instead of moving forward,
and fall behind permanently.
**Necessary refinement:** the *questions* are in order; the *options* within an
item are not [T2: IELTS Liz], and the official MCQ sample confirms options are
mentioned C-B-A.

### MYTH 7 — "You get time at the end to fix things, like in Reading."
**FALSE on computer.** 2 minutes for 40 answers [T1: ielts.idp.com — How IELTS on
computer works]. The 10-minute cushion existed only on paper, and paper is being
withdrawn from mid-2026 [T1: ielts.org — Updates to IELTS test delivery]. Any
study plan that assumes a tidy-up phase is training the wrong reflex.

### MYTH 8 — "There's a pause in Part 4 you can use to read ahead."
**FALSE in practice.** There is a short mid-Part-4 pause, but it is unannounced
and is for refocusing; **all** Part 4 reading time is given at the start
[T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.3]. Planning to read questions 35–40 during
it will leave you reading while the answers go past.

### MYTH 9 — "Take detailed notes while you listen."
**Unsupported and counterproductive.** Officially the advice is to "Write all
your answers **as you listen**" [T1: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs, p.2] — answers,
not notes. Corroborating Tier 1: "You should not take your attention away from
the Listening to write things down. You should instead follow along, stare at key
words that you underline, and predict the answer" [T1: ielts.idp.com — 9 myths].
**Why it's believed:** it feels diligent. **Cost:** every second spent writing
prose is a second not spent listening, and the audio does not wait.

### MYTH 10 — "Guessing is risky."
**FALSE.** "Try to answer all questions; **you will not lose points for incorrect
answers**" [T1: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test day advice]. A blank is a
guaranteed zero; a plausible guess of the right type is free expected value.
**But note the interaction with MYTH 1:** guess **one** answer, not two.

### MYTH 11 — "Everyone gets headphones, so audio quality is guaranteed."
**FALSE for paper.** "Certain testing centres offer a sound system with speakers"
[T1: ielts.idp.com — 9 myths, myth 4]. Computer-delivered candidates get their
own headphones and an on-screen volume control [T1: ielts.idp.com — IELTS on
computer vs paper; How IELTS on computer works]. One more reason this reader
should sit the computer test.

### MYTH 12 — "Listening on computer is exactly the same as on paper apart from typing."
**Officially stated, and officially contradicted.** IDP's myths page says "the
only difference will be the answers where you will input them in"
[T1: ielts.idp.com — 9 myths, myth 6], but IDP's own operational page states
10 minutes vs 2 minutes [T1: ielts.idp.com — How IELTS on computer works]. The
timing difference is real and is the single most training-relevant difference.
**Flagged for verifiers: prefer the operational page.**

### MYTH 13 — "Every plural you get wrong is an automatic zero."
**Overstated.** Official keys accept `summer school(s)`, `library/libraries`,
`metre(s)` [T1: ielts.org — Listening Sample Tasks, pp. 17, 13, 7]. Number is
marked when the gap or the content makes it determinate.
**Why this matters:** the overstated version drives panic over-correction, and
for a French-L1 reader over-correction means adding `-s` to English uncountables
(*informations*, *researches*, *advices*) — which **is** a real zero. The honest
rule is "match what you hear and check the gap's grammar", not "always plural".

## Band-descriptor implications

**There are no public band descriptors for Listening.** Listening and Reading are
scored objectively — 1 mark per correct answer out of 40, converted to a band
[T1: ielts.org — Listening test format]. The four-criterion descriptor apparatus
(Fluency, Lexical Resource, etc.) applies only to Writing and Speaking. The book
must not invent Listening "criteria", and any Tier 2/3 page offering "IELTS
Listening band descriptors" is describing raw-score bands, not a rubric.

**What replaces descriptors for this chapter: the mark budget.**

| Target band | Approx. raw marks / 40 | Losses affordable |
|---|---|---|
| 6.0 | 23 | 17 |
| 6.5 | ~26–27 | ~13 |
| 7.0 | 30 | 10 |
| 7.5 | ~32 | ~8 |
| 8.0 | 35 | 5 |

(From `knowledge/sources.md`, official; conversion varies slightly by version.
The 6.5 and 7.5 rows are interpolations and should be labelled as such.)

**The implications that should shape the chapter:**

1. **Every rule in the Answer-rule reference is worth whole bands.** Three
   word-limit breaches plus two spelling slips is 5 marks — the exact distance
   from 30 to 35, i.e. band 7 to band 8, and from 26 to 31, i.e. 6.5 to 7. For a
   reader whose comprehension is already adequate, **mark-preservation is a
   bigger lever than listening skill.**
2. **All four parts are worth the same 10 marks.** Part 1 is the cheapest 10
   marks in the entire IELTS test. Any study plan that spends equal time on all
   four parts is misallocating.
3. **Because there is no partial credit and no negative marking, the optimal
   policy is: one answer in every box, always.** There is no defensive play.
4. **The 6.5→7.0 gap is 3–4 marks.** That is: one map-labelling item recovered,
   one self-correction caught, one plural fixed. The chapter should be framed
   around finding four specific marks, not around "improving listening".

**Cross-skill note for the Writing/Grammar chapters:** the L1 error families that
cost this reader marks in Writing (uncountable plurals, article misuse,
false friends) are *the same families* that cost marks in Listening gaps, because
a Listening answer is a written answer. `progress/error-log.md` entries from
Writing drills should be re-tested as Listening dictation items. That is a
genuine efficiency the book can offer.

## Open questions for verifiers

1. **`ONE WORD ONLY` as an exact official Listening instruction string.** The
   2023 sample-tasks PDF contains only `NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER`
   and `NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS`. `ONE WORD ONLY` is universally reported but I
   could not verify it verbatim in an official Listening document. Confirm before
   the book prints it as an official wording. Tagged `[UNVERIFIED]` above.

2. **Part 3 speaker count.** ielts.org says "two main speakers"; British Council
   says "up to four people". Both Tier 1. Decide which the book leads with. My
   recommendation: lead with "two to four", cite both.

3. **The Part 4 mid-recording pause.** The Cambridge FAQ PDF states there *is* a
   short unannounced pause mid-Part-4. Most prep material and the prior work say
   Part 4 has no break. Both can be true, but confirm the FAQ PDF is current —
   check whether ielts.org or British Council restate it, and whether it holds for
   computer delivery.

4. **Paper section order.** IDP's vs-paper page claims paper order is
   Writing→Reading→Listening while computer is Listening→Reading→Writing. No
   corroboration found. Do not publish without a second Tier 1 source.

5. **Currency-symbol handling in the answer box on computer.** On paper the
   pre-printed `£` sits on the question paper and is clearly not to be copied.
   Whether the computer interface pre-fills or omits it in the input box could
   not be verified from official material. This determines a concrete piece of
   test-day advice, so it is worth chasing in the official computer-delivered
   familiarisation test.

6. **The 6.5 and 7.5 raw-mark rows.** Official guidance publishes bands 5, 6, 7,
   8 only. The 6.5/7.5 figures in the table above are interpolated by me and must
   be labelled as estimates, not official.

7. **Whether Listening has ever used non-native-speaker accents.** Official
   sources say "native-speaker accents" and name five countries. If the book says
   "you will only hear native accents", verify that is still the current position,
   since IELTS has published research on accent inclusivity.

8. **Marks per item on multi-answer MCQ.** I state that a "choose TWO" item
   occupies two question numbers and is worth two marks. This is consistent with
   the official sample layout (short-answer pairs 11 & 12, 15 & 16 each get their
   own number) but I did not find it stated explicitly for MCQ. Confirm.
