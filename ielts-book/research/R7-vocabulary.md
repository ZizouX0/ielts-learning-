## What Lexical Resource actually measures

### The official definition (Tier 1, verbatim scope, paraphrased content)

The *IELTS Writing Key Assessment Criteria* (ielts.org, created 2023-05-03) gives
LR one short definition and then a list of seven things the criterion assesses.
The definition — my single permitted quote for this criterion:

> "This criterion refers to the range of vocabulary the candidate has used and the
> accuracy and appropriacy of that use in terms of the specific task."
> — *IELTS Writing Key Assessment Criteria*, p.4 (Tier 1)

Note the shape of that sentence. **Range** is one of three nouns. **Accuracy** and
**appropriacy** are the other two, and both are qualified by *in terms of the
specific task* — meaning a word is not "good vocabulary" in the abstract; it is
good only if it fits this task.

The seven assessed sub-features, restated in my own words from the same page:

| # | Sub-feature (my paraphrase) | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The range of general words, e.g. using synonyms to avoid repetition | Don't repeat *problem* five times |
| 2 | Adequacy and appropriacy of vocabulary — topic-specific items, and words that signal the writer's attitude | Topic lexis + hedging/stance language |
| 3 | Precision of word choice and expression | The exact word, not the nearby one |
| 4 | Control and use of collocations, idiomatic expressions and sophisticated phrasing | **Word partnerships, not single words** |
| 5 | The **density and communicative effect** of spelling errors | Spelling is marked under LR |
| 6 | The **density and communicative effect** of word-formation errors | *significative*, *economical growth* → LR, not GRA |
| 7 | *(a stray empty bullet appears in the official PDF at this point — see Open questions)* | — |

**Three consequences a band-6.5 candidate usually does not know:**

1. **Spelling is an LR criterion, not a GRA criterion.** Sub-features 5 and 6 put
   spelling and word formation inside Lexical Resource. So does the band-scale
   wording at every level. Your spelling errors do not dilute across four
   criteria — they land entirely on one.
2. **The metric on errors is *density and communicative effect*, not presence.**
   Official phrasing, twice. This is the mathematical core of the whole chapter:
   errors are counted *per unit of text* and weighted by *how much they damage the
   message*. Adding an exotic word adds a numerator risk without adding
   denominator. See "Evidence" below.
3. **Only sub-feature 1 is about range.** Six of seven are about control.

The Speaking Key Assessment Criteria (ielts.org, created 2023-05-03) is
structurally the same and adds one item Writing does not have. Its "key indicators
of lexical resource", paraphrased: variety of words; adequacy and appropriacy in
terms of *referential meaning* (correctly labelling things and concepts), *style*
(formal/informal), *collocation including idiomatic expressions*, and *indicating
the speaker's attitude* (favourable / neutral / unfavourable); and **the ability to
paraphrase — getting round a vocabulary gap by using other words — with or without
noticeable hesitation.**

That last one matters enormously and is Speaking-only: *not knowing a word is not
penalised. Failing to route around it is.* Paraphrase is an explicitly assessed
skill.

### Writing LR, bands 5–8, decoded

Both Task 1 and Task 2 use near-identical LR wording. Task 1 adds the qualifier
**"within the scope of the task"** at bands 8 and 9; Task 2 does not. Restated in
my own words from the May 2023 PDF (T1 pp.3–5, T2 pp.7–9):

| Band | What the descriptor says (my paraphrase) | What it means operationally |
|---|---|---|
| **8** | A wide resource used fluently and flexibly to convey precise meanings. Skilful use of uncommon and/or idiomatic items **when appropriate**, despite occasional inaccuracies in word choice and collocation. Occasional spelling / word-formation errors with minimal impact. | Rare items are credited *only when appropriate*. Even band 8 tolerates occasional collocation error. |
| **7** | The resource is sufficient to allow **some flexibility and precision**. **Some** ability with less common and/or idiomatic items. **"An awareness of style and collocation is evident, though inappropriacies occur."** Only a few spelling / word-formation errors and they do not detract from overall clarity. | The 6→7 gate. Note the words *some* and *awareness* — this is a low bar on range and a real bar on control. |
| **6** | The resource is generally adequate and appropriate for the task. Meaning is generally clear **in spite of a rather restricted range or a lack of precision in word choice**. **"If the writer is a risk-taker, there will be a wider range of vocabulary used but higher degrees of inaccuracy or inappropriacy."** Some spelling / word-formation errors, but they do not impede communication. | **Two different people sit at band 6**: the narrow-but-safe writer, and the wide-but-wrong writer. |
| **5** | The resource is limited but minimally adequate. Simple vocabulary may be accurate but the range does not permit much variation. **Frequent** lapses in appropriacy of word choice; inflexibility shows as frequent simplifications and repetitions. Spelling / word-formation errors are noticeable and may cause the reader difficulty. | *Frequent* is the operative contrast with band 7's *a few*. |
| **4** | Resource limited/inadequate or unrelated to the task; basic and repetitive. **Inappropriate use of lexical chunks (e.g. memorised phrases, formulaic language and/or language from the input material).** Inappropriate word choice and/or word-formation and/or spelling errors **may impede meaning**. | Memorised phrases are named as a band-4 feature, in bold (= a negative feature that limits the rating). |
| **3** | Resource inadequate, which may be **because the response is significantly underlength**. Possible over-dependence on input material or memorised language. Control of word choice/spelling very limited; errors predominate and may severely impede meaning. | Under-length damages LR directly, not only Task Response. |

The PDF's own header rubric, at the top of every page: a script must **fully** fit
the positive features of a level to be rated there, and **bolded text marks
negative features that will limit a rating**. Both the "risk-taker" clause (band 6)
and the "memorised phrases" clause (band 4) are set in bold in the original.

### The band-6 "risk-taker" clause — the most important sentence in this chapter

> Band 6, Lexical Resource, both Task 1 and Task 2, May 2023 edition:
> **"If the writer is a risk-taker, there will be a wider range of vocabulary used
> but higher degrees of inaccuracy or inappropriacy."**

Read that as an official personality sketch. IELTS has written into its public
scale a description of the candidate who deliberately reaches for bigger
vocabulary, and it has placed that candidate **at band 6** — the same band as the
candidate with a "rather restricted range". The scale is explicitly telling you
that widening your range while degrading your accuracy **does not move you**. It
buys you nothing and it is the default outcome of thesaurus strategy.

Everything else in this chapter is downstream of that sentence.

### Speaking LR, bands 5–8, decoded

From `ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors.pdf` (created
2025-09-16), restated in my own words:

| Band | Lexical resource (my paraphrase) |
|---|---|
| **8** | Wide resource, readily and flexibly used to discuss all topics and convey precise meaning. Skilful use of less common and idiomatic items despite occasional inaccuracies in word choice and collocation. **Effective use of paraphrase as required.** |
| **7** | Resource flexibly used across a variety of topics. **Some** ability with less common and idiomatic items, and an **awareness of style and collocation** is evident though inappropriacies occur. **Effective use of paraphrase as required.** |
| **6** | Resource sufficient to discuss topics at length. **Vocabulary use may be inappropriate but meaning is clear.** Generally able to paraphrase successfully. |
| **5** | Resource sufficient for familiar and unfamiliar topics but limited flexibility. Attempts paraphrase but not always successfully. |
| **4** | Resource sufficient for familiar topics only; basic meaning only on unfamiliar ones. **Frequent inappropriacies and errors in word choice.** Rarely attempts paraphrase. |

**Note what is *not* there.** Speaking bands 5, 6 and 7 make no mention of
spelling (obviously) — but more usefully, band 6 explicitly tolerates
*inappropriate* vocabulary provided meaning is clear. The Speaking scale is more
forgiving of a wrong word than the Writing scale is, and it puts more weight on
**paraphrase** (named at 6, 7 and 8) than the Writing scale does. Speaking-side
practice should therefore drill *routing round a gap fluently*, not word lists.

**Cross-skill asymmetry, stated plainly:** in Writing, LR = precision + collocation
+ spelling. In Speaking, LR = flexibility + paraphrase + collocation, and *style*
is explicitly in scope (Speaking KAC names formal/informal as an appropriacy
dimension). This is why the same word can be a good choice in one paper and a bad
one in the other. See "Register" below.

## Evidence: precision beats rarity

The claim to be evidenced: **a rare word used wrongly scores worse than a common
word used correctly.** Here is the Tier-1 evidence, strongest first.

### E1 — The official examiner-comment contrast pair (5.5 vs 7.5)

Both from `https://ielts.org/cdn/Sample-tests/ielts-academic-writing-sample-tasks-2023.pdf`
(Tier 1, Author IELTS, created 2023-10-10), Task 2 sample scripts.

**Band 5.5, Task 2B Script A — examiner comment on lexis (paraphrased, key phrase
quoted):** the examiner records that a range of vocabulary *is* attempted and *is*
adequate for a good response to the task — but that "control is weak and there are
frequent spelling errors that can cause some difficulties for the reader,
**thus keeping the rating down for the lexical criterion**."

**Band 7.5, Task 2B Script B — examiner comment on lexis (paraphrased):** a wide
range of vocabulary used flexibly; the test taker can convey precise meanings;
"although awkward expressions or inappropriacies in word choice occur, these are
**only occasional and do not limit the rating for this criterion**." The examiner
then says the *lexical resource in particular* is why the response is a 7.5.

Put side by side, this is as clean an experiment as a public document can give:

| | Band 5.5 script | Band 7.5 script |
|---|---|---|
| Range of vocabulary | attempted, **adequate** | wide, flexible |
| Word-choice errors | present | present ("awkward expressions or inappropriacies") |
| **Frequency of error** | **frequent** | **occasional** |
| Effect on rating | **kept the rating down** | **did not limit the rating** |

Both candidates made word-choice/spelling errors. Both had a usable range. The
variable that moved the band two whole points was **error density**. That is
exactly the metric the Key Assessment Criteria names ("the density and
communicative effect of errors").

### E2 — The band-7.5 script's actual vocabulary is ordinary

From `https://ielts.org/cdn/computer-delivered-sample-tests-academic-writing/ielts-academic-writing-example-responses-to-parts-1-and-2-with-band-scores-and-examiner-comments.pdf`
(Tier 1, Author IELTS, created 2023-04-06), Part 2 Candidate Response 2, **Band 7.5**.

Inventory the lexis this candidate actually used: *fast food, sweets and chocolate,
busier, the art of cookery, obesity, gadgets, the latest devices, video games, bad
eyesight, laziness, spend time outdoors, applications, follow every trend,
downloading, gossips, celebraties*. There is not one showpiece word in the essay.
There is a **misspelling** (*celebraties*) and a **countability error**
(*gossips*) still sitting in a 7.5 script.

What did the examiner actually praise? Paraphrased: a wide range of vocabulary with
good use of less common items **as well as evidence of higher-level features, such
as 'softening'** — and the three examples the examiner chose to name were
**"They tend to"**, **"This appears to be"** and **"might disagree"**.

Every one of those is a *hedging structure built from the commonest words in
English*. The official examiner, given a free hand to name what made this lexis
band-7-plus, named **hedging**, not rarity.

**This is the single most actionable finding in R7.** The band-7 lexical feature
that examiners actually reward and that candidates systematically neglect is
**stance and hedging language**, not exotic nouns. Hedging kit, therefore, is a
first-class deliverable — see the Collocation sets section, table G.

### E3 — Collocation error is named as a cap, at both ends of the scale

- **Band 4**, Task 2A Script A (2023 sample tasks PDF): "frequent errors in word
  choice and **collocation** cause severe problems for the reader." Range was not
  the diagnosis; partnership error was.
- **Band 8.5**, Task 2A Script C, same PDF: the examiner credits "many examples of
  appropriate **modification, collocation and precise vocabulary choice**."

The same construct — collocation — is the stated reason for a 4 and the stated
reason for an 8.5. Rarity is named at neither end.

### E4 — Sophistication does not rescue a script; it is capped by other things

Band 7, Task 1C Script B (2023 sample tasks PDF). Examiner, paraphrased: a wide
range of **sophisticated lexis** is used to convey meaning with precision, **but
there are also occasional flaws in word choice that lead to some slightly awkward
expressions**; a wide range of structures is used fluently and the majority of
sentences are error-free — and yet "in spite of the high level of language
proficiency, the flaws in format and organisation limit the rating for this
response to Band 7."

Two lessons. (a) Even genuinely sophisticated lexis produced "flaws in word choice"
— sophistication and error travel together. (b) Vocabulary cannot buy back marks
lost on Task Achievement and Coherence. Vocabulary is one of four criteria; at
Task 2 weighting it is one-quarter of two-thirds of the Writing band.

### E5 — L1 word choice is explicitly named by an examiner

Band 5.5, Task 2 Candidate Response 1 (CD examiner-comments PDF). Examiner,
paraphrased: a range of vocabulary is attempted, but there is a lack of precision
in word choice and errors in spelling and word formation, and **"there also appears
to be some interference from the test taker's first language, e.g. 'alimentation'"**.

*Alimentation* is a direct Romance transfer (Fr. *l'alimentation* = diet /
nutrition; the English word exists but means something else and is not used this
way). The script also contains *"an inequivoque sintom"* — a reach for a rare word,
mangled in both form and spelling — and *"cigars"* for *cigarettes* (Fr. *cigarette*
vs *cigare*), and *"habbits"*, and *"discovers"* for *discoveries*.

This matters for you specifically. **An IELTS examiner, in an official publication,
named French-shaped word choice as a thing they notice and comment on.** The
interference is visible to them. It is not a private worry.

### E6 — Tier 1 says in plain words that you do not need uncommon words

IDP IELTS, *Understanding the IELTS Speaking band descriptors* (Tier 1). Its gloss
on lexical resource states that demonstrating this skill "doesn't mean that you need
to use very uncommon words, or phrases and clichés that you have memorised", and
that idiomatic language takes time and practice to use naturally.

### E7 — The descriptor's own internal logic (derivable from Tier 1)

Three clauses in the May 2023 Writing scale, taken together, make the case without
any external source:

1. Band 6 LR: the **risk-taker** clause — wider range + higher inaccuracy = band 6.
2. Band 7 LR: **"An awareness of style and collocation is evident, though
   inappropriacies occur."** — band 7 is defined by *awareness of partnership and
   register*, and it already forgives inappropriacies.
3. Band 8 LR: uncommon/idiomatic items are credited **"when appropriate"** — the
   qualifier is in the official text. An inappropriate rare item is not a
   part-credit; it is an inaccuracy, and inaccuracies are counted by density.

So: the ceiling on a common-word-but-accurate script is set by range (you will
stall around 6–7). The ceiling on a rare-word-but-inaccurate script is set by
error density (you will stall at 6, per the risk-taker clause) **and** you have
spent effort to get there. Between two candidates with the same study hours, the
precision-first candidate is strictly better off.

### E8 — Tier 2 corroboration (two independent experts, as the promotion rule requires)

- **IELTS Liz**, *IELTS Vocabulary: Paraphrasing Tips*: over-paraphrasing is named
  as a score-lowering behaviour — students "try so hard to paraphrase a word that
  they are actually making too many mistakes which will lower their band score";
  and "you do NOT aim to impress, you aim for absolute accuracy." She also
  advises **not** paraphrasing *people* repeatedly — some words should be repeated.
- **My IELTS Classroom**, *5 Common IELTS Lexical Resource Mistakes*: explicitly
  warns against thesaurus-driven substitution and against replacing functional
  language (*firstly*, *I believe*) with obscure alternatives; names dependent
  prepositions and spelling as two of the five biggest LR losses.
- **IELTS Simon**: don't chase "difficult" words; individual words are easy, the
  difficulty is words *in combination*; use **topic-specific** vocabulary rather
  than linking words, because linking words do not raise the vocabulary score.

Three independent Tier-2 experts, converging with Tier 1. The claim is safe.

### The finding, stated for the book

> Range gets you *considered* for band 7. Precision, collocation and spelling
> decide whether you get it. The official scale contains a written description of
> the candidate who widens their vocabulary at the cost of accuracy, and that
> description sits at **band 6**.

## Collocation sets

Why collocation and not word lists: LR sub-feature 4 is "the control and use of
collocations, idiomatic expressions and sophisticated phrasing"; band 7 requires
that "an awareness of style and **collocation** is evident"; band 8.5 examiner
credit was for "appropriate modification, **collocation** and precise vocabulary
choice"; band 4 examiner criticism was "errors in word choice and **collocation**".
IDP (Tier 1) states directly that using a wide range of collocations accurately
"can help you achieve a higher band score for Lexical Resource", and — a genuinely
useful detail — that the **noun and adjective forms of a collocation are the more
complex, higher-scoring ones**: *have a great influence on* outranks *influence
greatly*; *have a preference for* outranks *prefer*.

These sets are designed for this reader and this test. Designing them is authoring
work, not a factual claim; the *claim that collocation raises LR* is the sourced
part (Tier 1, above).

### G. Hedging / stance kit — the feature the official examiner actually named

Directly motivated by E2 above, where the examiner credited "softening" at band 7.5
and named *They tend to*, *This appears to be*, *might disagree*. Also serves LR
sub-feature 2 ("indicators of writer's attitude") and the Speaking KAC item
"indicating the speaker's attitude … favourable, neutral or unfavourable".

| Function | Items |
|---|---|
| Soften a generalisation | tend to, have a tendency to, are inclined to, are likely to, in most cases, by and large, on the whole, broadly speaking |
| Soften a claim | appears to, seems to, would appear to, suggests that, indicates that, points to |
| Modal hedge | may, might, could, can, is likely to, is unlikely to |
| Quantify cautiously | many, a significant proportion of, the majority of, relatively few, a minority of |
| Attribute | it is widely believed that, critics argue that, proponents claim that, according to |
| Concede | admittedly, while it is true that, granted, although this may be the case |
| Assert (use sparingly) | clearly, undoubtedly, there is little doubt that, it is evident that |
| Limit scope | to some extent, in certain contexts, particularly in developing countries, at least in the short term |

**Why this is the best value in the whole chapter for you.** Hedges are built from
words you already own; they are structurally repetitive so they are cheap to learn;
they simultaneously feed LR (attitude/appropriacy), Task Response (avoids the
band-7 fault "a tendency to over-generalise", which is named in the official Task 2
band-7 descriptor), and Coherence. Nothing else in vocabulary study pays into three
criteria at once.

## Paraphrase families for Task 1

R4 already supplies the trend-verb, adverb, approximation, comparison, proportion,
process, map and introduction-paraphrase toolkits — see
`/home/user/ielts-learning-/ielts-book/research/R4-writing-task1.md` §§A–I. **This
section deliberately does not duplicate them.** It supplies the layer R4 does not:
the **semantic precision boundaries** between the words candidates treat as
interchangeable. Getting these wrong is exactly the "lack of precision in word
choice" that the band-6 descriptor names.

### The six families, and where each one breaks

**1. PROPORTION family** — part of a whole, always relative.
*proportion · percentage · share · fraction · segment · ratio*
- ✓ *the proportion of households with internet access*
- **Breaks when:** you attach it to a raw count. ✗ *the proportion of 5 million*.
  A proportion has no unit; a figure does.
- **Precision:** *percentage* takes *of*; *per cent* follows a number
  (*40 per cent*, *the percentage of*). *Ratio* needs two things and *to*:
  *a ratio of 3 to 1*, *a ratio of men to women*.
- **Countability:** *proportion* is countable (*a small proportion*); *percentage*
  is countable (*a high percentage*).

**2. FIGURE family** — a specific reported number.
*figure · number · quantity · total · level · value · reading*
- ✓ *the figure for car use rose to 11 million*
- **Breaks when:** used for a percentage change. *The figure rose by 50%* is
  ambiguous with percentage points — R4 covers this trap.
- **Precision:** *the number of* + **countable plural** + **singular verb**
  (*the number of students **is** rising*); *a number of* + plural + **plural verb**
  (*a number of students **are** absent*). This distinction is a clean band-7 signal.
- **Register:** *figure* is the most Task-1-natural of the family. *Value* and
  *reading* suit scientific/measurement charts only.

**3. RATE family** — a quantity per unit of something else.
*rate · pace · speed · frequency · incidence · density*
- ✓ *the unemployment rate*, *the birth rate*, *at a rapid rate*
- **Breaks when:** substituted for *number* or *proportion*. ✗ *the rate of cars was
  11 million*. A rate is **per something** — per year, per 1,000 people, per capita.
- **Precision:** *the rate of increase* ≠ *the increase*. If growth slows but does
  not stop, the **rate** falls while the **figure** still rises. Writing that
  sentence correctly is a genuine band-7 move.
- **Collocation:** *at a rate of*, *a rate of X per Y*, *a growth rate*, *rates
  rose/fell*.

**4. AMOUNT family** — quantity of an uncountable thing.
*amount · quantity · volume · level · degree · extent*
- ✓ *the amount of waste*, *the volume of traffic*, *the level of investment*
- **Breaks when:** attached to a countable noun. ✗ *the amount of children*
  → **the number of children**. This is a top-three error for French speakers
  because *la quantité de* covers both. Note the official band-6 sample script
  (2023 CD PDF) contains exactly this: *"the amount of children who travelled…"* —
  in a **band 6** script.
- **Rule:** *amount / much / less* → uncountable. *number / many / fewer* →
  countable. *Quantity* leans to uncountable or measured stock; *volume* to
  flow, traffic, sales, water; *level* to abstractions (*investment, pollution,
  satisfaction*).

**5. TREND family** — direction of movement over time.
*trend · pattern · tendency · movement · trajectory · development · shift*
- ✓ *an upward trend*, *a downward trend*, *the overall pattern*, *a marked shift
  towards*
- **Breaks when:** applied to a chart with no time axis. R4 flags this: a bar chart
  comparing countries has **no trend**, it has **differences**. Writing *sales
  increased in Germany compared to France* is a lexical error, not a style choice.
- **Precision:** *trend* = sustained direction. *Fluctuation* = up-and-down with no
  net direction. *Pattern* = a shape that repeats (peaks each morning). Use
  *pattern* for a process/cycle, *trend* only for time-series direction.
- **Collocation:** *a trend **towards** X* · *the trend **for** Y* · *buck /
  reverse a trend* · *an upward/downward/steady/marked/gradual* trend.

**6. PERIOD family** — a stretch of time.
*period · era · decade · timeframe · span · interval · phase · stage*
- ✓ *over the twenty-year period*, *throughout the period*, *in the first decade*
- **Breaks when:** *period* is used for a point. ✗ *in the period of 1990* →
  *in 1990*. Also ✗ *during two years* → **for two years** / **over a two-year
  period**.
- **Precision:** *phase* and *stage* are for **processes**, not time-series;
  *period* and *span* are for **time**. Process diagrams take *stage*.
- **Hyphenation (a word-formation point, i.e. LR):** *a twenty-year period*
  (hyphen, singular *year* — it is an adjective now) vs *twenty years*
  (noun, plural). Getting *a five-year period* right is free LR credit; writing
  *a five-years period* is a word-formation error under LR sub-feature 6.

### The one-line rule

> **Proportion** = part of a whole. **Figure** = the number itself. **Rate** =
> per something. **Amount** = of uncountable stuff. **Number** = of countable
> things. **Trend** = direction over time. **Period** = the stretch of time.
> They are not synonyms. Swapping them is precisely the "lack of precision in
> word choice" that band 6 describes.

careless. *(Cross-reference: R1 covered the BC page on British vs American

## Band-6 → band-7 upgrades, with the failure cases

**Read this section as a warning as much as a resource.** Per the band-6
"risk-taker" clause, a swap is an upgrade **only if the collocation and the
register both survive it**. Below, each upgrade is given with the case where it
fails. If you cannot remember the failure case, do not make the swap.

| Band-6 word | Upgrade | Correct use | **Failure case — where the upgrade is WRONG** |
|---|---|---|---|
| important | **crucial / vital / pivotal** | *a crucial role*, *vital resources*, *a pivotal moment* | ✗ *a crucial person*, ✗ *my crucial friend*. **Also:** *important* is not a band-6 word — the band-8.5 official sample uses ordinary words freely. Swap it perhaps twice per essay, not every time. |
| good | **beneficial / advantageous / favourable** | *beneficial to health*, *a favourable outcome* | ✗ *a beneficial student*, ✗ *beneficial weather*. *Beneficial* needs a **thing that benefits something else**, and takes ***to***, not *for*. |
| bad | **detrimental / adverse / harmful** | *a detrimental effect on*, *adverse consequences* | ✗ *a detrimental person*, ✗ *the weather was adverse* (odd). *Adverse* collocates with *effects, conditions, consequences, reaction* — a short closed list. |
| big / large | **substantial / considerable / significant** | *a substantial increase*, *considerable investment* | ✗ *a substantial building* (use *large*), ✗ *a considerable man*. These are **quantity** adjectives only. |
| a lot of | **a great deal of / numerous / a significant proportion of** | *a great deal of research* (uncount.), *numerous studies* (count.) | ✗ *a great deal of students*, ✗ *numerous information*. **Countability decides which one** — see French uncountables below. |
| very | *(delete it, or use a stronger adjective)* | *exhausted* not *very tired*; *essential* not *very important* | ✗ *very crucial*, ✗ *very essential*, ✗ *very unique*. These adjectives are already absolute — intensifying them is an LR error, not emphasis. |
| think | **argue / maintain / contend / hold the view that** | *Critics argue that…* | ✗ *I contend to go* — these take **that-clauses**, not infinitives. Also *I contend* is very formal for a personal opinion; *I would argue that* is safer. |
| show (a chart) | **illustrate / depict / present / set out** | *The graph illustrates…* | ✗ *The graph depicts that…* — *depict* takes a **noun phrase**, not a that-clause. See R4 §I. |
| problem | **issue / challenge / drawback / shortcoming** | *address the issue*, *a major drawback* | ✗ *an issue in my car* (use *problem/fault*). *Issue* is a **topic of debate**; a broken thing is a *problem*. |
| help | **facilitate / assist / contribute to** | *facilitate communication*, *contribute to growth* | ✗ *assist to a lecture* — see French false friends. *Assist* takes ***in* + -ing** (*assist in reducing*) or a direct object (*assist students*). |
| get | **obtain / acquire / gain / receive** | *acquire skills*, *gain experience*, *obtain permission* | ✗ *obtain a bus*, ✗ *acquire tired*. *Get* is not always wrong — replacing every *get* mechanically produces exactly the risk-taker profile. |
| more and more | **increasingly / a growing number of** | *increasingly reliant*, *a growing number of commuters* | ✗ *increasingly people* — *increasingly* modifies **adjectives and verbs**, not nouns. Use *a growing number of* before a noun. |
| nowadays | **currently / in recent years / at present** | *In recent years, …* | ✗ ***actually*** — the French false friend. See below. Also: *nowadays* is not an error; it is just overused. |
| people | *(often: keep it)* | — | Liz's specific advice (Tier 2): do **not** repeatedly paraphrase *people*. *Individuals*, *citizens*, *the public* each carry different meaning and forcing them creates precision errors. Rotate at most once. |
| in conclusion | *(keep it)* | — | Linking phrases sit under **Coherence & Cohesion**, not LR. Replacing them with exotic alternatives (*to bring the curtains down*) gains nothing on LR and loses register. Simon (Tier 2) makes exactly this point. |

**The meta-rule for this table:**

> An upgrade is only an upgrade if you know (a) what part of speech follows it,
> (b) which preposition it takes, and (c) whether it fits an academic register.
> If you know fewer than three of those, the common word scores higher. That is
> the band-6 risk-taker clause restated as an instruction.

## French cognates: the asset and the minefield

### The asset — and it is a real one

English academic vocabulary is overwhelmingly Latinate, and French is your direct
route into it. The AWL Sublist 1 headwords are, in full:

> analyse, approach, area, assess, assume, authority, available, benefit, concept,
> consist, constitute, context, contract, create, data, define, derive, distribute,
> economy, environment, establish, estimate, evident, export, factor, finance,
> formula, function, identify, income, indicate, individual, interpret, involve,
> issue, labour, legal, legislate, major, method, occur, percent, period, policy,
> principle, proceed, process, require, research, respond, role, section, sector,
> significant, similar, source, specific, structure, theory, vary
> *(source: eapfoundation.com AWL lists page, Tier 2 academic; compiled from
> Coxhead 2000)*

Look at that list as a French speaker. *analyser, approche, aire, assumer,
autorité, concept, consister, constituer, contexte, contrat, créer, données,
définir, dériver, distribuer, économie, environnement, établir, estimer, évident,
exporter, facteur, finance, formule, fonction, identifier, indiquer, individuel,
interpréter, impliquer, légal, majeur, méthode, période, politique, principe,
procéder, processus, requérir, recherche, répondre, rôle, section, secteur,
significatif, similaire, source, spécifique, structure, théorie, varier…*

Roughly **four out of five** of the highest-frequency academic English words have a
transparent French cognate. Your effective academic vocabulary is far larger than
your study time suggests. **This is a genuine structural advantage over a Chinese,
Korean or Arabic-monolingual candidate, and you should exploit it deliberately.**

*(Note: I have not counted these formally and I am not claiming a statistic. The
observation "most of AWL Sublist 1 has a French cognate" is checkable by eye
against the list above. — `[UNVERIFIED as a quantified claim]`)*

**How to exploit it:** when you need an academic word, try the French one in an
English shape *and then check three things*: (1) does the **suffix** transfer?
(2) does the **countability** transfer? (3) is it a **false friend**? Suffix and
countability are where French speakers lose the marks the cognates gave them.

| *homeworks* | homework | a piece of homework |
| *staffs* | staff | a member of staff |
| *machineries* | machinery | a piece of machinery |
| *behaviours* (usually) | behaviour | *behaviours* exists in technical psychology only |

**Downstream agreement rule:** these all take **singular verbs** and **much / less
/ a great deal of / an amount of** — never *many / fewer / a number of*.
✓ *Much research **has** been conducted.* ✗ *Many researches **have** been conducted.*

### The minefield, part 3 — articles with abstract nouns

French keeps the definite article where English drops it. This is your
highest-frequency error class and it is **not** an LR error — it lands on **GRA**.
It is listed here because the trigger is lexical: it fires on a specific *set of
nouns*.

✗ *the society is changing* → ✓ **society is changing**
Same family, all article-less when used in a general sense: *nature, life,
technology, education, history, science, art, music, poverty, crime, pollution,
unemployment, globalisation, government* (as a concept).

The article **returns** when the noun is specific: ✓ *the society **of the
nineteenth century***, ✓ *the education **that my parents received***.

### The minefield, part 4 — register borrowed from French academic style

French academic prose is more ornate than English academic prose. Transferring it
produces exactly the "awkward expressions" examiners name. Symptoms to hunt in
your own drafts:

- Very long sentences joined by commas and *and* where English wants a full stop.
  Arabic rhetorical structure compounds this (long, additive, coordinated chains).
- *In my opinion I think that* — pick one.
- *Moreover / Furthermore* opening three consecutive paragraphs.
- Elaborate throat-clearing openings (*Since the dawn of time, humanity has…*) —
  these read as memorised formulaic language, which is a **bold negative feature at
  band 4** in the official LR descriptor.
- Nominalising everything. English academic prose tolerates a verb.

## Retention method

### The constraint

90 minutes a day, 6–10 weeks, one skill per session. Vocabulary is not one of the
four skills, so it cannot own a session. It has to run **alongside** and cost
almost nothing.

### The evidence base

Kim & Webb (2022), *The Effects of Spaced Practice on Second Language Learning: A
Meta-Analysis*, **Language Learning** 72(2), 269–319 — a peer-reviewed meta-analysis
of 98 effect sizes from 48 experiments, n = 3,411. Reported findings: spaced
conditions significantly outperformed massed conditions; **longer spacing beat
shorter spacing at longer retention intervals**; equal and expanding schedules were
statistically equivalent. *(Retrieved via publisher listing and Semantic Scholar;
the Wiley full text returned HTTP 403 — see Open questions.)*

Two operational conclusions follow, and only two:
1. **Space it.** Ten minutes daily beats seventy minutes on Sunday.
2. **Do not over-engineer the interval.** Since equal and expanding schedules came
   out statistically equivalent, a simple fixed ladder is fine. Do not spend study
   time tuning an algorithm.

The unit of study is set by Tier 1 and Tier 2 together: the KAC assesses
**collocations**, and Simon's position (Tier 2) is that single words are easy and
combinations are hard. Therefore **the flashcard's front is never a single word.**

### The method

**The card format.** Your repo already has `progress/vocab-bank.md` and an
`/anki` export to `exports/anki-vocab.csv`. Use them, with this discipline:

| Field | Rule |
|---|---|
| Front | The **collocation or phrase**, with a gap — *place a strain ___ health services* — or a short L1 prompt |
| Back | The full phrase + **one sentence you wrote yourself**, on an IELTS topic |
| Meaning | In English if you can, French only if you must |
| Topic | One of the ten topics above |
| Last tested | Date, for the ladder |

Never a bare word. Never an English→French translation pair.

**The ladder.** Day 1 → Day 3 → Day 7 → Day 16 → Day 35. Anything you fail drops
back to Day 1. Five successful reviews over five weeks is enough to survive a test
date at week 6–10.

**The daily 10 minutes, inside the 90.**
- **5 min, start of session:** review the due cards. Recall **actively** — say or
  type the phrase before flipping. Recognition is worthless; you need production.
- **5 min, end of session:** mine the session you just did. Take **no more than 5
  items** from your own graded work — words the examiner (me) corrected, or words
  you needed and did not have. Nothing from a listicle.

**The weekly 20 minutes, folded into `/review`.**
- Write **five sentences** using ten of the week's items, on the topic you are
  weakest at. Have them graded for collocation and register specifically.
- Delete any card you have failed four times. It is above your level; the cost of
  keeping it exceeds the marks it can buy.

### The three rules that make this work

1. **Harvest from your own errors first.** Your error log is a better vocabulary
   syllabus than any published list, because it targets your actual gaps and
   because you already have retrieval cues attached (you remember the essay).
2. **Cap the intake.** ~5 new items a day × 6 weeks ≈ 210 items, of which maybe
   140 will be usable. That is far more than the "4 or 5 less common items"
   Tier-2 practitioners say a band 7 needs *(flagged: that number is a
   practitioner heuristic and is **not** in the descriptors — see MYTHS/Open
   questions)*. More intake does not help; retention and accuracy do.
3. **A word is not learned until you have used it in a graded piece and it was not
   corrected.** That is the only test that matches the exam. Move items through
   three states in `vocab-bank.md`: **learned → used → survived grading**. Only
   the third state counts.

### Fitting it to the topics

Six to ten weeks, ten topics. Run **one topic per week** (weeks 1–8 or 1–10 doubled
up), with the week's Writing Task 2 drill and Speaking session on that same topic.
This gives you the massed *encounter* and spaced *review* combination the
meta-analysis supports, and it means every item gets a production test in the same
week you meet it.

## MYTHS

Vocabulary advice that **lowers** the score, with the mechanism.

**MYTH 1 — "Use rare and impressive words to show off your range."**
**Why it lowers the score:** the May 2023 band-6 LR descriptor contains, in bold,
the risk-taker clause: a wider range with higher inaccuracy or inappropriacy is
**band 6**. Band 8 credits uncommon items only "when appropriate". The official
band 7.5 sample essay contains no showpiece vocabulary at all; the examiner
credited *hedging*. Rare words add error density (the metric the KAC names) without
adding any credit unless the collocation, part of speech and register are all
correct. Tier 1 (IDP) states plainly that you do not need very uncommon words.

**MYTH 2 — "Paraphrase every word from the prompt; never repeat a word."**
**Why it lowers the score:** two mechanisms. (a) Forced paraphrase is the single
biggest source of precision errors — Liz (Tier 2): students "try so hard to
paraphrase a word that they are actually making too many mistakes which will lower
their band score". (b) Some words have **no** synonym (a country, *carbon dioxide
emissions*, *people*). Substituting one is a bigger LR error than repeating.
Repetition of *general* words costs you under sub-feature 1; a wrong substitution
costs you under sub-features 3 and 4, which are weighted more heavily by the
band descriptions.

**MYTH 3 — "Memorise a bank of band-9 sentences and phrases to drop in."**
**Why it lowers the score:** the LR descriptor names this explicitly at **band 4**,
in bold: inappropriate use of lexical chunks, e.g. memorised phrases, formulaic
language. Band 3 names over-dependence on memorised language. And **band 0** is
reserved partly for a response "totally memorised". Memorised phrases are not
neutral — they are a scored negative. An examiner recognises them instantly
because they sit at a different level from the rest of the script.

**MYTH 4 — "Learn the AWL (all 570 word families) — it's the academic vocabulary."**
**Why it is bad advice for you, specifically:**
- *What the AWL is:* Coxhead (2000), 570 word families, from a 3.5-million-word
  corpus of 414 academic texts across science, arts, commerce and law; selected on
  range (10+ occurrences in each of four sub-corpora, present in ≥15 of 28 subject
  areas), frequency (100+ in corpus), and **exclusion of the first 2,000 GSL word
  families**. Coverage ≈10% of the source academic corpus. *(Therova 2020, TESL-EJ
  24(1), reviewing Coxhead 2000 — Tier 2 academic.)*
- *Critique from the literature:* Hyland & Tse (2007, TESOL Quarterly) found AWL
  items are not of equal value across disciplines — words differ in range,
  collocation, frequency and meaning between fields, and they argue the list can
  "mislead learners into believing that there is a single collection of words which
  they can learn and transfer across fields". Coxhead's own figures show uneven
  coverage (commerce 12% vs science 9.1%).
- *Why it fails the helps-not-hurts filter for IELTS:* the AWL is a **reading-
  comprehension** tool — it was built to tell you which words you will *meet* in
  academic texts. It is not a production list. It is organised by frequency, not by
  collocation, so it teaches exactly the unit that Tier 1 says is assessed
  (sub-feature 4: collocations) the wrong way round. And 570 families is arithmetic
  suicide on a 6–10 week schedule at 5 items/day.
- **The honest verdict:** the AWL is a legitimate *checking* resource — if a word
  is in Sublist 1–3, it is safe, mid-frequency academic register, and probably has
  a French cognate you already half-know. Use it that way. **Memorising it as a
  list is bad advice.** Learn ~10 collocations per topic instead.
  *(Caveat in fairness: the Hyland & Tse critique is about discipline-specific
  variation, and IELTS Task 2 is deliberately general-interest and
  non-disciplinary. So the critique bites less hard for IELTS than for EAP. The
  argument against memorising the AWL for IELTS rests mainly on the wrong-unit and
  time-budget grounds, not on Hyland & Tse. Flagged for verifiers.)*

**MYTH 5 — "Use lots of idioms to sound natural."**
**Why it lowers the score in Writing:** the British Council (Tier 1) states that
using the wrong register is one of the commonest mistakes in the IELTS Writing test
and that slang is never appropriate in formal contexts; phrasal verbs like *figure
out* and *call off* are named as not always appropriate academically. But note the
**genuine nuance**, because half the internet gets this wrong in the opposite
direction: the Writing band-7 descriptor *does* say "some ability to use less common
and/or **idiomatic** items", and band 8 says "skilful use of uncommon and/or
idiomatic items **when appropriate**". "Idiomatic" in descriptor language means
**natural, native-like phrasing and collocation** — *a growing body of evidence*,
*strike a balance* — not colourful figurative idioms (*rain cats and dogs*, *the ball
is in their court*). The first raises LR in Writing; the second lowers it on
appropriacy.
**In Speaking it flips.** IDP (Tier 1) explicitly recommends idioms for Speaking,
notes they are assessed from band 7, and warns only against overuse, topic mismatch
and forced/overused ones. The Speaking descriptor at band 7 credits "less common and
idiomatic items".
**Rule:** figurative idioms → Speaking Parts 1 and 3, sparingly. Never in Academic
Writing. Idiomatic *collocation* → both.

**MYTH 6 — "Replace linking words with sophisticated alternatives to boost
vocabulary."**
**Why it does nothing:** linking words are assessed under **Coherence & Cohesion**,
not Lexical Resource — the KAC puts "the appropriate use of discourse markers" under
CC. Swapping *In conclusion* for something exotic gains zero LR marks and risks
register. Simon (Tier 2) makes this point directly: linking words do not help your
vocabulary score. Worse, the CC descriptors at bands 5 and 6 penalise **overuse** of
cohesive devices, so decorating them can lose marks on the criterion they *are*
scored under.

**MYTH 7 — "Cambridge research shows 73% of test-takers lose Lexical Resource marks
on collocation."**
**Why this is in MYTHS:** I traced this claim. It appears on `langogh.com`
(a Tier 3 commercial listicle), attributed to "Cambridge Assessment research" with
**no study named, no year, no link**. A targeted search for the figure surfaced only
that same page. It does not appear in any Cambridge or IELTS publication I
retrieved. **Treat as fabricated until someone produces the study.** Flagged because
it is exactly the kind of authoritative-sounding number that propagates into prep
books. *(The underlying qualitative claim — that collocation matters more than range
— is well supported; see the Evidence section. The number is not.)*

**MYTH 8 — "Long words are more academic than short ones."**
**Why it lowers the score:** LR sub-feature 3 is *precision*, not length. The
band-8.5 official sample was credited for "precise vocabulary choice", not long
words. *Use* beats *utilise* in almost every IELTS sentence (*utilise* implies
putting something to a practical use it was not designed for). Choosing the longer
word by reflex is the risk-taker profile in miniature.

**MYTH 9 — "Spelling is a minor issue / spelling is marked under grammar."**
**Why it costs you badly:** spelling is inside **Lexical Resource** (KAC
sub-features 5 and 6), and it appears at every band from 3 to 9. The band 5.5
official sample was held down **specifically** because "frequent spelling errors …
can cause some difficulties for the reader, thus keeping the rating down for the
lexical criterion". A French speaker's predictable misspellings — *developement,
governement, maintainance, adress, exemple, independant, recieve, occured,
enviroment, responsable* — are concentrated, learnable, and worth a full half band.
Note also: computer-delivered IELTS has **no spellchecker**.

**MYTH 10 — "A thesaurus is a study tool."**
**Why it lowers the score:** a thesaurus gives you synonyms without collocation,
register or countability, which are three of the four things LR actually measures.
My IELTS Classroom (Tier 2) names thesaurus use as a top-five LR mistake. **Use a
collocation dictionary or a learner's dictionary with example sentences instead** —
you need to see the word in a phrase, which is the assessed unit.
