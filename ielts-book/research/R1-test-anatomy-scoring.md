## Verified facts

### 1. Test papers, order, timing, breaks

- The four papers are Listening, Reading, Writing, Speaking; **total test time is
  "2 hours and 45 minutes"** `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic test format and sections]`
- **Listening: "Approximately 30 minutes"**, **4 parts**, **40 questions**
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Listening test format]`
- **Academic Reading: "60 minutes (including transfer time)"**, **3 passages**,
  total text length **"2150–2750 words"**, **40 questions**
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`
- **Writing: "Time allowed: 60 minutes"**; Task 1 "at least 150 words in about 20
  minutes"; Task 2 "at least 250 words in about 40 minutes"
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Writing test format]`
- **Speaking: "11–14 minutes"**, 3 parts — Part 1 "4–5 minutes long", Part 2 "3–4
  minutes, including the preparation time", Part 3 "4–5 minutes". It is a
  "face-to-face interview between the test taker and an examiner" and "The
  Speaking test is recorded"
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Speaking test format]`
- **Order and breaks:** "You'll take the first three parts of the test on the same
  day, in the following order: Listening, Reading and Writing" and "there are no
  breaks between these tests"
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS test format explained]`
- ielts.org states the Listening + Reading + Writing session "takes 2 hours 40
  minutes, and there are no breaks between each section of the test"
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS test day advice: what to expect]`
  → The 2h45m headline and the 2h40m session figure are **different quantities**:
  2h40m = paper-mode L(30) + transfer(10) + R(60) + W(60); the 2h45m headline is
  the four papers including a ~15-minute Speaking test and **excluding** the
  10-minute Listening transfer. Both appear on official pages; neither is wrong,
  but a book must not present them as the same number. **[CORRECTS PRIOR WORK —
  prior notes carried 2h45m with no explanation of the 2h40m figure]**
- **Speaking on a different day:** "Your Speaking test will be held either on the
  same day or seven days before or after that"
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS test format explained]`
  The British Council FAQ page words it differently: "either on the same day or
  seven days before or two days after that, depending on local arrangements"
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Frequently asked questions]`
  IDP says: for IELTS on computer, "the Speaking test before or after this test
  session" on the same day; for paper, "In some test centres, you will sit the
  Speaking test on the same day, or up to 7 days before or after your test date"
  `[src: ielts.idp.com — What to expect on IELTS test day]`
  → The window is **centre-dependent**; the safe statement for the book is "same
  day, or up to 7 days before or after, depending on your centre — confirm with
  your centre." The "two days after" variant is flagged below.
- Delaying the Speaking test to a week later "may cause a delay in your IELTS
  results" `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Frequently asked questions]`

### 2. Listening internals (relevant to timing)

- Each recording is heard **once only**: "You will hear each recording ONCE only."
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS FAQs, Academic module (PDF)]`
- Questions follow the order of the recording: "Yes. This is true for all question
  types in IELTS Listening."
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS FAQs, Academic module (PDF)]`
- "There is a pause between each of the parts. Also, there is one break during
  Parts 1, 2 and 3 to allow you time to look at the following questions. In Part 4
  there is a short pause in the middle of the recording … but this is not
  mentioned on the recording."
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS FAQs, Academic module (PDF)]`
  → **Part 4 has no signposted mid-part reading gap.** This matters for training.
- Spelling and grammar are marked: "Yes. You will lose marks for incorrect spelling
  and grammar." Both UK and US spellings are accepted.
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS FAQs, Academic module (PDF)]`
- Over the word limit = wrong: "Answers which are over the word limit will be
  marked as incorrect." Two answers where one is required = no mark, "Even if one
  of your answers is correct, you will not receive a mark."
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS FAQs, Academic module (PDF)]`

### 3. Reading internals

- **No extra transfer time in either mode:** "You must transfer your answers during
  the hour you are given for the Reading test. Unlike the Listening test, no extra
  transfer time is given."
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`
  Reinforced: "You will NOT be given extra time to do this at the end of the test."
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS FAQs, Academic module (PDF)]`
- Official pacing advice: "You should spend about 20 minutes on each text."
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS FAQs, Academic module (PDF)]`
- 11 official Academic Reading question types are listed: multiple choice;
  identifying information (True/False/Not given); identifying writer's
  views/claims (Yes/No/Not given); matching information; matching headings;
  matching features; matching sentence endings; sentence completion;
  summary/note/table/flow-chart completion; diagram label completion; short-answer
  questions `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`

### The transfer-time trap — resolved definitively

**The 10-minute Listening transfer time does NOT exist in computer-delivered
IELTS.** Two independent Tier 1 sources state it explicitly:
- "At the end of the Listening section you only have two minutes to check your
  answers." `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS on paper vs computer: how to choose]`
- "The Listening section takes about 30 minutes, however, no extra time is given to
  transfer your answers… You will only have 2 minutes to review your answers at the
  end of the test." `[src: ielts.idp.com — How IELTS on computer works]`

A third confirms the paper-only nature of the 10 minutes: "If you are taking a
paper-based test, transfer your answers at the end of the test. You have 10 minutes
for this." `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS FAQs, Academic module (PDF)]`

**The trap for writers:** `ielts.org`'s Listening format page still says
"Approximately 30 minutes (plus 10 minutes to transfer your answers to an answer
sheet)" with **no computer caveat at all**
`[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Listening test format]`. Any book that quotes
that page alone will teach the reader something false about the test he will
actually sit. Prior work got this right; it must survive editing.

## Scoring mechanics

### Band scale
Nine-band scale, 0–9. "There is no pass or fail in IELTS." All section scores and
the overall score are reported "in whole bands or half bands, e.g. 7.0, 8.5."
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers (PDF)]`

### Listening and Academic Reading: raw → band

40 questions, "Each correct answer is awarded 1 mark."
`[src: ielts.org — IELTS scoring in detail: band scores explained]`

**This is the complete officially published conversion. There is no fuller
official table.** Only four anchor points per test are published:

| Band | Listening (marks /40) | Academic Reading (marks /40) | General Training Reading (marks /40) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 4 | — | — | 15 |
| 5 | **16** | **15** | 23 |
| 6 | **23** | **23** | 30 |
| 7 | **30** | **30** | 35 |
| 8 | **35** | **35** | — |

`[src: ielts.org — IELTS scoring in detail: band scores explained]` and
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers (PDF)]`
(identical figures in both)

**Official caveat — must be reproduced in the book:**
- "The precise number of marks needed to achieve these band scores will vary
  slightly from test version to test version."
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS scoring in detail]`
- "Although all IELTS test materials are pretested and standards fixed before being
  released as live tests, there are inevitably minor differences in the difficulty
  level across tests. To equate different tests, the band score boundaries are set
  so that all test takers' results relate to the same scale of achievement. This
  means, for example, that the Band 6 boundary may be set at a slightly different
  raw score across individual tests."
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers (PDF)]`

**Do not publish half-band raw scores (6.5 = 26–29 etc.).** No official source
gives them. Every such table online is a Tier 2/3 reconstruction from retired
Cambridge practice books, not from IELTS. `[UNVERIFIED — half-band raw-score
thresholds]`

**Reader's working targets** (state as approximate, with the caveat attached):
- Band 6 floor: **23/40** in each of Listening and Academic Reading
- Band 6.5: between 23 and 30 — **assume 27/40 to be safe** `[UNVERIFIED]`
- Band 7: **30/40**
- Band 8: **35/40**

Note the one asymmetry: Academic Reading needs **15** for band 5 while Listening
needs **16**; from band 6 upward the two are identical. GT Reading is markedly
harder per band (30 marks for band 6 vs 23 for Academic).

### Writing and Speaking: criterion marking

Both are marked by certificated examiners on **four criteria, each weighted 25%**:

| Writing criterion | Weight | Speaking criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Task achievement (T1) / Task response (T2) | 25% | Fluency and coherence | 25% |
| Coherence and cohesion | 25% | Lexical resource | 25% |
| Lexical resource | 25% | Grammatical range and accuracy | 25% |
| Grammatical range and accuracy | 25% | Pronunciation | 25% |

`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers (PDF)]`

"Each task is assessed independently. The criteria are weighted equally and the
score on the task is the average."
`[src: ielts.org — Understanding and setting IELTS scores]`

### Task 2 double-weighting — CONFIRMED, with a caveat

Three Tier 1 sources use the **exact** phrase:
- "Task 2 contributes twice as much as Task 1 to the Writing score"
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Writing test format]`
- "Are the two tasks both worth the same number of marks? No. Task 2 contributes
  twice as much as Task 1 to the Writing score."
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS FAQs, Academic module (PDF)]`
- "The assessment of Task 2 carries more weight in marking than Task 1."
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Writing key assessment criteria (PDF)]`

**Caveat that must not be glossed over:** the **1:2 ratio is official**, but IELTS
does **not** publish the arithmetic used to combine the two task scores into the
reported Writing band, nor how the result is rounded to a half band. The formula
usually taught —

> Writing band ≈ (Task 1 band + 2 × Task 2 band) ÷ 3

— is a **reasonable inference from the published ratio, not a published formula**.
Present it as a planning heuristic, not as IELTS's method.
`[UNVERIFIED — exact combination and rounding formula for the Writing band]`

Worked example of the heuristic (label it as such):
- T1 = 6.0, T2 = 6.5 → (6.0 + 13.0) ÷ 3 = 6.33 → reported Writing likely **6.0** or 6.5
- T1 = 5.5, T2 = 7.0 → (5.5 + 14.0) ÷ 3 = 6.5 → **6.5**
- T1 = 7.0, T2 = 5.5 → (7.0 + 11.0) ÷ 3 = 6.0 → **6.0**
  → The last two show the asymmetry: a strong Task 2 rescues a weak Task 1; the
  reverse is much weaker. Spending the honest 40 minutes on Task 2 is arithmetic,
  not advice.

### Overall band: averaging and rounding

Rule: "The overall band score is the average of the four section band scores
rounded to the nearest half band. … if the average of the four sections ends in
.25, the overall band score is rounded up to the next half band, and if it ends in
.75, the overall band score is rounded up to the next whole band."
`[src: ielts.org — IELTS scoring in detail: band scores explained]`

Official worked examples (all three published):

| | L | R | W | S | Sum | Average | Reported |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Test taker A | 6.5 | 6.5 | 5.0 | 7.0 | 25.0 | 6.25 | **6.5** (rounded up) |
| Test taker B | 4.0 | 3.5 | 4.0 | 4.0 | 15.5 | 3.875 | **4.0** (nearest half) |
| Test taker C | 6.5 | 6.5 | 5.5 | 6.0 | 24.5 | 6.125 | **6.0** (rounded down) |

`[src: ielts.org — IELTS scoring in detail]` and
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers (PDF)]` — identical
examples in both, which is a strong cross-check.

| Writing stuck, Reading pushed | 7.5 | 7.5 | 6.0 | 6.5 | 27.5 | 6.875 | **7.0** | but Writing 6.0 fails a 6.5 sub-score requirement |
| Balanced band 7 | 7.0 | 7.0 | 7.0 | 7.0 | 28.0 | 7.0 | **7.0** | — |

**The single most valuable arithmetic fact for this reader:** because .75 rounds up
to the next *whole* band, a total of **27.0 out of 28** (i.e. average 6.75) already
reports as **7.0**. He does not need four 7.0s. He needs three 7.0s and one 6.5 —
or 7.5/7.5/6.0/6.5, except that a 6.0 breaks the 6.5-per-section requirement. Given
the per-section floor of 6.5, the minimum viable profile is **6.5 / 6.5 / 6.5 / 7.0
= 26.5 → 6.625 → 6.5** (fails), so realistically **7.0 / 7.0 / 6.5 / 6.5 = 6.75 →
7.0** is the cheapest legitimate route: push Listening and Reading (objectively
markable, fastest to train) to 30/40, and get Writing and Speaking to 6.5.

## Strategies found

Only Tier 1 official strategy is recorded here; Tier 2 skill-specific technique is
the scope of agents R2–R6.

**Tier 1 — official IELTS/Cambridge advice** `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS
FAQs, Academic module (PDF)]`:
- Listening: "Write all your answers as you listen – remember, you won't hear the
  recording a second time." Critical on computer, where there is no transfer time.
- Listening: "Try to answer all the questions, even if you don't feel sure about an
  answer" — there is no negative marking.
- Listening: "Don't try to rephrase what you hear. Try to write down the words you
  hear which fit the question."
- Reading: "Don't waste time reading the whole text each time for each set of
  questions." "Skim the questions, where appropriate, before reading the text so
  that you have a purpose for reading."
- Reading: answers must be spelled and grammatically correct — "Spelling mistakes
  will mean that you will lose the mark for that question."
- Writing: "Finish Writing Task 1 after about 20 minutes to allow enough time to
  answer Writing Task 2. Remember Task 2 contributes twice as much as Task 1."
- Writing Task 1 (Academic): "you do not need to guess about the reasons for things
  in Task 1. (For example, do not write *I think this is probably because …*)"
- Writing Task 1: an introduction is expected — "you need to write an introduction,
  although this can be very short. Also, a short summary of the main trends or
  features is a good way to finish."
- Writing Task 2: "There is no separate assessment for introductions and
  conclusions. However, if you do not write an introduction and conclusion, you may
  be penalised under 'Task response' and/or 'Coherence and cohesion'."
- Writing: "Don't simply copy words and phrases from the question paper – try to use
  your own words at all times by paraphrasing the question."
- Speaking: "Practise ways of delaying answers to give yourself time to think in
  Part 3."
- Speaking Part 2: "You will have the opportunity to talk for 2 minutes. You should
  try to talk for the full 2 minutes." Being stopped is neutral: "Don't worry if the
  examiner stops you in the Part 2 long turn. It means you have spoken for 2 minutes."
- Speaking Part 2: notes are optional; "Don't write on the Part 2 task card."
- Speaking: "It is OK to correct yourself. If you can't correct yourself, forget it
  and carry on."

**Tier 1 — computer-mode specific:**
- Highlighting is free of consequence: "you can highlight as much or as little text
  as you want without any impact on your test result", and you need not clear it
  before moving on `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS on computer:
  Highlighting text]`
- Use the untimed official familiarisation test to learn the interface before test
  day; it uses the real test screens
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Free IELTS familiarisation test]`
- On computer, note on paper any Listening item you want to revisit — the 2-minute
  window closes automatically `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS on paper
  vs computer]`

**Tier 2 — recorded but not relied on:**
- IELTS Liz restates the official rounding rules correctly and gives the same 6.25 →

## Common mistakes & traps

1. **Believing the 10-minute Listening transfer applies on computer.** It does not.
   It is 2 minutes to *check*, not 10 minutes to *transfer*. `ielts.org`'s own
   Listening page is the trap, because it states the 10 minutes with no
   computer-mode caveat.
2. **Quoting a "complete" raw-score-to-band table.** Only bands 5–8 are officially
   published, and even those carry a "varies slightly from test version to test
   version" caveat. Half-band thresholds are folklore.
3. **Saying "2 hours 45 minutes" and "2 hours 40 minutes" interchangeably.** They
   measure different things (see §1).
4. **Claiming under-length writing carries a fixed word-count penalty.** The
   official position is more subtle and is a genuine Tier 1 conflict — see below.
5. **Assuming One Skill Retake is a safety net.** It requires a computer-delivered
   full test, a 60-day window, the same country, a participating centre, one use per
   full test, and acceptance by the receiving institution. Any one of those can fail.
6. **Assuming paper is still bookable.** In Tunisia the British Council lists only
   computer and Online.
7. **Assuming Speaking is always the same day.** It can be up to a week either side,
   and choosing a later slot can delay results.
8. **Copying the question wording into an essay.** "Any copied rubric must be
   discounted" — it does not count as your writing.
   `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Writing band descriptors (PDF)]`
9. **Writing two answers in Listening/Reading to hedge.** "Even if one of your
   answers is correct, you will not receive a mark."
   `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS FAQs (PDF)]`
10. **Exceeding the stated word limit in Listening/Reading gap-fills.** "Answers
    which are over the word limit will be marked as incorrect."
    `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS FAQs (PDF)]`

**The under-length conflict — important, and it contradicts this repository's
current coaching rule. [CORRECTS PRIOR WORK]**

- `ielts.org` Writing format page: "you will be penalised if your answer is too
  short" `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Writing test format]`
- Cambridge official FAQ: "**There is no direct penalty** for writing fewer than 150
  words for the Task 1 question and 250 words for the Task 2 question. However,
  writing fewer words may impact on the range of ideas and evidence produced and may
  therefore affect your score."
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS FAQs, Academic module (PDF)]`
- The May-2023 band descriptors contain **no word-count deduction**. Under-length
  appears only as a *cause* of low criterion scores: band 3 LR — "The resource is
  inadequate (which may be due to the response being significantly underlength)";
  band 3 GRA — "Length may be insufficient to provide evidence of control of
  sentence forms." Plus the hard floor: "Responses of 20 words or fewer are rated at
  Band 1."
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Writing band descriptors (PDF)]`
- The key assessment criteria define TA/TR as fulfilling the task "using a minimum
  of 150 words" / "using a minimum of 250 words" — i.e. length is folded into Task
  Achievement/Response, not deducted separately.
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Writing key assessment criteria (PDF)]`

**Resolution for the book (and for `CLAUDE.md`'s grading rule):** there is **no
fixed arithmetic penalty** for under-length. A short script is punished *through*
the criteria — most reliably Task Achievement/Response (the task cannot be "fully"
addressed in 190 words), then Lexical Resource and Grammatical Range if length is
too short to evidence range. The correct coaching sentence is: *"You wrote 212
words. There is no fixed deduction, but 212 words cannot fully develop two
positions, so Task Response is capped at 6."* Not: *"minus half a band for being
under length."* This is a real change from the repository's current instruction to
"apply the penalty and say so explicitly."

## Band-descriptor implications

- **Each criterion is 25% of its task's band** — so on Task 2, Coherence & Cohesion
  is worth exactly as much as Task Response. Coaching that treats CC as a
  linking-words afterthought is mis-weighting a quarter of the mark.
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers (PDF)]`
- **Writing is the reader's binding constraint.** Task 2 is double-weighted within
  Writing, and Writing is the lowest-mean skill for both Arabic and French L1
  candidates. Task 2 therefore carries roughly **2/3 of the Writing band**, and
  Writing carries **1/4 of the overall band** — so Task 2 alone is about **17% of
  the whole IELTS result**, more than any other single task in the test.
- **Memorised language is descriptor-visible, not just an ethics rule.** The
  descriptors name memorised chunks as evidence *against* Lexical Resource and
  Grammatical Range at bands 2–4, and total memorisation triggers band 0. A reader
  with strong French academic register is at real risk of importing memorised
  formulae; this must be trained out.
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Writing band descriptors (PDF)]`
- **Task 1 must not speculate.** The key criteria define Academic Task 1 as "an
  information-transfer task, which relates narrowly to the factual content of a
  diagram, graph, table, chart, map or other visual input, **not to speculative
  explanations that lie outside the given data**". Speculation is a Task Achievement
  error, not a stylistic one.
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Writing key assessment criteria (PDF)]`
- **Task 1 TA explicitly requires comparison, not description**: "comparing or
  contrasting the information by adequately highlighting the identifiable trends,
  principal changes or differences in the data … (rather than mechanical description
  reporting detail)."
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Writing key assessment criteria (PDF)]`
- **Task 2 TR explicitly requires a position and a conclusion**: it assesses "how
  clearly the candidate opens the discourse, establishes their position and
  formulates conclusions." An essay that never commits loses TR marks by definition.
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Writing key assessment criteria (PDF)]`
- **Speaking pronunciation is 25%** — equal to grammar. For an Arabic/French L1
  speaker this is a scoreable, trainable quarter, not a fixed accent penalty.
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers (PDF)]`
