# R5 — Writing Task 2 (Academic)

Research dossier for the revision book. Reader: Arabic (Tunisian) + French L1,
target band 7.0 overall, 6.5 minimum per section, 6–10 weeks of self-study.

**Why this chapter matters most.** Task 2 is worth two thirds of the Writing band
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources], and Writing is the weakest paper for
both of this reader's L1 groups. Every half-band gained here is worth double a
half-band on Task 1.

**Method note.** Everything marked Tier 1 was pulled from official PDFs
downloaded and text-extracted locally (pdfplumber), or from live official pages.
The band descriptor PDF used throughout is the **May 2023 edition**, confirmed by
PDF metadata (`Author: IELTS`, `CreationDate: D:20230503120242`, `Title: IELTS
Writing Band Descriptors`). Task 2 descriptors are on pages 7–9. The 2013 edition
circulating at `assets.ctfassets.net/.../writingbanddescriptorstask1and2.pdf` was
**not** used and must not be cited — see the "throughout" finding in §2 for a
place where the two editions actually differ.

### What Task Response is officially defined as

> Candidates must "formulate and develop a position in relation to a given prompt
> in the form of a question or statement, **using a minimum of 250 words**. Ideas
> should be supported by evidence, and examples may be drawn from a candidate's
> own experience."
> [src: ielts.org — Key assessment criteria PDF, p.2; identical wording in the CD sample responses PDF, p.1]

The five things TR assesses, officially:

1. how fully you respond to the task
2. how adequately the main ideas are **extended** and **supported**
3. how relevant your ideas are
4. how clearly you open the discourse, **establish your position**, and formulate conclusions
5. how appropriate the format is
[src: ielts.org — Key assessment criteria PDF, p.3]

Official gloss on 2, which is the single most useful sentence in this whole
dossier for a band-6 writer:

- **Extension** = adding greater detail and depth to the idea.
- **Support** = relevant examples, which may come from your own knowledge or experience.
- **No specialist knowledge is required** to answer well.
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources]

### The band-5 cap that most candidates trip over

> "To achieve Band 6 or higher, you should address all the main parts of the
> prompt when answering the question… **If you don't discuss both, you will be
> limited to Band 5.**"
> [src: ielts.org — Writing test resources]

This is corroborated by the descriptor grid itself, where the band-5 TR cell
"incompletely addressed" is set in **bold** — and the PDF's own legend says
bolded text marks negative features that limit a rating. Band 6 tolerates uneven
coverage ("some may be more fully covered than others"); band 5 is where a part
is left *incomplete*. [src: ielts.org — Writing band descriptors PDF, p.8]

### Copied rubric

Three independent Tier 1 statements, converging:

1. Descriptor grid, band-1 TR cell: **"Any copied rubric must be discounted."**
   [src: ielts.org — Writing band descriptors PDF, p.9]
2. Examiner comment on an official Task 2 script: *"The first five lines of this
   response are directly copied rubric; **no credit is given for copied rubric**."*
   (Band 5.5) [src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF, p.24]
3. British Council test-day advice: **"Don't copy entire sentences from the
   question – you will not get any marks for this."**
   [src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test day advice]

And on a second official script (Band 6.5): *"It is a shame that the first
paragraph, and beginning of the second are mainly copied from the rubric… The
repetition of language from the rubric, while integrated, reveals a lack of
ability to paraphrase."* [src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF, p.20]

Note what the examiner did there: copying didn't just cost Task Response, it was
used as **evidence against Lexical Resource** — inability to paraphrase.

**Does copied rubric come off the word count?** Tier 1 says it is "discounted"
and earns "no marks", but never states the mechanics. Two independent Tier 2
sources say the words are not counted:
- "If you copy the information given by IELTS for your introduction, the examiner
  will not count those words." [src: ieltsliz.com — How many words for IELTS writing]
- "If you copy the task question word for word without attempting to paraphrase
  then the examiner will not count those words." [src: ieltsfocus.com — Word count in IELTS writing]
Also asserted by IELTS Advantage. **Promoted** under rule (a)+(b): consistent
with Tier 1 ("discounted") and confirmed by 2+ Tier 2 experts.

**Practical consequence, and this is the sibling agent's point, quantified:** a
typical Task 2 prompt statement runs 25–45 words. If you copy it verbatim into
your introduction and write 265 words in total, your *counted* length can land
around 225–240 — under length, invisibly. The band-5.5 official script had
**five lines** of copied rubric. Paraphrasing is therefore not a stylistic nicety;
it is what keeps your word count real.

### Under-length — how it actually costs marks

This needed care, because the popular account ("automatic band 5") is a
historical rule, not a current published one.

**What Tier 1 actually says.** Only three things:

1. TR is *defined* as fulfilling the task "using a minimum of 250 words". Under
   length, you have not met the definition of the task.
   [src: ielts.org — Key assessment criteria PDF, p.2]
2. **"Responses of 20 words or fewer are rated at Band 1"** — in all four
   criteria. This is the only published arithmetic threshold.
   [src: ielts.org — Writing band descriptors PDF, p.9]
3. At **band 3 only**, under-length is named as a cause: Lexical Resource may be
   inadequate "due to the response being significantly underlength", and under
   GRA, "Length may be insufficient to provide evidence of control of sentence
   forms" (bolded = limiting).
   [src: ielts.org — Writing band descriptors PDF, p.9]

**Structural finding — check this yourself.** I extracted the bold runs from the
Task 2 descriptor pages by font name (`OpenSans-Bold` vs `OpenSans-Regular`).
The complete list of bolded (rating-limiting) negative features in the Task 2
grid is:

| Band | Criterion | Bolded negative feature |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | TR | main parts "incompletely addressed" |
| 5 | CC | paragraphing "inadequate or missing" |
| 4 | TR | format may be inappropriate |
| 4 | LR | resource "unrelated to the task" |
| 4 | GRA | subordinate clauses rare, simple sentences predominate |
| 3 | GRA | length insufficient to show control of sentence forms |
| 2 | CC | entire response may be off-topic |
| 1 | all four | 20 words or fewer |
| 0 | — | proof the answer was totally memorised |

**There is not a single bolded negative feature at bands 6, 7, 8 or 9.**
[src: ielts.org — Writing band descriptors PDF, pp.7–9, bold extracted by font]

So the correct model is: **under-length has no tariff. It has a mechanism.**
Writing 190 words means you cannot have extended and supported two main ideas.
The examiner marks the missing development, not the missing words. This is
exactly what the official examiner comment does on the band-5.5 CD script:

> "A clear position is presented from the outset, supported by relevant ideas.
> These would require further development to achieve a higher score. It is also
> useful to note, this response does not meet the minimum word count for Task 2
> of 250 words, **so there is room for further development.**"
> [src: ielts.org — CD sample responses PDF, p.4]

By my own count that script is **≈198 words**. Read the examiner's logic
carefully: under-length is framed as *headroom the candidate didn't use*, not as
a deduction. The rating was still 5.5, not a forced 5.

**What to tell the reader.** Do not chase 250. Chase 270–290 (§9), because at 250
exactly, a paraphrase-shy introduction plus one copied clause can silently put
you under, and because two properly developed ideas do not fit in 250.

### Length above the minimum

Official evidence that long is not penalised: the official **Band 7.5** Task 2
exemplar in the CD sample pack is **≈375 words** by my count.
[src: ielts.org — CD sample responses PDF, p.5]

British Council's own practice page: aim for about 250 and "don't be afraid to go
a little over" [src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Academic Writing practice test Task 2].
There is no upper limit anywhere in Tier 1. The cost of a long essay is time and
error density (GRA), not length. See MYTHS #6.

### Memorised language — official position

| Where | What it says |
|---|---|
| Key assessment criteria | Responses penalised if "partly or wholly plagiarised" [src: ielts.org — Key assessment criteria PDF, p.1] |
| Band **4** LR | inappropriate use of lexical chunks — memorised phrases, formulaic language, and/or language taken from the input material [src: descriptors PDF p.9] |
| Band **3** LR | possible over-dependence on input material or memorised language [src: descriptors PDF p.9] |
| Band **3** GRA | errors predominate *except in memorised phrases or those taken from the input material* [src: descriptors PDF p.9] |
| Band **2** LR | few recognisable strings apart from memorised phrases [src: descriptors PDF p.9] |
| Band **0** | used "where there is proof that a candidate's answer has been totally memorised" [src: descriptors PDF p.9] |
| British Council | "examiners can spot 'model answers'" and such an answer is deemed invalid [src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test day advice] |

**The decisive observation:** in the official grid, memorised language is named at
bands 4, 3, 2 and 0 — and **nowhere above band 4**. It is not a neutral shortcut
that fails to earn credit; it is listed as *diagnostic of a low band*. Memorised
chunks are also explicitly discounted for GRA at band 3 (they are not treated as
your grammar). A template opener therefore cannot help LR and can actively drag
the LR judgement toward the band-4 description. This is the strongest
descriptor-derived argument in the chapter and it should be stated plainly.

### Question types — the honest position

**IELTS does not publish an official taxonomy of Task 2 essay types.** I checked
ielts.org's format pages, the sample tasks PDF, the key assessment criteria and
the band descriptors: none of them names essay families. What ielts.org says is
generic — the prompt "typically concludes with one or two questions requiring you
to either agree/disagree or discuss multiple perspectives"
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources].

The two official owners' *teaching* pages do publish lists, and they don't match:

- **British Council (5):** opinion · discussion · problem/solution ·
  advantages/disadvantages · two-part question
  [src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — How to write an English essay for IELTS]
- **IDP (6):** opinion · discussion · advantage/disadvantage · positive/negative ·
  cause/solution · multi-part
  [src: ielts.idp.com — How to understand Task 2 writing questions]

So the "five families" in `knowledge/tricks.md` §4 are **a recognition heuristic,
not an examined category** — and the book must say so. The families are still
worth teaching, because they are reliable *shapes*, but the underlying rule that
actually earns the marks is the Tier 1 one: **count the instructions in the
prompt and answer every one of them.** A candidate who memorises five templates
and meets a hybrid prompt will misfire; a candidate who counts question parts
never will.

The two official 2023 sample tasks, for calibration:
- 2A: a statement about children raised without much money + *"To what extent do
  you agree or disagree with this opinion?"* → opinion family.
- 2B: a two-sentence framing of international tourism + *"Do the disadvantages of
  international tourism outweigh the advantages?"* → advantages/disadvantages,
  weighed.
Both end with the fixed instruction *"Give reasons for your answer and include
any relevant examples from your own knowledge or experience"* and *"Write at
least 250 words."* [src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF, pp.6–7]

## The five question families

**Read this first.** The family is decided by the *final instruction line*, not by
the topic and not by the statement. Underline the last sentence that contains a
question mark or an imperative. Count the separate demands in it. That count is
the number of things your essay must visibly deliver.

A three-column triage that takes 30 seconds:

| I see… | Demands | Family |
|---|---|---|
| *To what extent do you agree or disagree?* / *Do you agree?* | 1 (your position) | 1. Opinion |
| *Discuss both views and give your own opinion.* | 3 (view A, view B, your view) | 2. Discussion |
| *What are the causes? What can be done?* / *problems … solutions* | 2 (diagnose, prescribe) | 3. Problem–cause–solution |
| *Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?* | 2 (both sides + a verdict) | 4. Advantages–disadvantages |
| Two questions of different shapes, e.g. *Why…? Is this positive or negative?* | 2 (one each) | 5. Two-part / direct question |

If two of these appear at once, you have a hybrid; obey the demand count, not the
family name.

---

### Family 1 — Opinion (agree / disagree)

**Recognise it by:** *To what extent do you agree or disagree?* · *Do you agree
or disagree?* · *What is your opinion?* The prompt gives you a proposition and
asks you to judge it. Only one demand.

**Structure that works (Tier 2 consensus, descriptor-safe):**

```
Intro   (~45 w)  Paraphrase the proposition · state your position in one clear sentence
Body 1  (~110 w) Reason 1 — topic sentence, explain, example, consequence
Body 2  (~110 w) Reason 2 — topic sentence, explain, example, consequence
Conc.   (~35 w)  Restate the position in new words; no new argument
```

A partial-agreement position is legitimate and often *stronger*, but it must be
stated as a rule, not as fence-sitting: *"This is true of vocational subjects but
not of the humanities"* is a position. *"There are arguments on both sides"* is
not.

**The trap.** *To what extent* invites candidates to write "I partly agree" and
then produce one paragraph for and one against with no adjudication. The
descriptor for band 6 flags exactly this outcome — conclusions that are "unclear,
unjustified or repetitive". [src: ielts.org — descriptors PDF, p.8]

**Band-6 failure mode.** The position is present in the introduction and again in
the conclusion, but the two body paragraphs are neutral surveys that would fit
either side. TR band 6 explicitly covers this: position relevant, but conclusions
unclear/unjustified. Fix: each body topic sentence must contain the word or idea
that carries your stance ("The stronger reason for rejecting this claim is…").

---

### Family 2 — Discussion (both views + your own opinion)

**Recognise it by:** *Discuss both views and give your own opinion.* Three
demands, and the third is the one people drop.

**Structure:**

```
Intro   (~50 w)  Paraphrase both views · state YOUR position now, not at the end
Body 1  (~100 w) View A — presented fairly, developed
Body 2  (~120 w) View B — developed, and this is where you side with one, with reasons
Conc.   (~35 w)  Your position restated
```

Alternative that is safer for a 6.5-band writer: three bodies (A / B / mine, ~75 w
each). It costs cohesion polish but makes the third demand impossible to miss.

**The trap.** Reporting the two views like a journalist. "Some people say X.
Other people say Y." Reported opinion is not developed opinion, and the third
demand ends up as a single final clause.

**Band-6 failure mode.** Both views covered well; opinion tacked on in seven
words at the end. Under the descriptors this is "the main parts of the prompt are
addressed (though some may be more fully covered than others)" — the literal
definition of band 6 TR. And ielts.org's own guidance is blunt: if you don't
discuss both, you're limited to band 5; the implication for the third demand is
the same in kind. [src: ielts.org — descriptors PDF p.8; ielts.org — Writing test resources]

**Fix that costs nothing:** put your opinion in the introduction's last sentence
*and* make Body 2's topic sentence agree with it. Now the position is visible in
three places and the "clear and developed position" test at band 7 is met.

---

### Family 3 — Problem / cause and solution

**Recognise it by:** *What are the causes of this problem and what measures could
be taken to address it?* · *Why is this happening and what can be done?* Two
demands, and they are *coupled*.

**Structure:**

```
Intro   (~45 w)  Paraphrase the problem · signpost: causes then remedies
Body 1  (~105 w) Cause 1 (fully) + Cause 2 (briefly), or one cause developed deeply
Body 2  (~110 w) Remedy addressing cause 1, remedy addressing cause 2 — named as such
Conc.   (~35 w)  The problem is tractable if the named causes are attacked
```

**The trap.** Mismatched halves. Causes: rural poverty and weak enforcement.
Solutions: "governments should raise awareness and people should be more
responsible." Nothing in the solution paragraph attacks anything in the cause
paragraph.

**Why it costs two criteria, not one.** TR loses "how relevant the candidate's
ideas are to the task"; CC loses coherence because "information and ideas are
logically organised" fails at the level of the whole essay, not the sentence.
[src: ielts.org — Key assessment criteria PDF, pp.3]

**Fix.** Write the solution paragraph with explicit back-reference — *"The first
of these causes is the easiest to attack, because…"* This also does free work for
CC: referencing and substitution are named in the band-7 CC descriptor, and
"these causes" is exactly that.

**Band-6 failure mode.** Vague, unimplementable remedies ("the government should
take action", "people should change their mentality"). Under band 6 LR these read
as "a lack of precision in word choice"; under band 6 TR they are "supporting
arguments and evidence… less relevant or inadequate". Name the actor, the
instrument and the mechanism: *who* does *what*, and *why that changes the cause*.

---

### Family 4 — Advantages and disadvantages

**Recognise it by:** two sub-shapes that need different essays.

| Wording | What it demands |
|---|---|
| *What are the advantages and disadvantages?* | Describe both. No verdict required (a verdict is still safe). |
| *Do the advantages **outweigh** the disadvantages?* | Both sides **and an explicit judgement of weight.** |

This is the family in the official 2023 sample task 2B: *"Do the disadvantages of
international tourism outweigh the advantages?"*
[src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF, p.7]

**Structure for the *outweigh* version:**

```
Intro   (~50 w)  Paraphrase · state the verdict now ("the benefits are the greater")
Body 1  (~95 w)  The weaker side, honestly presented
Body 2  (~120 w) The stronger side, developed further — and say WHY it weighs more
Conc.   (~35 w)  Verdict restated with the reason for the weighting
```

Give the winning side more words. That is itself an argument; a symmetrical essay
reads as a draw.

**The trap.** Answering the *outweigh* question as if it were the *what are*
question — three advantages, three disadvantages, "in conclusion there are both
advantages and disadvantages". That is a missing demand, and by ielts.org's own
statement, missing a demand caps you at band 5 for TR.

**Band-6 failure mode.** The verdict exists but is asserted, never weighed:
"Overall, I think the advantages are more important." Weighing means giving a
criterion — *more people affected*, *longer-lasting*, *reversible vs
irreversible*, *falls on those least able to bear it*. One clause of criterion
turns a band-6 verdict into a band-7 one.

---

### Family 5 — Two-part / direct question

**Recognise it by:** two question sentences of *different shapes*, e.g. *"Why do
you think this is happening? Is this a positive or negative development?"* The
"positive or negative development" prompt lives here when it is paired, and
behaves like Family 1 when it stands alone.

**Structure:**

```
Intro   (~45 w)  Paraphrase · one-line answer to BOTH questions
Body 1  (~105 w) Question 1, fully
Body 2  (~110 w) Question 2, fully — and it must be an evaluation, not more causes
Conc.   (~35 w)  Both answers, compressed
```

**The trap.** Answering question 1 twice. Candidates find causes easy and
evaluation hard, so Body 2 quietly becomes another cause paragraph.

**Band-6 failure mode.** Unequal coverage — the exact wording of band 6 TR,
"though some may be more fully covered than others". If Body 2 shrinks to three
sentences you are a band 6 on TR no matter how good the English is; if it
disappears you are looking at band 5.

**Test before you write the conclusion:** point at the sentence that answers
question 2. If you can't point at one, you haven't written it.

---

### Cross-family rule (the one that survives every prompt)

Every official Task 2 rubric ends with the same two instructions: *"Give reasons
for your answer and include any relevant examples from your own knowledge or
experience"* and *"Write at least 250 words."*
[src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF, pp.6–7]

So there are always **at least two more demands than the family implies**:
reasons, and examples. A response with a clear position, two developed reasons
and two concrete examples satisfies the rubric even if you misjudged the family.

## Band descriptor decoder

All wording below is my paraphrase of the May 2023 Task 2 descriptors
[src: ielts.org — Writing band descriptors PDF, pp.7–8], except for the single
short quoted phrase per criterion marked in **bold quotes**. Do not reproduce the
official grid in the book.

### Task Response

| Band | What the descriptor actually requires |
|---|---|
| **8** | Prompt addressed appropriately *and sufficiently*. A clear, well-developed position. Ideas relevant, well extended, supported. Only occasional omissions or lapses in content. |
| **7** | **The main parts** of the prompt are addressed appropriately. **"A clear and developed position"** is presented. Main ideas are extended and supported — but there may be a tendency to over-generalise, or the supporting material may lack focus and precision. |
| **6** | The main parts are addressed, but some more fully than others. An appropriate format is used. The position is relevant to the prompt, yet the conclusions drawn may be unclear, unjustified or repetitive. Main ideas are relevant but some are underdeveloped or unclear; some support is weak or less relevant. |
| **5** | *(bolded/limiting)* The main parts are **incompletely** addressed. Position expressed but development not always clear. Ideas limited, insufficiently developed, or padded with irrelevant detail. Some repetition. |

**Correction to prior work — the "throughout" question.** The 2013 edition of the
descriptors said band 7 TR "presents a clear position **throughout** the
response". The **2023 edition does not.** Band 7 now reads simply "a clear and
developed position is presented"; the word *throughout* survives at band 9 (a
position that "directly answers the question/s") and, crucially, in **Coherence &
Cohesion band 7**, where progression must be clear throughout the response.
[src: ielts.org — Writing band descriptors PDF, pp.7–8]

So the coaching line "hold your position throughout" is still right, but its
descriptor home has moved. In 2023 terms:
- *TR* wants the position **clear and developed** — visible and reasoned.
- *CC* wants **progression throughout** — the reader can trace the argument from
  paragraph to paragraph without backtracking.

The practical instruction is unchanged and is confirmed by an official examiner
comment on a Band 7.5 script: *"The test taker addresses both aspects of the task
and presents a clear position throughout the response."*
[src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF, p.26]

**6 → 7 on TR, in one line:** at 6, ideas are *stated*; at 7, ideas are
*extended and supported*.

**Before / after — an asserted idea becoming a developed one**

> **Band 6 (asserted):** Online education is convenient for students. It saves
> time and money. Therefore many students prefer it.
>
> **Band 7 (extended + supported):** Online education removes the daily commute,
> which for a student travelling into Tunis from a satellite town can absorb two
> hours a day. That time is not merely saved; it is redirected into study or paid
> work, so the same course yields more actual learning per week. The gain is
> largest precisely for the students who most need it — those combining a degree
> with a job.

What changed, mapped to the descriptor: a *mechanism* was given (commute →
reclaimed hours), an *instance* was given (Tunis satellite town), and a
*consequence* was drawn (more learning per week for working students). Three
moves, one paragraph. Note the last sentence also hedges the generalisation,
which pre-empts band 7's named weakness ("a tendency to over-generalise").

**Before / after — a conclusion that is "unclear, unjustified or repetitive"
becoming one that isn't**

> **Band 6:** In conclusion, there are advantages and disadvantages of tourism,
> and governments should think carefully about it.
>
> **Band 7:** In conclusion, tourism's economic gains are real but narrowly
> distributed, while its environmental costs fall on everyone and are slow to
> reverse. That asymmetry is why, on balance, the drawbacks are the weightier.

The second one *justifies* — it names the criterion (distribution, reversibility)
on which the verdict rests.

### Coherence & Cohesion

| Band | What the descriptor actually requires |
|---|---|
| **8** | Message followed with ease; ideas logically sequenced; cohesion well managed; occasional lapses; paragraphing sufficient and appropriate. |
| **7** | Information and ideas logically organised, with clear progression throughout (a few minor lapses allowed). A range of cohesive devices *including reference and substitution* used flexibly but with some inaccuracies or **"some over/under use"**. Paragraphing generally effective, and sequencing *within* a paragraph generally logical. |
| **6** | Generally coherent with clear overall progression. Cohesive devices work to some good effect, but cohesion within and/or between sentences may be faulty or **mechanical** through misuse, overuse or omission. Reference and substitution may lack flexibility or clarity, producing repetition or error. Paragraphing may not always be logical and the central topic may not always be clear. |
| **5** | Organisation visible but not wholly logical; weak overall progression. Sentences not fluently linked. Limited or overused devices with inaccuracy. *(bolded/limiting)* Paragraphing may be **inadequate or missing**. |

**This is the criterion most damaged by "good" advice.** Over-use is named at
band 7 and again at band 6. Nothing in the grid rewards quantity of linkers.
Officially, cohesive devices include three families — logical connectors,
conjunctions, and **pronouns/reference** — and reference and substitution are the
ones band 7 asks for by name.
[src: ielts.org — Key assessment criteria PDF, p.3; descriptors PDF p.7]

IDP's own teaching page says the same in plain words: don't overuse conjunctions
and don't put them "repeatedly in the same location within the sentence"; use
pronouns and substitution words (*this*, *one*, *so*) to cut repetition.
[src: ielts.idp.com — IELTS writing prep: coherence and cohesion]

**What over-use looks like — the official example.** The examiner on the Band 7.5
CD script wrote: *"Cohesive devices are generally used flexibly, but there is some
overuse of sequencers in paragraph 2 [Firstly | So | Also | Yet]."*
[src: ielts.org — CD sample responses PDF, p.5]

Four sequencers in one paragraph was enough for an examiner to write it down. The
paragraph in question is otherwise strong. That is the calibration: **more than
about two discourse markers per paragraph is visible.**

**Before / after — mechanical cohesion becoming invisible cohesion**

> **Band 6 (mechanical — five markers, four sentences):**
> Firstly, cities are becoming overcrowded. Moreover, housing is expensive.
> Furthermore, transport systems cannot cope. In addition, air quality is poor.
> Therefore, governments should act.
>
> **Band 7 (reference + substitution doing the work):**
> Cities are absorbing people faster than they can house them. That mismatch
> shows up first in rents, then in transport systems that were designed for a
> smaller population, and finally in the air itself. **None of these pressures**
> eases on its own, which is why they end up on the government's desk.

Devices used in the rewrite, all of which the descriptor names: *that mismatch*
(reference to the previous clause), *these pressures* (substitution + reference),
*first / then / finally* inside one sentence rather than at the head of four, and
a relative clause carrying the causal link instead of *therefore*.

**Paragraphing.** For Task 2, paragraphing is expected at band 6 and above
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources]. Band 5 CC has "paragraphing may be
inadequate or missing" in **bold** — a limiting feature. An unparagraphed Task 2,
however good, is a band-5 CC. On computer-delivered IELTS, leave a blank line
between paragraphs so the break is unmissable.

**6 → 7 on CC, in one line:** at 6 the joins are visible; at 7 the reader stops
noticing them and simply follows the argument.

### Lexical Resource

| Band | What the descriptor actually requires |
|---|---|
| **8** | Wide resource used fluently and flexibly for precise meaning; skilful use of uncommon/idiomatic items despite occasional inaccuracy in choice and collocation; occasional spelling/word-formation errors with minimal impact. |
| **7** | Resource sufficient for some flexibility and precision. Some ability with less common and/or idiomatic items. **"An awareness of style and collocation"** is evident, though inappropriacies occur. Only a few spelling or word-formation errors, and they do not detract from overall clarity. |
| **6** | Generally adequate and appropriate. Meaning generally clear despite a rather restricted range or a lack of precision in word choice. A risk-taker will show wider vocabulary but with higher inaccuracy. Some spelling/word-formation errors, but they don't impede communication. |
| **5** | Limited but minimally adequate. Simple vocabulary may be accurate, but range doesn't permit variation. Frequent lapses in appropriacy; frequent simplification and repetition; noticeable spelling/word-formation errors causing the reader difficulty. |

**The band-6 clause everyone should read twice:** *"If the writer is a risk-taker,
there will be a wider range of vocabulary used but higher degrees of inaccuracy
or inappropriacy."* The descriptor explicitly anticipates the thesaurus strategy
and places it **at band 6**. Reaching for rare words without collocation control
is a band-6 behaviour by definition. [src: ielts.org — descriptors PDF, p.8]

**Before / after — rarity vs precision**

> **Band 6 (risk-taker):** Governments must ameliorate the pecuniary predicament
> of impecunious households.
> **Band 7 (precise):** Governments must relieve the financial pressure on
> low-income households.

*Ameliorate* is a real word; *ameliorate a predicament* is not a collocation.
*Relieve pressure on households* is. The descriptor rewards the second.

> **Band 6 (imprecise):** Pollution is a big problem that gives bad effects to
> the environment.
> **Band 7 (precise + collocated):** Industrial emissions place sustained strain
> on air quality, particularly in cities that grew before emissions rules
> existed.

Changes: *big problem* → a named mechanism; *gives bad effects to* → *place strain
on* (correct collocation); *the environment* → *air quality* (specific).

**Topic vocabulary beats general vocabulary.** LR assesses "the adequacy and
appropriacy of the vocabulary (e.g. topic-specific items…)"
[src: ielts.org — Key assessment criteria PDF, p.4]. Ten precise
education words (*curriculum, streaming, tuition fees, attainment gap, vocational
training, drop-out rate, teacher retention, rote learning, contact hours,
lifelong learning*) do more for LR than twenty synonyms for *important*.

**6 → 7 on LR, in one line:** at 6 the word is understandable; at 7 it is the
right word and it keeps the company it normally keeps.

### Grammatical Range & Accuracy

| Band | What the descriptor actually requires |
|---|---|
| **8** | A wide range of structures used flexibly and accurately. The majority of sentences are error-free; punctuation well managed. Occasional, non-systematic errors with minimal impact. |
| **7** | A variety of complex structures used with some flexibility and accuracy. Grammar and punctuation generally well controlled and **"error-free sentences are frequent"**. A few errors may persist but do not impede communication. |
| **6** | A mix of simple and complex sentence forms, but limited flexibility. The complex structures are not as accurate as the simple ones. Errors in grammar and punctuation occur but rarely impede communication. |
| **5** | Limited, repetitive range. Complex sentences attempted but tend to be faulty; greatest accuracy on simple sentences. Errors may be frequent and cause the reader difficulty. Punctuation may be faulty. |

**The measurable test.** Band 7 needs error-free sentences to be *frequent*; band
8 needs them to be *the majority*. So count. In a 280-word essay of ~16 sentences,
if fewer than about 7–8 are completely clean, you are arguing for a 6.

**What "complex" officially means at band 7+:** passive constructions, modal
verbs, comparative forms, and complex noun phrases — not just subordinate clauses.
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources]

**Systematic vs non-systematic matters at band 8**: a repeated article error
across the whole script is systematic and blocks band 8; three unrelated slips
are non-systematic. [src: ielts.org — Writing test resources] For an Arabic/French
L1 writer this is decisive — article errors are the classic *systematic* error.

**Before / after — band-6 complexity becoming band-7 complexity**

> **Band 6 (complex but faulty, comma-spliced):** Although many people believes
> that technology improve the education, but the students in rural areas they
> cannot access to internet, this is the real problem.
>
> **Band 7 (same idea, controlled):** Although many people believe that technology
> improves education, students in rural areas often cannot access the internet,
> and that gap is the real problem.

Repairs: subject–verb agreement (*people believe*), no *although…but* doubling,
no resumptive pronoun (*students… they*), *access* takes no preposition, comma
splice replaced by a coordinator, article dropped from *education* used
generally.

> **Band 7 range-builder (same content, four structures in two sentences):**
> Where broadband has been rolled out, attainment has risen; where it has not,
> the gap has widened. Unless connectivity is treated as infrastructure rather
> than a consumer product, the technology that was meant to level the field will
> continue to tilt it.

Structures on display: fronted adverbial clauses in parallel, semicolon,
*unless* + present perfect passive, comparative *rather than*, a relative clause,
and a purposeful contrast. That is "a variety of complex structures used with
some flexibility".

**Punctuation is inside GRA.** The band-7.5 official script was told "punctuation
is unhelpful at times" [src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF,
p.26]. Comma splices are the single punctuation error this reader is most likely
to make (see L1 alert).

## Evidence from official examiner comments

Six officially awarded Task 2 scripts with published examiner commentary. These
are the best calibration data available anywhere, and they contradict several
popular beliefs.

### Band 4 — Sample Task 2A, Script A

> Position "difficult to identify"; ideas limited and, though supported with
> examples from experience, they "remain unclear"; no overall progression;
> cohesive devices present but assisting "only minimally"; **"Language from the
> input material is used inappropriately"**; attempts at subordination "rare and
> tend to be very inaccurate".
> [src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF, p.18]

**What capped it:** an unlocatable position plus reliance on prompt language.
Note the second point — using the input material's wording is named as a *fault*,
matching band-4 LR in the grid.

### Band 5.5 — Sample Task 2B, Script A

> **"The first five lines of this response are directly copied rubric; no credit
> is given for copied rubric."** Topic addressed and position relevant, but
> patches where development is unclear; ideas sometimes insufficiently developed;
> "some mechanical use of linkers in places"; paragraphs "sometimes rather too
> short and inappropriate"; vocabulary range adequate **but** frequent spelling
> errors keep LR down; mix of simple and complex structures with variable
> control. *"Although there are some features of a higher band in this response,
> flaws in the paragraphing and the errors in vocabulary limit this rating to
> Band 5.5."*
> [src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF, p.24]

**What capped it:** paragraphing + spelling. Not ideas, not range. Two mechanical
things a 6-week study plan can fix outright.

### Band 5.5 — CD sample, Part 2 Response 1 (≈198 words)

> Clear position from the outset, supported by relevant ideas, but they "would
> require further development"; the response "does not meet the minimum word
> count for Task 2 of 250 words, so there is room for further development";
> coherent arrangement and clear progression, but "paragraphing is not always
> logical"; lack of precision in word choice; spelling and word-formation errors;
> **"There also appears to be some interference from the test taker's first
> language, e.g. 'alimentation'"**; mix of sentence forms "but the level of error
> is too high to achieve a higher band score."
> [src: ielts.org — CD sample responses PDF, p.4]

**What capped it:** under-development (caused by under-length) and error density.
**Read this twice if you are French-influenced:** an official examiner named L1
interference in the published commentary, and the example they chose —
*alimentation* for *diet/nutrition* — is a French false friend. This is direct
Tier 1 evidence that the L1 chapter of this book is not paranoia.

### Band 6.5 — Sample Task 2A, Script B

> Arguments "generally well developed", ideas appropriate, clear position. **"It
> is a shame that the first paragraph, and beginning of the second are mainly
> copied from the rubric."** Better paragraphing "would have allowed a clearer
> focus… and prevented the lapse into generalisation towards the end".
> Referencing usually accurate and effective, but "better use of linkers would
> have improved the cohesion". Vocabulary varied and flexible, choice not always
> precise; **"The repetition of language from the rubric, while integrated,
> reveals a lack of ability to paraphrase."** Regular errors detract from the
> range of structures. *"the weaknesses in organisation and grammatical control
> limit the rating to Band 6.5."*
> [src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF, p.20]

**What capped it — and this is the most useful single data point in the dossier
for a 6.5 candidate aiming at 7:** organisation (paragraphing) and grammatical
control. The *ideas were fine*. Copying the rubric was penalised twice over — once
as lost credit, once as evidence against LR.

Also note: this is a case of **under-use** of linkers, not over-use. The band-7 CC
descriptor's "over/**under** use" is not decoration.

### Band 7.5 — CD sample, Part 2 Response 2 (≈375 words)

> "A strong response. A clear position is presented at the outset and some ideas
> are explored to support it. An alternative position is also considered but
> rejected." Ideas logically organised with clear progression. **"Cohesive
> devices are generally used flexibly, but there is some overuse of sequencers in
> paragraph 2 [Firstly | So | Also | Yet]."** Paragraphing generally effective but
> paragraphs 2 and 3 should have been broken down. Wide vocabulary with good use
> of less common items and **higher-level "softening"** — the examiner names
> *They tend to*, *This appears to be*, *might disagree*. Spelling and
> word-formation errors rare. Variety of complex structures with frequent
> error-free sentences, though some errors occur and "some overuse of rather
> short sentence forms".
> [src: ielts.org — CD sample responses PDF, p.5]

**Four findings from this script that the book must carry:**

1. **Hedging is explicitly credited.** The examiner singled out *tend to*,
   *appears to be*, *might* as evidence of higher-level lexis. This is the
   cheapest LR upgrade available and it simultaneously defuses band 7 TR's named
   weakness (over-generalising).
2. **Contractions did not stop 7.5.** The script uses *don't* three times.
   Register purism is over-sold; see MYTHS #8.
3. **"In my opinion, I think that…"** appears in the opening paragraph — a
   redundancy this reader is prone to — and the script still scored 7.5. Errors
   are weighed by *density and effect*, not by presence.
4. **≈375 words did not attract a comment.** Length was never mentioned.

Residual errors that survived at 7.5 (my own reading of the script text):
*"This prevents people to go outside"* (→ *from going*), *"technology is
developing everyday"* (→ *every day*), *"reading gossips"* (→ *gossip*,
uncountable), *"celebraties"*, *"many phone industries"* (→ *manufacturers*).
Useful for calibrating how forgiving band 7.5 actually is.

### Band 8.5 — Sample Task 2A, Script C

> "The topic is very well addressed and explored in depth. The position is clear
> throughout and directly answers the question." Ideas relevant and very well
> supported, "apart from some over-generalisation in the penultimate paragraph".
> "The answer can be read with ease due to the sophisticated handling of cohesive
> devices, with only minimal lapses (for example, the use of 'e.g.')." Wide,
> natural vocabulary with full flexibility; "many examples of appropriate
> modification, collocation and precise vocabulary choice." Syntax varied and
> sophisticated; only occasional errors.
> [src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF, p.22]

**What was still criticised at 8.5:** over-generalisation, and writing *e.g.* in
running prose. At the top of the scale the examiner is docking abbreviations. The
lesson for a 7-target: write *for example*, not *e.g.*; write *and so on*, not
*etc.*

### Pattern across all six

| Script | Band | Named limiting factors |
|---|---|---|
| 2A-A | 4 | position unfindable · input language misused · inaccurate subordination |
| 2B-A | 5.5 | copied rubric · paragraphing · spelling |
| CD-1 | 5.5 | under-length → under-development · error density · L1 interference |
| 2A-B | 6.5 | **paragraphing/organisation · grammatical control** (ideas were fine) |
| 2B-B | 7.5 | occasional content lapses · under-use of connectives · punctuation |
| CD-2 | 7.5 | over-use of sequencers · paragraphs too long |
| 2A-C | 8.5 | over-generalisation · an abbreviation |

**Nobody in this set was capped for lacking impressive ideas.** They were capped
for organisation, control, copying, and length. That is where the reader's 90
minutes a day should go.

## Idea banks by topic

Purpose: end the "I have nothing to say" freeze that produces under-length
essays. Each bank gives **mechanisms** (not opinions), because a mechanism is
what "extension" means in the descriptor. Deploy one mechanism per body
paragraph, then support it with a concrete instance — Tunisia/Malta/Europe
examples are explicitly permitted [src: ielts.org — Key assessment criteria PDF, p.2].

**No invented statistics.** Nothing here contains a number, deliberately. A
fabricated figure adds no marks (the criteria never mention data) and risks
looking absurd. "A significant minority", "in many low-income neighbourhoods",
"in my own city" are safe and score identically.

### 1. Education

| For | Against / cost |
|---|---|
| Education raises *individual* earning capacity **and** the productivity of everyone around the graduate (spillover) | Public funding of higher education transfers money from non-graduate taxpayers to future high earners |
| Free access widens the talent pool: ability is distributed evenly across income, opportunity is not | Fees signal seriousness and fund quality — contact hours, laboratories, staff retention |
| Vocational training matches skills to actual vacancies faster than academic degrees | Narrow vocational training ages badly when the occupation changes |
| Streaming by ability lets teaching target the right level | Streaming entrenches early disadvantage; a child's stream at eleven predicts their life at forty |

**Topic lexis:** curriculum · attainment gap · rote learning · critical thinking ·
tuition fees · means-tested · vocational · apprenticeship · drop-out rate ·
teacher retention · contact hours · lifelong learning · state-funded ·
higher education.

**Ready mechanisms:** *opportunity cost* (a student who works at 18 is not idle,
they are supporting a family) · *signalling* (a degree may prove capability
rather than create it) · *intergenerational transmission* (educated parents
produce educated children, so gaps compound).

### 2. Environment

| For action | Against / complication |
|---|---|
| Environmental costs are *externalities* — the polluter doesn't pay, so the market alone won't fix it | Regulation raises costs for firms competing with unregulated foreign producers |
| Damage is often irreversible on a human timescale; the asymmetry justifies caution | Poor countries are asked to forgo the growth path rich countries already used |
| Individual behaviour follows infrastructure: people recycle when collection exists | Individual action is a rounding error next to industrial and agricultural emissions |
| Carbon pricing changes behaviour without dictating it | Flat energy taxes fall hardest on low-income households |

**Topic lexis:** emissions · carbon footprint · renewable · fossil fuels ·
biodiversity · deforestation · landfill · single-use · sustainable ·
conservation · water scarcity · desertification · public transport network ·
environmental degradation.

**Ready mechanisms:** *externality* · *irreversibility* · *free-rider problem*
(everyone benefits from restraint, nobody wants to pay for it) · *infrastructure
before virtue*.

**Local example the reader owns:** water scarcity and desertification in North
Africa; coastal development pressure in Mediterranean tourist towns.

### 3. Technology

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Removes distance as a constraint on work, study and healthcare | Access is unequal, so it widens the gap between connected and unconnected |
| Automates dangerous and repetitive labour | Automation displaces workers whose skills don't transfer, and retraining is slow |
| Lowers the cost of information to near zero | Attention, not information, is now the scarce good; platforms optimise for engagement, not truth |
| Enables remote work, easing housing pressure on capital cities | Blurs work and home; erodes the boundary that protected rest |

**Topic lexis:** digital divide · automation · algorithm · surveillance · data
privacy · screen time · misinformation · remote working · digital literacy ·
platform · obsolescence · connectivity.

**Ready mechanisms:** *substitution vs complementarity* (does the machine replace
the worker or make them more productive?) · *network effects* · *displacement
lag* (jobs vanish quickly, new ones appear slowly).

### 4. Health

| For public intervention | Against |
|---|---|
| Ill health has costs the individual doesn't bear alone — lost productivity, hospital capacity | Personal autonomy: adults may make bad choices about their own bodies |
| Prevention is cheaper than treatment, and the saving is systemic | Preventive campaigns often reach the already-healthy; they widen the health gap |
| Environment shapes behaviour: cycle lanes create cyclists | "Nanny state" measures breed resentment and non-compliance |
| Food labelling and taxation change what is bought | Taxes on food are regressive |

**Topic lexis:** sedentary lifestyle · processed food · obesity · public health ·
preventive medicine · life expectancy · mental health · healthcare provision ·
GP / primary care · waiting lists · nutrition · chronic illness · wellbeing.

**Ready mechanisms:** *prevention vs cure* · *default choice* (people take the
easy option, so make the healthy option easy) · *social determinants* (income
predicts health more reliably than willpower).

**Note:** both official CD Task 2 exemplars are on this topic (whether health will
improve or worsen). Worth drilling.

### 5. Globalisation

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Specialisation raises output; countries do what they do best | Specialisation creates dependence and vulnerability to shocks |
| Contact between cultures reduces hostility built on ignorance | Cultural homogenisation: the same brands, films and food everywhere |
| Migration relieves labour shortages in ageing societies and remittances flow home | Brain drain strips the sending country of the professionals it trained |
| Global standards can raise labour and environmental floors | Production migrates to wherever standards are lowest |

**Topic lexis:** multinational · outsourcing · supply chain · tariff ·
remittances · brain drain · cultural identity · homogenisation · migration ·
labour market · trade liberalisation · economic interdependence.

**Ready mechanism the reader owns:** North African graduates emigrating to Europe
— the training is paid for by the sending state and the return accrues to the
receiving one.

### 6. Crime

| Deterrence / punishment | Prevention / rehabilitation |
|---|---|
| Certainty of being caught deters more than severity of sentence | Most offending correlates with unemployment, poor housing and school exclusion |
| Prison incapacitates, protecting the public during the sentence | Prison is an effective school for crime; reoffending rates show it |
| Victims and society have a legitimate claim to retribution | Rehabilitation is cheaper over a lifetime than repeated incarceration |
| Visible policing reassures communities | Over-policing damages trust and makes witnesses uncooperative |

**Topic lexis:** deterrent · custodial sentence · reoffending / recidivism ·
rehabilitation · community service · petty crime · white-collar crime ·
surveillance · miscarriage of justice · restorative justice · law enforcement.

**Ready mechanism:** *certainty beats severity* — this single idea answers almost
every crime prompt and is genuinely well-founded in criminology, so it never
sounds glib.

### 7. Work

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Shorter hours can raise output per hour; exhaustion is not productivity | Some work is inherently time-based; you cannot compress a nurse's shift |
| Remote work widens the hiring pool and relieves city housing pressure | Remote work weakens the informal mentoring that trains junior staff |
| Job security enables long-term planning, family formation, mortgages | Rigid protection can freeze the labour market against young entrants |
| Salary is a weak motivator above subsistence; autonomy and purpose matter more | For most people the wage is the point; "purpose" is a luxury framing |

**Topic lexis:** work–life balance · job satisfaction · burnout · flexible hours ·
gig economy · zero-hours contract · career progression · salary expectations ·
workforce · retirement age · productivity · staff turnover.

**Ready mechanism:** *hours vs output* — the assumption that time worked equals
value produced is exactly what a good essay questions.

### How to use a bank under exam pressure

Do **not** memorise these as sentences — that is memorised language and it is
named at band 4 (§Verified facts). Memorise the **mechanism words**:
externality · opportunity cost · irreversibility · displacement lag · certainty
beats severity · default choice · signalling · brain drain · free-rider. Nine
concepts, each of which generates an original paragraph on demand. That is the
difference between a template (penalised) and a thinking tool (invisible).

## Strategies found

Each is tagged with tier and the reason it survives the "how does this raise a
6.5–7.5 per the descriptors?" filter.

### S1 — Count the demands before you choose a structure (Tier 1)
Underline the final instruction line; count the separate demands; write one body
paragraph per demand as a minimum. Directly serves the Tier 1 rule that failing
to address all main parts limits you to band 5.
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources; ielts.idp.com — How to understand Task 2 writing questions]

### S2 — Paraphrase the prompt, never copy it (Tier 1)
Change the nouns and the verb; keep the proposition. Two Tier 1 sources say
copied rubric earns nothing, and one official examiner used it as evidence of
weak LR. Also protects the real word count (§Verified facts).
[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test day advice; ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF, pp.20, 24]

### S3 — Position in the introduction, echoed in every topic sentence (Tier 1 + descriptor-derived)
ielts.org shows an exemplar presenting position in the opening paragraph, and
band 7 TR needs a clear, developed position. Echoing it in topic sentences also
delivers the CC band-7 requirement for progression throughout.
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources; descriptors PDF p.7]

### S4 — The four-move body paragraph: claim → because → so what → instance (Tier 1-derived)
This *is* the official gloss on TR: extension = greater detail and depth; support
= relevant examples. The four moves make both visible.
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources; Key assessment criteria PDF, p.3]
Corroborated by Tier 2: bodies of roughly five sentences and ~10 minutes each
[src: ielts-simon (mirrored) — Writing Task 2]; "explanations and relevant
examples" [src: ieltsadvantage.com — Writing Task 2].

### S5 — Two developed ideas, not four asserted ones (Tier 2, descriptor-safe)
At 280 words, four ideas get 60 words each — assertion length. Two get 110 each —
development length. Band 6 TR names underdeveloped main ideas as the fault; band
7 needs extension and support. Consistent across IELTS Simon, IELTS Liz and IELTS
Advantage.
[src: ieltsliz.com — How many words for IELTS writing; ieltsadvantage.com — Writing Task 2]

### S6 — Replace half your connectors with reference and substitution (Tier 1)
Band 7 CC names reference and substitution specifically and penalises over-use of
devices. IDP's own page teaches pronoun reference and *this/one/so* substitution.
Highest-value CC move available to a band-6 writer.
[src: ielts.org — descriptors PDF p.7; ielts.idp.com — coherence and cohesion]

### S7 — Cap discourse markers at ~2 per paragraph (Tier 1-calibrated)
An official examiner logged four sequencers in one paragraph of a Band 7.5 script
as "some overuse". Two is demonstrably safe; four is visible.
[src: ielts.org — CD sample responses PDF, p.5]

### S8 — Hedge every generalisation (Tier 1-credited)
*tend to · appears to · is likely to · in most cases · broadly · often.* An
official examiner named exactly this as a *higher-level feature* worth crediting
under LR, and it neutralises band 7 TR's named weakness (over-generalising).
Double-scoring move.
[src: ielts.org — CD sample responses PDF, p.5; descriptors PDF p.7]

### S9 — Blank line between paragraphs; 4–5 paragraphs, never 1 (Tier 1)
Paragraphing is expected at band 6+; "inadequate or missing" is a bolded limiter
at band 5; two official scripts were docked for paragraphing (5.5 and 6.5) and
one at 7.5 was told to break long paragraphs down.
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources; descriptors PDF p.8; sample tasks PDF pp.20, 24; CD responses PDF p.5]

### S10 — Target 270–290 words; hard floor 260 (Tier 2 consensus, Tier 1-compatible)
Three independent Tier 2 sources land in the same zone: 270–290
[src: ieltsliz.com], 260–295 [src: ieltsfocus.com], 280–340
[src: blog.myieltsclassroom.com]. The floor is set above 250 because copied or
near-copied rubric is discounted and because two developed ideas do not fit in
250. No upper penalty exists — the official Band 7.5 exemplar is ≈375 words.

### S11 — 5 / 32 / 3 inside the 40 minutes (Tier 2)
5 min analyse + plan · 32 min write · 3 min check. Tier 2 recommendations range
from 5 to 10 minutes of planning [src: ieltsadvantage.com — Writing Task 2]; 5 is
the right choice for a candidate who writes at ~9 words/minute, since 32 minutes
at that rate yields ~290 words. Checking time is not optional — ielts.org's own
advice is to reserve time for proofreading.
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources]

### S12 — Proofread for *your* three errors, not for "mistakes" (descriptor-derived)
Band 8 is blocked by *systematic* errors. For this reader the systematic three are
articles with abstract nouns, pluralised uncountables, and comma splices (§L1
alert). Three targeted passes of one minute each beat one vague re-read.
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources; descriptors PDF p.7]

### S13 — Write Task 2 first *only if* you have proved you overrun (Tier 2, conditional)
Rationale: Task 2 is worth two thirds, so time stolen from it costs double.
Tier 2 opinion is genuinely split — some recommend Task 1 first as a low-effort
warm-up. Computer-delivered IELTS lets you start from either task
[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]. **Decide in practice,
not on the day**, and if you switch, always set a hard 40-minute stop so Task 1
still gets its 20. No Tier 1 source expresses a preference. `[UNVERIFIED]` as a
general recommendation; safe as a personalised one.

### S14 — Build topic lexis, not synonym lists (Tier 1-derived)
LR assesses topic-specific items and collocation control. Ten precise education
nouns outperform twenty synonyms for *important*, and carry no collocation risk.
[src: ielts.org — Key assessment criteria PDF, p.4]

### S15 — Concede once, then counter (descriptor-derived)
The Band 7.5 CD script was praised because "an alternative position is also
considered but rejected". Conceding demonstrates a developed position rather than
weakening it — and gives you a natural complex structure (*While X is true, …*).
[src: ielts.org — CD sample responses PDF, p.5]

## MYTHS

Score-lowering advice, with the descriptor mechanism that makes it backfire.

**M1 — "Open with a strong general statement about the modern era."**
*"In this contemporary era of rapid globalisation, it is an undeniable fact
that…"* This is memorised formulaic language, which the official grid names at
bands 4, 3 and 2 as a marker of low lexical resource, and which at band 3 is
explicitly **discounted** for GRA. It also costs you ~25 words of your 280 that
contain no response to the prompt, damaging TR. Nothing in the descriptors
rewards a "hook".
[src: ielts.org — descriptors PDF p.9]

**M2 — "Memorise a template essay / model answers for common topics."**
British Council states plainly that examiners can spot model answers and that
such a response is deemed invalid; band 0 is reserved for answers with proof of
total memorisation; plagiarised responses are penalised.
[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test day advice; ielts.org — descriptors PDF p.9; Key assessment criteria PDF p.1]

**M3 — "Start every sentence with a linker: Firstly, Moreover, Furthermore, In addition."**
Over-use is a named fault at band 7 CC *and* band 6 CC. An official examiner
docked a 7.5 script for four sequencers in one paragraph. Linker density is one
of the few things that can actively lower CC.
[src: ielts.org — descriptors PDF pp.7–8; CD sample responses PDF p.5]

**M4 — "Use the most advanced synonyms you can find."**
Band **6** LR describes precisely this behaviour: the risk-taker with a wider
range but "higher degrees of inaccuracy or inappropriacy". Band 7 asks for
awareness of *collocation*, i.e. the right word in its normal company. Thesaurus
substitution moves you toward 6, not 7.
[src: ielts.org — descriptors PDF pp.7–8]

**M5 — "Write 400+ words to show off your range."**
Not penalised as length, but every extra sentence is another chance to break the
band-7 GRA requirement that error-free sentences be frequent, and it eats the
proofreading time ielts.org tells you to reserve. It also risks the band-7 TR
weakness "a lack of focus and precision in supporting ideas".
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources; descriptors PDF p.7]

**M6 — "Never go over 300 words / the sweet spot has a hard ceiling."**
The reverse myth, and it is in the current `knowledge/tricks.md`. The official
**Band 7.5** exemplar is ≈375 words and length drew no comment; British Council's
practice page says don't be afraid to go a little over. Correct the book: there
is a floor, not a ceiling.
[src: ielts.org — CD sample responses PDF p.5; takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Academic Writing practice test Task 2]

**M7 — "Writing under 250 words costs you an automatic band 5 in Task Response."**
This was a real, *unpublished* examiner rule before ~2018 and is reported as
removed. No current Tier 1 document contains it. The current published arithmetic
is only ≤20 words → band 1. The correct model is causal, not tariff-based: too
short means undeveloped, and undeveloped is what the descriptor marks.
[src: ielts.org — descriptors PDF p.9; ieltsliz.com — Penalty for writing under word count (Tier 2, historical claim only)] `[UNVERIFIED as current rule]`

**M8 — "Contractions / any informality will cap you at band 6."**
Overstated. Register belongs to LR ("awareness of style"), and errors are weighed
by density and effect. The official Band 7.5 script uses *don't* three times and
writes *In my opinion, I think*. **Still avoid them** — they buy nothing and cost
a little — but do not spend exam minutes hunting them while a whole question part
sits unanswered.
[src: ielts.org — CD sample responses PDF p.5]

**M9 — "You need real statistics and named studies to 'support' your ideas."**
Officially, no specialist knowledge is required and examples may come from your
own experience. Invented statistics are not credited and can look absurd.
"Support" means a relevant instance, not data.
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources; Key assessment criteria PDF p.2]

**M10 — "Never use I / personal experience — it's not academic."**
Contradicted directly: the printed rubric on every official task invites examples
from your own knowledge or experience, and the key assessment criteria say the
same. A first-person position statement is exactly what TR asks for.
[src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF pp.6–7; Key assessment criteria PDF p.2]

**M11 — "Sit on the fence to sound balanced / academic."**
Band 6 TR names conclusions that are "unclear, unjustified or repetitive"; band 7
needs a clear, developed position. Balance without adjudication is a band-6
signature.
[src: ielts.org — descriptors PDF pp.7–8]

**M12 — "Learn the five essay types and you've learned Task 2."**
IELTS publishes no official essay-type taxonomy, and its two owners' teaching
pages give lists of five and six that don't match. Types are a recognition aid;
the examined requirement is to address every main part of *this* prompt. A
memorised type-template meeting a hybrid prompt is how candidates lose TR.
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources; takeielts.britishcouncil.org — How to write an English essay; ielts.idp.com — How to understand Task 2 writing questions]

**M13 — "Bullet points / sub-headings make the structure clearer for the examiner."**
Explicitly penalised. "It should not have sub-headings or bullet points", and
note form "in any part of the response" is penalisable.
[src: ielts.org — Writing test resources; Key assessment criteria PDF p.1]

**M14 — "Copy the question into your introduction to save time."**
The most expensive shortcut in the test: no credit, discounted from the word
count, and used by examiners as evidence of inability to paraphrase (LR). Two
official scripts were docked for it.
[src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF pp.20, 24; descriptors PDF p.9; takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Test day advice]

**M15 — "Abbreviations like e.g. and etc. are fine in an essay."**
An official examiner listed *e.g.* as a lapse in an **8.5** script. Cheap to fix:
*for example*, *and so on*.
[src: ielts.org — Academic Writing sample tasks 2023 PDF p.22]

**M16 — "Rhetorical questions make a strong introduction."**
No Tier 1 rule exists, so this is not a penalty claim — but the descriptor logic
is one-way. A question is not a position, so an introduction built on questions
delays TR's "establishes their position"; and questions are not cohesive devices,
so they do nothing for CC. Upside zero, downside real. Treat as *avoid*, not
*forbidden*. `[UNVERIFIED as an official rule]`
[src: ielts.org — Key assessment criteria PDF p.3, derived]

## L1 alert — Arabic and French interference in Task 2

Official evidence that this matters: an IELTS examiner's published comment on a
Task 2 script names it outright — *"There also appears to be some interference
from the test taker's first language, e.g. 'alimentation'"* — and the script was
held at 5.5 partly because "the level of error is too high".
[src: ielts.org — CD sample responses PDF, p.4]

Under the descriptors these errors bite in two places: **GRA**, where band 8
requires errors to be *non-systematic* (an L1 pattern is systematic by
definition), and **LR**, where word-formation and appropriacy errors are counted.

### 1. Articles with abstract nouns — the highest-frequency, highest-cost error

Task 2 is *made of* abstract nouns used generally, so this error recurs on every
line. French forces the article; English drops it.

| ✗ | ✓ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *The society is changing* | Society is changing | Uncountable abstract noun used in a general sense takes no article |
| *The technology has transformed the education* | Technology has transformed education | Same rule, twice |
| *The nature must be protected* | Nature must be protected | |
| *The life in cities is stressful* | Life in cities is stressful | |
| *The globalisation* | Globalisation | |
| *The crime is increasing* | Crime is increasing | |
| *The unemployment among the young people* | Unemployment among young people | |

Same family to watch in every essay: *society, nature, life, technology,
education, history, crime, poverty, health, pollution, art, music, science,
government* (as an institution in general), *work, research, progress*.

**The article *is* required** when you narrow the noun: *the education system of
Tunisia*, *the technology used in classrooms*, *the crime rate*. Test: if a
following phrase specifies *which one*, keep the article.

**One-minute proofread pass:** search your essay for *the* followed by an
abstract noun with no modifier. Delete.

### 2. Uncountables pluralised

French pluralises these freely; English does not. Each instance is a
word-formation error under LR. The official Band 7.5 script itself contains
*"reading gossips"* — evidence that this exact error family reaches even strong
scripts.

*informations · researches · advices · knowledges · equipments · furnitures ·
softwares · evidences · feedbacks · progresses · trainings · staffs ·
accommodations · pollutions · transports · vocabularies*

→ information · research · advice · knowledge · equipment · furniture · software ·
evidence · feedback · progress · training · staff · accommodation · pollution ·
transport · vocabulary

Quantify with a partitive: *a piece of research · some advice · two items of
equipment · a great deal of evidence*.

Also: **a research** → *a study* / *a piece of research*.

### 3. Preposition transfer

| ✗ | ✓ | Source of the error |
|---|---|---|
| *depend of* | depend **on** | *dépendre de* |
| *discuss about* | discuss Ø | *discuter de* |
| *participate to* | participate **in** | *participer à* |
| *consist in* (meaning composed of) | consist **of** | *consister en* |
| *responsible of* | responsible **for** | *responsable de* |
| *suffer of* | suffer **from** | *souffrir de* |
| *interested by* | interested **in** | *intéressé par* |
| *access to* (as verb) | access Ø | *accéder à* |
| *in the other hand* | on the **other** hand | blend of *d'autre part* |
| *contrary to* (as "on the contrary") | **by** contrast / conversely | *au contraire* |
| *since ten years* | **for** ten years | *depuis* covers both |
| *married with* | married **to** | *marié avec* |
| *arrive to* | arrive **in** (country/city) / **at** (building) | *arriver à* |
| *permit to do* | permit/allow **somebody to** do | *permettre de faire* |
| *prevent somebody to do* | prevent somebody **from** doing | in the official 7.5 script |

### 4. False friends in academic register

These are the ones that surface specifically in essays:

| Word | What it means in English | What the writer meant | Use instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| *actually* | at present is **not** its meaning — it means *in reality* | currently | currently, at present |
| *eventually* | in the end | possibly | possibly, potentially |
| *assist* | to help | to attend | attend |
| *formation* | shaping/creation | training/education | training, education |
| *sensible* | having good judgement | sensitive | sensitive |
| *deception* | trickery | disappointment | disappointment |
| *demand* | to require forcefully | to ask | ask, request |
| *support* | to bear, to back | to tolerate | tolerate, put up with |
| *achieve* | to accomplish | to finish | complete, finish |
| *control* | to have power over | to check/inspect | check, monitor, inspect |
| *engaged* | committed/betrothed | politically committed | committed |
| *stage* | phase | internship | internship, placement |
| *society* (French *société*) | a community | a company | company, firm |
| *alimentation* | **not English** | diet/nutrition | diet, nutrition *(the official examiner's own example)* |
| *significative* | **not English** | significant | significant |
| *inconvenient* (as noun) | **not a noun in English** | drawback | drawback, disadvantage |
| *actual* | real, genuine | current | current, present |
| *important* (of a number) | serious/major | large | large, substantial |
| *evolution* | slow development | change/trend | change, development, trend |
| *precise* (as verb) | — | to specify | specify, clarify |
| *notably* | especially | in particular / namely | in particular |

### 5. French/Arabic run-ons and comma splices

Both L1s tolerate long coordinate chains; Arabic rhetorical structure adds
sentence-initial connectives that English handles with subordination or a full
stop. Under GRA this shows as faulty punctuation and, at band 5, "complex
sentences… tend to be faulty".

> ✗ *Many students today they cannot find a work after the university, this is a
> big problem for the society and the government must find solutions, moreover
> the companies they don't want to train the young people.*

Three faults: resumptive pronouns (*students… they*), two comma splices, and
*moreover* used as a comma-level connector.

> ✓ *Many graduates cannot find work, and that failure is now a public problem
> rather than a private one. Governments are expected to respond, yet employers
> are increasingly unwilling to train new entrants themselves.*

Rules to internalise:
- **A comma cannot join two independent clauses.** Use a full stop, a semicolon,
  or a coordinator (*and, but, so, yet*).
- **No resumptive pronoun.** *The students they think* → *The students think*.
- **Never *although … but* together.** French *bien que … mais* is normal; English
  allows only one.
- **Never *because of that's why*.** Pick one.
- **Discourse markers open sentences, not clauses.** *Moreover* after a comma is
  a splice.

### 6. Verb agreement, stacked verbs, and third-person -s

- *people is / peoples are* → **people are** (*people* is already plural; *peoples*
  means ethnic groups).
- *the government are decide* → the government **decides** (or *has decided*).
- *he do not have* → he **does not** have.
- *This affect the society* → This **affects** society.
- *is depend on* → **depends on**.
- Avoid stacking a bare auxiliary against a bare verb: *can to do*, *must to be*.

Third-person *-s* is the single most common systematic error in Arabic-L1
academic writing and is worth a dedicated one-minute proofread pass, since
*systematic* is exactly the word that blocks band 8.

### 7. Register imported from French academic writing

French academic prose favours abstraction, nominalisation and formulaic
openings; carried into English it produces sentences that are grammatical but
imprecise — which is band 6 LR ("a lack of precision in word choice"), not band 7.

> ✗ *It is necessary to underline the fact that the problematic of the
> globalisation possesses numerous incidences on the societies.*
> ✓ *Globalisation affects societies in several ways, and three of them matter
> most here.*

Also drop: *In my opinion I think* (pick one — the official 7.5 script contains
this and it is still a redundancy), *nowadays* at the head of every paragraph,
*we can say that*, *it is a fact that*.

### 8. This reader's four-pass proofread (3 minutes)

1. **Articles** — every *the* before an unmodified abstract noun.
2. **Plurals** — *information/research/advice/knowledge/evidence/equipment* with
   an *s*.
3. **Commas** — any comma with a full sentence on both sides.
4. **-s** — every present-simple verb with a singular subject.

Four passes, all mechanical, all targeting *systematic* errors. This is a higher
return than re-reading for "mistakes" in general.
