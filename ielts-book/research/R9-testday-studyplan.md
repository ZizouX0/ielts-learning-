## Verified facts

### Marking — there is no negative marking

This underpins the entire guessing strategy, so it is stated first and sourced
hardest.

- **Reading is positively marked only.** "Each correct answer receives 1 mark."
  There is no deduction clause anywhere on the official format page — the marking
  scheme has no mechanism to subtract.
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`
- **Official instruction to answer everything.** "Answer all questions – you
  don't lose marks for any blank spaces," given for both Listening and Reading.
  `[src: ielts.idp.com — IELTS Dos and Don'ts]`
- **Corrections are not penalised either:** "marks are not deducted for making
  corrections." `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Frequently asked questions]`

**Therefore, for the book:** a blank and a wrong answer score identically — zero.
A guess is a free lottery ticket. Leaving a Listening or Reading gap empty is
strictly dominated by guessing. This is one of the few genuinely free bands
available to a 6.5-band candidate.

**The important caveats that stop this becoming bad advice** — guessing is free,
but these three things do cost marks:

- **Over the word limit = wrong.** "Answers which are over the word limit will be
  marked as incorrect."
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF (269898), p.4 and p.6]`
  So "write more words to increase my chances" is the opposite of free.
- **Spelling and grammar are marked.** "You will lose marks for incorrect
  spelling and grammar."
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF (269898)]`
  Also stated on the Reading format page in the context of transferring answers.
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`
- A sibling agent (R2) established from official answer keys that **two answers
  written in one gap score zero even if one is right.** So a "cover both options"
  guess is a zero, not a hedge. Guess *one* thing.

### Results — timing, validity, sharing

- **Computer-delivered turnaround.** The official figures genuinely differ across
  pages. The best-sourced statement is on ielts.org's dedicated results page:
  "Most IELTS on Computer results are now issued within 2 days", "Globally 80% of
  IELTS scores are available within 2 days", "About half are ready within only
  1 day." `[src: ielts.org — Fast IELTS results: get and share your score]`
  Other Tier 1 pages give **1–5 days**
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Results: Check and Understand Your
  Scores]` `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Frequently asked questions]`
  `[src: ielts.org — Ways to take IELTS]`, and the British Council booking page
  gives **1–2 days**
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS on Computer: Faster Results &
  Flexible Scheduling]`.
  **Resolution for the book:** report it as a distribution, not a single number —
  about half arrive in 1 day, ~80% within 2 days, official worst case 5 days.
  Plan university deadlines against **5 days**, not 2.
- **Paper turnaround:** "Up to 13 days."
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Results: Check and Understand Your
  Scores]`; ielts.org states "Results are available 13 days after test
  completion." `[src: ielts.org — Ways to take IELTS]`
- **IELTS Online turnaround:** "You will receive your results 6-8 calendar days
  after taking your test."
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Your IELTS Online FAQs Answered]`
  (ielts.org agrees: "Test results are available 6–8 days after taking the test."
  `[src: ielts.org — Ways to take IELTS]`)
- **Validity: two years.** "Test Report Form (TRF) is valid for two years"
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Frequently asked questions]`;
  "IELTS results are officially recognised as valid for 2 years from the test
  date." `[src: ielts.idp.com — How Long Are IELTS Scores Valid?]`
  Note the wording IELTS itself uses is a *recommendation* to receiving
  organisations, not a hard expiry — the institution decides. The book should say
  "two years from the test date; some institutions apply a shorter window, so
  check Malta/EU university requirements directly."
- **The Test Report Form** "can either be a paper copy or a digital version
  (eTRF)." IELTS Online issues eTRF only; test-centre tests receive paper forms,
  with eTRFs "in some countries."
  `[src: ielts.org — Getting and sharing your IELTS results]`
- **Sending scores to institutions.** "You can share your results by postal mail
  with up to five organisations free of charge"; e-delivery is unlimited — "you
  can share your results by IELTS Results Service e-delivery with as many
  organisations as you like."
  `[src: ielts.org — Getting and sharing your IELTS results]`
  The British Council states the free copies are available if addresses are given
  "within two years of taking the test."
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Results: Check and Understand Your
  Scores]`
- **Test Taker Portal.** Results are accessed online with "your passport or ID
  number" and candidate number; the British Council emails a link when results
  are ready. `[src: ielts.org — Getting and sharing your IELTS results]`
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Results: Check and Understand Your
  Scores]` Organisations "will be able to verify your results using the IELTS
  Results Service" — i.e. universities verify electronically at source, so a
  scanned TRF is not what they trust.
  `[src: ielts.org — Getting and sharing your IELTS results]`

### Test structure and timing on the day

- **Listening + Reading + Writing run in one session with no breaks:** "You will
  do the Listening, Reading, and Writing sections in one test session. This takes
  2 hours 40 minutes, and there are no breaks between each section of the test."
  `[src: ielts.org — What to expect on IELTS test day]`
  The British Council states the same duration and adds "Please note that there
  will be no breaks during the tests."
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Essential Tips to Excel in IELTS Test Day]`
- **Order for computer-delivered:** "you will do the tests in the following order
  on the same day: Listening, Reading and Writing, with the Speaking test before
  or after this test session." `[src: ielts.idp.com — Test day]`
- **Computer-delivered Listening has no transfer time.** "No extra time is given
  to transfer your answers"; "You will only have 2 minutes to review your answers
  at the end of the test."
  `[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]`
  Paper-based gives "10 minutes to transfer your answers onto the answer sheet."
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Essential Tips to Excel in IELTS Test Day]`
  **This is a real behavioural difference and the reader is on computer** — see
  §Computer-delivered interface tactics.
- **Reading includes transfer time inside the hour:** "60 minutes (including
  transfer time)." `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`
- **Speaking:** 11–14 minutes, 3 parts (Part 1: 4–5 min; Part 2: 3–4 min
  including preparation; Part 3: 4–5 min). "The Speaking test is recorded."
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Speaking test format]`
- **Writing weighting:** "Task 2 contributes twice as much as Task 1 to the
  Writing score." `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF (269898)]`
  This is the official basis for the 20/40-minute split; the same FAQ says "you
  may wish to spend 20 minutes on Task 1 and 40 minutes on Task 2" but is explicit
  that "It is your choice how you divide this time."

### Paper is being withdrawn

- "From mid-2026, we will no longer offer IELTS as a paper-based test. All IELTS
  tests will be delivered on computer. Exact timelines will vary by market."
  `[src: ielts.org — Updates to IELTS test delivery]`
- A **"Writing on Paper"** mode is being introduced "in selected markets",
  allowing test takers to "handwrite their answers to the 'Writing' component on
  paper" while otherwise sitting the computer test.
  `[src: ielts.org — Updates to IELTS test delivery]`
- **No country-by-country timeline is published.** Tunisia, Africa and the Middle
  East are not named. `[src: ielts.org — Updates to IELTS test delivery]`
  Whether "Writing on Paper" is offered in Tunisia is **[UNVERIFIED]** — the book
  must say "confirm with British Council Tunisia; do not assume it is available."
- The change "does not alter the IELTS skills assessed, the test construct, or the
  way results should be interpreted by institutions."
  `[src: ielts.org — Updates to IELTS test delivery]`
  **Helps-not-hurts note:** this matters for the reader because it means practice
  material written for paper IELTS is still valid content — only the *interface
  drills* need to be computer-specific.

### The single most important Tunisia decision: do NOT choose IELTS Online

The reader needs a score for **university admission in Malta/Europe**, which for
a Tunisian national means a **student visa**. IELTS Online is a distinct product
and carries a real acceptance risk:

- "IELTS Online is not currently accepted for immigration purposes."
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Online]`
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Your IELTS Online FAQs Answered]`
- Acceptance by institutions is **optional, not guaranteed**: "Receiving
  organisations can decide whether they are happy to accept results from IELTS
  Academic tests that are taken online or not."
  `[src: ielts.org — Ways to take IELTS]`
- IELTS itself tells you to check first: "We advise you to check with your chosen
  institution(s) directly before booking IELTS Online."
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Online]`
- **Headphones are banned on IELTS Online:** "No, headphones are not permitted" —
  you must use speakers.
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Your IELTS Online FAQs Answered]`
  For a Listening test where a single missed word is a mark, sitting it on room
  speakers instead of headphones is a self-inflicted handicap.
- IELTS Online requires **a passport** specifically (a national identity card is
  listed for centre booking but the Online equipment list says "a passport").
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Your IELTS Online FAQs Answered]`
- Results are slower — 6–8 days versus ~1–5 on computer at a centre.
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Your IELTS Online FAQs Answered]`
- eTRF only, no paper TRF. `[src: ielts.org — Getting and sharing your results]`

**Recommendation for the book:** book **IELTS Academic on computer at a British
Council Tunisia centre**. It is accepted everywhere the reader needs it, it is
the fastest result, and it gives headphones. The only reason to consider IELTS
Online would be an inability to travel to one of the five centres — and even then
the university and the Maltese visa authority must be asked in writing first.

## Registration, ID and what to bring

### Registration and ID

1. **Book online** with British Council Tunisia (or in person at their centre).
   `[src: britishcouncil.tn — Réservez votre examen IELTS (FR)]`
2. **Register with a valid passport or national identity card (CIN).**
   `[src: britishcouncil.tn — Réservez votre examen IELTS (FR)]`
3. **Bring the identical document on test day.** "Bring the same identification
   document that you used when you booked your test. This is important. You won't
   be able to take your test if you bring the wrong ID or forget to bring it."
   `[src: ielts.org — What to expect on IELTS test day]`
   "If you arrive without the correct ID you will not be able to take the test."
   `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Essential Tips to Excel in IELTS Test Day]`
   Tunisia states it in the strongest terms: a different, invalid or expired
   document "will not be accepted."
   `[src: britishcouncil.tn — Réservez votre examen IELTS (FR)]`
4. **A photograph is taken before the test** and "will be used on your Test Report
   Form." A finger scan may also be required in some countries.
   `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Essential Tips to Excel in IELTS Test Day]`
5. **The same ID is required again for the Speaking test.**
   `[src: ielts.idp.com — Test day]`

**Non-obvious tactical point — register with your passport, not your CIN.** Both
are accepted in Tunisia `[src: britishcouncil.tn — Réservez votre examen IELTS
(FR)]`, but the ID number on the registration is what appears on the TRF and what
the Test Taker Portal uses to release your results
`[src: ielts.org — Getting and sharing your IELTS results]`. A Maltese or European
university and a visa office will be matching your English certificate against
your **passport**. Registering with the passport removes a name-transliteration
and document-mismatch risk that is entirely avoidable — a real issue for Arabic
names rendered differently on a CIN and a passport. Also check that your passport
does not expire before you finish using the score.
*(This is my reasoning from the sourced rules, not a quoted policy — flagged as
inference, not fact.)*

## Test-day timeline

Written for **computer-delivered at a Tunisian centre**, the reader's likely
format. Times before check-in are recommendations, not policy; everything from
check-in on is sourced above.

| When | What happens |
|---|---|
| Night before | Charge nothing, pack nothing electronic. Put **ID + booking confirmation** by the door. "Set an alarm and leave enough time to get to the test centre without rushing." `[src: ielts.org — What to expect on IELTS test day]` |
| Morning | "Have something to eat and drink before the test." `[src: ielts.org — What to expect on IELTS test day]` No food is allowed inside, and there are no breaks for 2h40 — under-eating is a real performance risk. |
| T−60 to T−45 | Travel. Build in Tunis traffic margin. Lateness may forfeit the test. |
| T−30 (minimum) | Arrive at the centre. `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Test Day (blog)]` |
| Check-in | ID verified against the booking; **photograph taken** for the TRF; possible finger scan; belongings into a locker; phone off. `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Essential Tips to Excel in IELTS Test Day]` `[src: ielts.idp.com — Test day]` |
| Immediately before entry | **Go to the toilet.** "Make sure you go to the toilet before you enter the test room." `[src: ielts.org — What to expect on IELTS test day]` |
| Seated | Receive login details sheet + pen and paper. `[src: ielts.idp.com — Test day]` Set headphone/on-screen volume now, not once the audio starts. |
| Listening (~30 min) | Plays once. **No transfer time**; only "2 minutes to review your answers at the end." `[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]` |
| Reading (60 min) | Transfer time is **inside** the 60 minutes. `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]` |
| Writing (60 min) | Task 1 + Task 2. Task 2 is worth double. `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]` |
| Throughout | **No breaks.** Total 2h40. `[src: ielts.org — What to expect on IELTS test day]` |
| Speaking | Same day (before or after) or a different day, at the centre. `[src: ielts.idp.com — Test day]` `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS on Computer]` Bring the same ID again. |
| Results | Most within 2 days; official range up to 5. Email link → Test Taker Portal. `[src: ielts.org — Fast IELTS results]` |

**Unresolved: the Speaking scheduling window.** Official sources disagree.
ielts.org's format page describes Speaking as "a face-to-face interview between
the test taker and an examiner" with no window given
`[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Speaking test format]`; IDP says computer
Speaking is "before or after this test session" on the same day, while for paper
"Speaking may occur up to 7 days before or after"
`[src: ielts.idp.com — Test day]`; the British Council booking page says only
"You will also take this at a test centre, but it may be on a different day"
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS on Computer]`.
**The book must not print a single number.** It should say: your Speaking slot is
on your booking confirmation; it may be the same day or a different day, and you
must plan your energy for the possibility that Speaking happens *after* 2h40 of
writing on the same day.

**Unresolved: face-to-face vs video call.** ielts.org says face-to-face
`[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Speaking test format]`; the British Council
computer-delivered booking page says "Your Speaking test is a live conversation
with an examiner, either face-to-face or via video call."
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS on Computer]`
Both Tier 1. **Practical consequence for the reader:** rehearse both. A
video-call Speaking test at a centre changes nothing about the criteria, but it
does change eye-contact habits and makes clear articulation matter more. Prepare
for face-to-face, be unshaken if it is a screen.

## Computer-delivered interface tactics

Every feature below is sourced; the *tactic* attached to it is my analysis and is
marked as such. Each tactic is filtered for "does this raise a 6.5 to a 7?"

### 1. Copy-paste in Reading — the single biggest free-marks feature

**The feature.** You can copy text from the passage into the answer box with
CTRL+C / CTRL+V. "Instead of retyping words, use copy-paste to avoid spelling
mistakes in fill-in-the-blank type questions."
`[src: ielts.idp.com — Want to Ace Your IELTS on Computer Reading Component?]`
"You can copy and paste from the text for the Reading and Writing tests."
`[src: ielts.idp.com — Seven benefits of taking IELTS on computer]`
Corroborated Tier 2: "You can copy/paste from the text using CTRL+C and CTRL+V
which reduces the chances of typos."
`[src: ieltsliz.com — Computer Delivered IELTS: Pros & Cons]`

**Why it raises a band for *this* reader.** Completion answers in Reading must be
taken from the passage unchanged, and spelling is marked. An Arabic/French-L1
typist retyping *accommodation*, *phenomenon*, *entrepreneurship* or
*bureaucracy* under time pressure will eventually mistype one. Copy-paste makes
the spelling error rate structurally zero on those items. On a 40-question paper
where each mark is worth roughly a quarter of a band near the 6.5–7 boundary,
recovering two or three typo losses is a genuine half-band.

**Tactic (my analysis):** use copy-paste for every completion-type answer where
the word is visible in the passage. Then **re-read the pasted text** — a drag
selection easily grabs a trailing space, a comma, or an extra word, and an answer
over the word limit "will be marked as incorrect."
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]`
Do *not* use it for question types requiring your own words, and never paste a
two-word phrase into a "ONE WORD ONLY" gap.

### 2. Highlight tool

**The feature.** "Simply left click and drag your cursor over the section of text
you wish to highlight, then right click and select the 'highlight' option"; to
remove, "right click on the highlighted area and select 'clear all'."
Highlighting is invisible to examiners and does not affect your score, and you do
not need to clear it before moving on.
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Highlighting text (IELTS on computer)]`
Also available in Reading via right-click, with notes attached the same way
`[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]`, and available in
Listening `[src: ieltsliz.com — Computer Delivered IELTS: Pros & Cons]`.

**Tactic (my analysis):** highlight *evidence*, not topic. One highlight per
answered question, on the phrase that proves it. This gives you a physical audit
trail so that if you finish early you can re-verify answers in seconds rather than
re-reading paragraphs. Highlighting whole paragraphs is the beginner failure mode
— it marks everything, so it marks nothing.

### 3. Note-taking tool

**The feature.** "If you wish, you can select the part of the text and click right
on it to highlight it with a different color and take notes on the screen."
`[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]`
The British Council publishes a dedicated "IELTS on computer - Making notes"
tutorial. `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS on computer: how it works]`
Plus physical paper: the centre provides pen and paper
`[src: ielts.idp.com — Test day]`, and you may write on the login details sheet
`[src: ielts.idp.com — Test day]`.

**Tactic (my analysis):** use **paper** for Writing planning and the on-screen
tool only for micro-notes in Reading. Planning a Task 2 essay in an on-screen note
box costs mouse time and screen space; a 60-second paper plan (position →
paragraph 1 idea → paragraph 2 idea → conclusion) is faster and is visible while
you type.

### 4. On-screen word count

**The feature.** "You can also see the number of words you typed in the lower-left
corner." `[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]`
"You do not need to count your words. The computer will show the word count."
`[src: ieltsliz.com — Computer Delivered IELTS: Pros & Cons]`

**Why it matters.** This eliminates the paper candidate's tax of counting lines,
and it eliminates the risk of finishing 20 words under 250 without knowing.
Under-length damages Task Response and Task Achievement.

**Tactic (my analysis):** treat **270 for Task 2 and 170 for Task 1** as the
target — a margin above the minimum, not a target of exactly 250/150. Two
reasons: R4 established that **copied rubric is discounted before the word count**,
so a candidate who paraphrases weakly can be under length while the counter says
they are over; and 250 exactly leaves no room to delete a bad sentence during
checking. Glance at the counter twice only — at the end of body paragraph 1 and
at the end. Watching it continuously is a fluency killer.

### 5. Review / flag function

**The feature.** In Listening, "when you click the 'Review' button in the
lower-left corner, the question number changes from a square to a circle, so you
mark which question you will review."
`[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]`

**Tactic (my analysis), and it is important given there is no transfer time.**
On paper, a Listening candidate has 10 minutes at the end to tidy up; on computer
you have **2 minutes**. `[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]`
So: when you miss an answer, **type your best guess immediately, flag it, and move
on**. Never leave it blank intending to return — there is no negative marking
`[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`
`[src: ielts.idp.com — IELTS Dos and Don'ts]`, so the guess costs nothing and
protects you if the 2 minutes evaporate. Spend the 2 minutes only on flagged
items and on scanning for empty boxes.

### 6. Timer

**The feature.** The countdown sits "in the upper middle part of the screen" and
"starts to flash in the last 10 minutes and 5 minutes before reading and writing
tests end." `[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]`
Tier 2 adds that it stops showing seconds in the final minute
`[src: ieltsliz.com — Computer Delivered IELTS: Pros & Cons]`.

**Tactic (my analysis):** the flash at 10 minutes is a *designed interruption*.
Decide in advance what you do when it flashes — for Writing that is "stop
generating new content, start checking articles and verb endings"; for Reading it
is "stop reading, fill every remaining blank with a guess." Deciding this on the
day is how candidates lose 4 marks in the last 10 minutes.

### 7. Volume control and audio

**The feature.** "With the volume control bar in the upper right corner, you can
provide full volume control during the test."
`[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]`
Headphones are typically provided at centres but this **varies by centre** —
candidates are advised to check locally. **[UNVERIFIED for Tunisia]**
Contrast: IELTS Online forbids headphones entirely.
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Your IELTS Online FAQs Answered]`

**Tactic (my analysis):** set the level during the instructions, before the first
recording, and set it slightly higher than feels comfortable in silence — the room
gets noisier once forty people start typing. You retain control during the test,
so if the first speaker is quiet, adjust immediately rather than straining through
a whole section.

### 8. Auto-save and screen layout

**The features.** "Since the answers are saved automatically, you do not need to
think about if your answers are saved or not."
`[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]`
Reading is split-screen — "the computer test displays the passage on one side and
questions on the other"
`[src: ielts.idp.com — Want to Ace Your IELTS on Computer Reading Component?]` —
and font size can be increased
`[src: ieltsliz.com — Computer Delivered IELTS: Pros & Cons]`.

**Known limitation:** "You cannot use a search function to look for words"
`[src: ieltsliz.com — Computer Delivered IELTS: Pros & Cons]` — CTRL+F does not
work. **Tactic:** scanning is still a human skill; do not build a strategy that
assumes you can search.

### 9. Typing itself is a scored variable

**The reasoning (my analysis, from sourced facts).** Writing is marked on what
appears on screen; typos are indistinguishable from spelling errors to a marker
reading the script, and "You will lose marks for incorrect spelling and grammar"
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]`. Tier 2 flags exactly this
risk: "We unintentionally make typo errors" despite knowing the correct spelling
`[src: ieltsliz.com — Computer Delivered IELTS: Pros & Cons]`.

**Tactic:** for a French-keyboard user this is a specific, fixable hazard. Tunisian
candidates typically type on **AZERTY**; test centre keyboards are commonly QWERTY
**[UNVERIFIED — ask the centre]**. Practise every timed Writing task on the same
layout you will face. If you cannot confirm the layout, practise on QWERTY, since
that is the more likely default and the more damaging surprise. There is **no
spellchecker** in the IELTS writing box — assume it and prove it in a
familiarisation test rather than discovering it on the day.

## Study plans

### The architecture, and why it is shaped this way

**Evidence driving the weighting.** R1 extracted official 2024–25 test-taker
performance data: Arabic-L1 candidates average **5.54** on Academic Writing
(5.95 overall); French-L1 candidates average **6.15** on Writing (6.75 overall).
`[src: ielts.org — IELTS test taker performance data 2024–2025 (xlsx)]`
For both of this reader's language backgrounds, **Writing is the weakest skill**,
and it is also the skill with the lowest per-hour improvement rate. It therefore
gets the largest share of practice time and the earliest start.

**Time budget.** 90 minutes/day, applying the prescribed 70 / 20 / 10 split:

| Block | Share | Minutes | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| Theory input | 10% | **9 min** | One technique, one rule, one question type. Read/watch, no practice. |
| Practice + feedback | 70% | **63 min** | Timed task, then self-marking against descriptors or answer key. The *feedback* half is not optional — practice without marking is just typing. |
| Error review | 20% | **18 min** | Re-read yesterday's errors, log today's into `progress/error-log.md`, `vocab-bank.md`, `band-tracker.md`. |

**Daily shape (identical every day, so it needs no decisions):**

```
00–09  THEORY      one narrow thing. Never a whole chapter.
09–72  PRACTICE    timed task under test conditions + self-marking
72–90  ERROR WORK  log to the three progress files; re-read the last 3 days' errors
```

**Weekly rotation.** Six working days plus one lighter consolidation day. Writing
gets two full days plus a share of the mixed day; Speaking gets a full day plus
10 minutes folded into other days, because fluency is a motor skill that decays
without frequent contact.

| Day | Focus |
|---|---|
| Mon | **Writing Task 2** (the double-weighted task) |
| Tue | **Reading** |
| Wed | **Writing Task 1** |
| Thu | **Listening** |
| Fri | **Speaking** (record yourself; all three parts) |
| Sat | **Mixed / timed section under full conditions** |
| Sun | **Review day** — no new material: re-read the error log, quiz the vocab bank, rewrite one old essay |

**The rewrite rule, which is the highest-leverage habit in the whole plan.** Every
Sunday, rewrite one essay you have already been graded on, applying the
corrections. Producing a *new* mediocre essay teaches you nothing; converting a
band-6 essay of your own into a band-7 essay teaches you exactly what the gap
feels like. (My recommendation, not a sourced claim.)

**Test date is not set** — `CLAUDE.md` records it as NOT YET SET. The plans below
are numbered by week, not dated. When the date is fixed, count backwards from it
and write real dates against each week.

---

### 6-week plan (≈54 hours) — for a reader already near 6.0

Compressed. Assumes the diagnostic shows no section below 5.5.

| Week | Phase | Focus | Milestone at the end of the week |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Diagnose | Day 1: full timed Writing Task 2 under exam conditions → establishes the baseline `CLAUDE.md` says is missing. Days 2–4: one timed section of each of Reading, Listening, Speaking. Days 5–7: build the error map from these four scripts. | Four baseline bands in `band-tracker.md`; top 5 recurring errors named. |
| **2** | Build | Writing Task 2 structure: the four question families and a paragraph template for each. Reading: the two question types you lost most marks on. Listening: prediction and spelling. | Task 2 essays finish inside 40 min at 270+ words, every time. |
| **3** | Build | Writing Task 1: overview + **grouping** (band-7 TA requires data "appropriately categorised", per R4). Reading: timing — 20 min per passage. Speaking: Part 2 fluency to 2 minutes without stopping. | Task 1 always has an overview and never lists data row by row. |
| **4** | Drill | Volume week. Full timed sections daily. Error log drives the theory slot — every 9-minute theory block this week must address a *logged* error, not a general topic. | Error frequency for your top 3 patterns is measurably falling. |
| **5** | Simulate | Two full Listening+Reading+Writing sittings (2h40, no breaks, computer, typed). One full Speaking simulation with pushback. | You know your true band under real conditions, not your practice band. |
| **6** | Taper | Days 1–3: light targeted drills only, no new material. Day 4: one final full sitting. Days 5–6: re-read error log and vocab bank only. Day 7 (or day before test): **rest**. | Arrive fresh. |

### 8-week plan (≈72 hours) — the default recommendation

The best fit for a 90-min/day reader targeting 7.0 from an unknown baseline.

| Week | Phase | Focus | Milestone |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Diagnose | Full baseline: one timed task in every skill, spread across the week. Plus: read the **public band descriptors** end to end (Writing and Speaking) — you cannot self-mark against a standard you have not read. | Four baselines logged; descriptors read once. |
| **2** | Diagnose → Build | Error taxonomy. Sort every logged error into: L1 interference (articles, uncountables, prepositions, false friends), structural (paragraphing, overview, position), or timing. Fix the *category* with the most marks attached first. | You can name your three most expensive error patterns. |
| **3** | Build | **Writing Task 2** deep work: the four question families; position statements; body paragraph = claim → explain → example. | Every essay answers *all parts* of the question — the commonest band-6 TR cap. |
| **4** | Build | **Writing Task 1** deep work: all visual types; overview; grouping; tense and comparison language. **Reading**: matching headings + TFNG/YNNG, the two highest-loss types. | Task 1 done in 20 min with an overview. |
| **5** | Drill | **Listening** + **Speaking** intensive. Listening: computer-delivered discipline — no transfer time, flag-and-guess. Speaking: record all three parts daily, transcribe two answers a week and mark your own grammar. | Listening raw score stable; you can hear your own L1 errors on playback. |
| **6** | Drill | Mixed high-volume week. Every day is a timed section. Theory slot = only logged errors. Rewrite two previously graded essays. | Error frequency falling; timing automatic. |
| **7** | Simulate | Two full 2h40 sittings on computer, typed, plus two full Speaking simulations. Mark strictly against the descriptors — award the lower band when torn. | A realistic predicted overall band. |
| **8** | Taper | Days 1–3: targeted drills on the weakest single criterion only. Day 4: last full sitting. Days 5–6: error log + vocab bank review, no new tasks. Day 7: rest, logistics check (ID, route, confirmation). | Fresh, and nothing left to organise. |

### 10-week plan (≈90 hours) — for a reader starting below 6.0, or nervous

Adds a genuine language-building block at the front. The extra time goes into
**grammar accuracy and vocabulary depth**, because a reader below 6.0 is usually
capped by Grammatical Range & Accuracy and Lexical Resource, not by technique.

| Week | Phase | Focus | Milestone |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Diagnose | Full baseline in all four skills + read both public band descriptor sets. | Baselines logged. |
| **2** | Foundation | **Grammar accuracy block 1** — articles with abstract nouns and uncountables (the highest-frequency French-interference errors). Daily: 20 sentences of your own, self-corrected. | *the society / informations / researches* stop appearing. |
| **3** | Foundation | **Grammar accuracy block 2** — complex sentences: relative clauses, conditionals, concession. Band 7 GRA needs a *range* of complex forms, not error-free simple ones. | Every essay contains 4+ correctly formed complex sentences. |
| **4** | Build | Writing Task 2: question families, position, paragraph structure. | Full essay in 40 min. |
| **5** | Build | Writing Task 1: all visual types, overview, grouping. | Overview present every time. |
| **6** | Build | Reading: all question types, then timing to 20 min/passage. | Full paper inside 60 min. |
| **7** | Drill | Listening + Speaking intensive, as week 5 of the 8-week plan. | Stable Listening score; recorded Speaking improving. |
| **8** | Drill | Mixed volume + rewrites of previously graded essays. | Top-3 error patterns visibly declining. |
| **9** | Simulate | Three full sittings + Speaking simulations, strictly marked. | Predicted band. |
| **10** | Taper | As week 8 of the 8-week plan. | Fresh. |

### Rules that apply to all three plans

1. **Never practise Writing untimed.** An essay that took 70 minutes tells you
   nothing about a 40-minute test.
2. **Always type.** The test is computer-delivered
   `[src: ielts.org — Test centre: British Council Tunis]`, and typing speed and
   typo rate are scored variables in practice.
3. **Mark before you move on.** An unmarked practice test is worse than no
   practice test, because it consolidates the errors.
4. **The theory slot is 9 minutes.** If you find yourself reading strategy
   articles for 40 minutes, you have converted a practice day into a browsing day.
   This is the most common way a 90-minute plan produces no band movement.
5. **Log every session.** The three progress files are the only thing that makes
   week 6 smarter than week 1.
6. **One full rest day before the test.** Do not sit a mock the day before.

## Self-assessment without a teacher

This is the hardest problem in self-study: Listening and Reading mark themselves,
but **Writing and Speaking are 50% of the test and neither can be scored by an
answer key.**

### Step 1 — read the actual descriptors

Download and read both public band descriptor documents:

- Writing: https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf
- Speaking: https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors.pdf

**Note for the book's author:** the descriptors must be **paraphrased**, never
reproduced verbatim, per the content-integrity rule in `CLAUDE.md`. The reader is
directed to the official PDFs to read the wording themselves.

Also read the official **examiner-commented sample responses** — seeing a real
script labelled band 6 and another labelled band 7, with the examiner's reasons,
calibrates you faster than any amount of rule-reading.
`[src: ielts.org — Academic Writing example responses with band scores and
examiner comments (PDF)]`

### Step 2 — the four-pass self-marking protocol (Writing)

Mark **one criterion per pass**. Marking all four at once produces a vague global
impression, which is exactly the error that inflates self-assessed bands.

1. **Task Response / Task Achievement.** Does it answer *every* part of the
   question? Is there a clear position held throughout? (Task 1: is there an
   overview, and is the data **grouped** rather than listed? R4 established
   grouping is in the band-7 descriptor.)
2. **Coherence & Cohesion.** Cover the essay and write the one-sentence point of
   each paragraph from memory. If you cannot, that paragraph has no central idea —
   a direct CC cap.
3. **Lexical Resource.** Count repeated content words. Count word-choice errors.
   Mark every French false friend (*significative*, *actually*, *eventually*,
   *assist*, *formation*, *sensible*, *deception*).
4. **Grammatical Range & Accuracy.** Count sentences. Count error-free sentences.
   The ratio is the single best proxy for GRA. Then count *types* of complex
   structure — band 7 needs range, not just accuracy.

**Then award the lower band when torn.** Per `CLAUDE.md`: a script must *fully*
meet a band's positive features to earn it.

### Step 3 — the 48-hour rule

Never mark your own writing on the day you wrote it. You still remember what you
meant, so you read your intention rather than your sentence. Wait 48 hours and you
read it as a stranger — which is what the examiner is.
*(My recommendation, not a sourced claim.)*

### Step 4 — Speaking self-assessment

You cannot grade your own Speaking live. You can grade a recording.

1. **Record all three parts** on your phone. Answer without stopping.
2. **Transcribe 60 seconds** by hand. Painful, slow, and the single most effective
   diagnostic available — every dropped third-person *-s*, every *peoples are*,
   every *depend of* appears in writing where you cannot miss it.
3. **Time your Part 2.** Under 2 minutes is a Fluency & Coherence cap.
4. **Count fillers and self-corrections** per minute; track the number weekly.
5. **Pronunciation:** you cannot mark your own reliably. Use a proxy — play a
   recording to a fluent-English speaker and ask only "which words did you have to
   work to understand?" Intelligibility, not accent, is what the criterion is
   about.

### Step 5 — using AI as an examiner, and its real limits

**The method that works:**

1. Paste the **official public band descriptors** into the prompt. Without them,
   an AI grades against a vague internalised average and drifts high.
2. Ask for **one band per criterion with a quoted piece of your own text as
   evidence for each**. A band with no quotation is not a grade, it is an opinion.
3. Explicitly instruct: **award the lower band when torn, and state exactly what
   was missing from the higher band.** This is the same instruction `CLAUDE.md`
   gives, and it exists precisely because the default behaviour is generosity.
4. Ask it to **hunt named error families** — for this reader: articles with
   abstract nouns, pluralised uncountables, French prepositions, false friends,
   run-on sentences. A general "check my grammar" prompt returns generic advice; a
   named-pattern prompt returns your actual recurring errors.
5. Ask for the **single highest-impact fix**, not a list of ten.

**The limits, stated honestly:**

- **An AI cannot hear pronunciation.** Pronunciation is 25% of the Speaking band.
  Any AI Speaking score is at best a three-criteria estimate wearing a
  four-criteria label. Transcript-based feedback covers Fluency & Coherence
  partially, Lexical Resource and Grammatical Range & Accuracy well, and
  Pronunciation not at all.
- **It grades generously by default.** Unless forced to the descriptors and
  instructed to round down, it will reward effort and length. Treat an unprompted
  AI band as roughly half a band optimistic and calibrate against the official
  examiner-commented samples.
- **It is not an examiner and has no calibration.** Real examiners are trained and
  standardised against sample scripts. An AI has no such anchor, so it is
  inconsistent across sessions — the same essay can score differently twice.
- **It cannot see the real rating scale.** Only the *public* descriptors are
  published; examiners work from a fuller internal version.
- **What it is genuinely good at**, and where the reader should lean on it: finding
  every instance of a *named* error pattern, explaining the rule behind a
  correction, generating unlimited original practice prompts, and marking Listening
  and Reading against a key. Use it as a tireless error-finder and a rule-explainer,
  not as an oracle for your band.
- **Triangulate.** If the AI says 7.0 and the official band-7 sample script is
  visibly better than yours, you are not a 7. The sample script wins.

*(This section is analysis and method, not sourced fact. The only sourced claims
in it are the descriptor URLs and the existence of the examiner-commented sample
responses.)*

### When an EOR is actually worth it (my analysis)

Apply for a remark only when **one section is anomalously below your practice
average and it is the section blocking your offer** — most often Writing, which is
the only section where human judgement, not a key, produces the score. A remark of
Listening or Reading is nearly always wasted money: those are machine-checkable
against a fixed key.

**Compare against the alternative.** If One Skill Retake is available at your
centre, a fresh attempt at the failing skill may beat a remark, because a remark
can only recover marks the examiner arguably missed, whereas a retake lets you
actually perform better. But note the clash of clocks: **OSR must be sat within 60
days** `[src: britishcouncil.tn — IELTS One Skill Retake]`, and an EOR can take up
to 21 days to return `[src: ielts.idp.com — How to request a remark]`. Applying
for a remark and *then* deciding on OSR can burn a third of the retake window.
Decide which route you want quickly.

## Test-day anxiety management that is actually actionable

Filtered hard: only techniques with a concrete instruction, and only where the
mechanism plausibly raises a 6.5–7.5 score. General reassurance is excluded.

### 1. Familiarity is the anxiety intervention with the biggest score effect

"Knowing exactly what to expect on test day is an important way to manage pre-test
anxiety", and practice tests "familiarise yourself with the test format and task
types".
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — How to Relax Before your Exam]`
`[src: ielts.idp.com — Mastering focus for IELTS success]`

**Why it raises a band:** anxiety consumes working memory, and working memory is
what you use to hold a Listening answer while the next question starts. Every
interface surprise on the day — an unfamiliar timer, an unexpected question layout
— spends attention you needed for content. This is why the British Council
computer familiarisation tutorials are on the resource list as a *priority*, not a
nice-to-have.

**Instruction:** sit at least two full mocks under real conditions (typed, timed,
no breaks, 2h40) before test day. The goal is that nothing on the day is new.

### 2. The physiological reset — 4-4-6 breathing

"Try inhaling deeply through your nose for four seconds, holding your breath for
four seconds, and exhaling slowly through your mouth for six seconds."
`[src: ielts.idp.com — Tips for conquering IELTS test anxiety]`

**When to deploy it, specifically (my analysis):** three moments —
(a) seated before the Listening audio starts; (b) the instant you realise you have
missed a Listening answer; (c) in the corridor before Speaking. The longer exhale
is the active ingredient; do not shorten it.

### 3. Body language, before Speaking

ielts.org publishes an unusually concrete protocol: identify your own confident
posture in relaxed settings ("How do you sit? What position is natural for your
feet? What do you usually do with your hands?"); rehearse it — "Sit down and adjust
your body to your 'confident' pose, starting at the feet and working your way up";
on the day, walk "in a way that says: 'I'm on a mission'"; on entering the room
"take a minute to adjust your body to your usual 'confidence' position" and
breathe deliberately to avoid shallow, robotic speech; see "the examiner as a human
being"; and "Wriggle your toes for a few seconds" to stimulate nerve endings and
sharpen focus. The rationale given is embedded cognition — posture influences brain
state bidirectionally.
`[src: ielts.org — Using body language to reduce stress levels and improve IELTS
performance]`

**Why it raises a band:** Fluency & Coherence penalises hesitation and
self-correction. Shallow, high-chest breathing produces short, broken phrases. The
mechanism from posture → breathing → phrase length → F&C band is direct.

### 4. Progressive muscle relaxation, the night before

"Starting from your toes, tense each muscle group in your body for five seconds,
then release, gradually moving up through your legs, abdomen, arms, and finally to
your face."
`[src: ielts.idp.com — Tips for conquering IELTS test anxiety]`
Sleep quality "can change how your body deals with stress".
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Tips for managing stress during IELTS test
preparation]`

### 5. The mistake-quarantine rule

"If you feel you've made an error, don't dwell on it — move forward and focus on
the next question, as stressing over a mistake can impact your performance in the
remaining sections."
`[src: ielts.idp.com — Mastering focus for IELTS success]`

**Why this is the most important one on the list, for this test specifically (my
analysis):** computer-delivered Listening plays once with **no transfer time**
`[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]`. A candidate who
spends four seconds regretting question 12 has also lost question 13. The single
missed answer costs one mark; the rumination costs three. **Rehearse the recovery,
not just the skill:** during practice, deliberately skip an answer and train the
reflex — *type a guess, flag it, eyes forward.*

### 6. Eat beforehand

"Have something to eat and drink before the test."
`[src: ielts.org — What to expect on IELTS test day]`
No food is permitted inside `[src: ielts.org — What to expect on IELTS test day]`
and there are no breaks for 2h40 `[src: ielts.org — What to expect on IELTS test
day]`. Writing is the last section — the one worth the most and marked the
hardest — and it is sat at the two-and-a-half-hour mark. Arriving under-fuelled is
a Writing-band decision disguised as a breakfast decision.

### 7. Remove logistics from the anxiety budget

The night before, place ID and booking confirmation by the door; confirm the route
and the reporting time from the booking confirmation; set two alarms. Every
uncertainty removed the night before is attention available the next morning.
Grounded in the sourced rules that wrong or missing ID forfeits the test
`[src: ielts.org — What to expect on IELTS test day]` and that lateness may forfeit
it `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Essential Tips to Excel in IELTS Test
Day]`.

## MYTHS

Test-day and study advice that is wrong, outdated, or actively score-lowering.

### M1. "Bring your own pencils and a spare eraser."

**Wrong, and it can get you turned away.** "Personal stationery, including pencils,
are not permitted in the test room."
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Essential Tips to Excel in IELTS Test Day]`
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — FAQs]`
For paper tests the centre supplies a black ballpoint pen
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — FAQs]`; for computer tests the centre
provides pen and paper `[src: ielts.idp.com — Test day]`.

**Genuine Tier 1 conflict, flagged for verifiers.** Cambridge's own IELTS Academic
FAQ still says "You can write in pen or pencil, but you must write clearly."
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF (269898), p.7]`
The British Council policy pages are the more recent and more operational source
and are unambiguous that personal pencils are barred. **The book should follow the
British Council wording** and add "the centre provides what you need; bring
nothing."
**Why the myth hurts:** it is one of the most-repeated pieces of test-day advice
online, and acting on it means arriving with contraband and starting the day with
a confiscation argument.

### M2. "You get 10 minutes at the end of Listening to transfer your answers."

**True on paper, false on computer** — and Tunisia is computer-delivered.
Computer: "no extra time is given to transfer your answers"; "You will only have 2
minutes to review your answers at the end of the test."
`[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]`
Paper: "you will be given 10 minutes to transfer your answers onto the answer
sheet." `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Essential Tips to Excel in IELTS
Test Day]`
**Why it hurts:** a candidate rehearsing the paper habit — jot roughly now, tidy
later — arrives at the end of computer Listening with untidied answers and 2
minutes. This is one of the most expensive format-transfer errors available.

### M3. "Leave it blank if you're not sure — a wrong answer looks worse."

**There is no negative marking.** "Each correct answer receives 1 mark."
`[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`
"Answer all questions – you don't lose marks for any blank spaces."
`[src: ielts.idp.com — IELTS Dos and Don'ts]`
**Why it hurts:** it converts a free ~25% shot at a mark into a guaranteed zero.
Over a 40-question Reading paper this is a measurable fraction of a band.

### M4. "Hedge your bets — write both possible answers in the gap."

**Scores zero.** R2 established from Cambridge's FAQ that two answers in one gap
score zero even if one of them is right. Combine with M3: **guess, but guess one
thing.**

### M5. "Write as much as possible — longer is safer."

**Not in Listening or Reading:** "Answers which are over the word limit will be
marked as incorrect."
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF (269898)]`
**And not in Writing either:** length substitutes for neither Task Response nor
accuracy, and every extra sentence is another chance to make the grammar errors
that cap GRA. Aim for 270 (Task 2) and 170 (Task 1), then spend the remaining time
checking.

### M6. "Memorise model essays and adapt them on the day."

Cambridge is explicit: "Don't waste your time learning essays by heart to use in
the exam. You will be penalised for this and you will waste valuable time that
could be spent developing good writing skills."
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF (269898)]`
Related, and equally penalised: "Don't simply copy words and phrases from the
question paper." `[src: same]` R4 found that copied rubric is discounted **before**
the word count — so copying can silently push you under length while your on-screen
counter says you are fine.

### M7. "IELTS Online is the same test, so it's fine for my university."

**It is a different product with different acceptance.** "Receiving organisations
can decide whether they are happy to accept results from IELTS Academic tests that
are taken online or not." `[src: ielts.org — Ways to take IELTS]`
"IELTS Online is not currently accepted for immigration purposes."
`[src: ielts.org — IELTS Online]`
**Why it hurts this reader specifically:** a Tunisian applying to Malta/Europe needs
a student visa. Booking IELTS Online risks a TND 750 test that the visa route will
not accept — and, separately, forces the Listening test through speakers because
"headphones are not permitted."
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Your IELTS Online FAQs Answered]`

### M8. "Computer results come in 24 hours, so I can book right up to the deadline."

**Overconfident.** The optimistic figure exists — the British Council booking page
says 1–2 days `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS on Computer]` and
ielts.org says about half are ready within one day
`[src: ielts.org — Fast IELTS results]` — but other official pages give 1–5 days
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — FAQs]` `[src: ielts.org — Ways to take IELTS]`.
Plan against 5 days. Note also that results must then reach the institution, and
EOR takes up to a further 21 days
`[src: ielts.idp.com — How to request a remark]`.

### M9. "You can wear your watch to keep time."

**No watches at all**, smart or otherwise. "Personal watches are not allowed in the
test room" — there is a wall clock.
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — FAQs]` `[src: ielts.org — What to expect on
IELTS test day]`
On computer, the on-screen timer is your clock and it flashes at 10 and 5 minutes
remaining. `[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]`

### M10. "I can bring a coffee/energy drink to stay sharp."

**Only water, only in a transparent bottle.** "You cannot bring food or drink into
the test room – with the exception of water in a transparent bottle."
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Essential Tips to Excel in IELTS Test Day]`
An opaque or labelled sports bottle risks being refused at the door.

### M11. "Doing more practice tests is the way to improve."

**Practice without marking and error-logging does not move a band** — it rehearses
the errors. This is the reason the plan enforces 70% practice-*and-feedback*, 20%
error review, 10% theory rather than 100% practice. A reader who sits five
unmarked mocks has spent 12 hours consolidating their current band. *(My analysis,
consistent with the plan structure required for this book.)*

### M12. "Study hard right up to the day before."

The plan's taper exists because the last 48 hours change nothing about your
English but change a great deal about your working memory. A mock the day before
produces a bad score, and the bad score produces the anxiety
`[src: ielts.idp.com — Mastering focus for IELTS success]` that costs marks on the
day. Rest is a strategy, not a reward.

### M13. "Highlighting lots of text in computer Reading helps."

The tool exists and examiners cannot see it
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Highlighting text (IELTS on computer)]`, but
highlighting broadly marks everything and therefore marks nothing, and consumes
mouse time inside a 60-minute paper that includes transfer time
`[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`. One highlight per answer,
on the evidence phrase. *(My analysis.)*

### M14. "There's a spellchecker in the computer Writing box."

Assume there is not. Nothing in any official description of the interface mentions
one; what is documented is a word counter
`[src: ielts.idp.com — How computer-delivered IELTS works]`, and spelling is
explicitly marked `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]`. Tier 2
warns specifically about typos in the computer format
`[src: ieltsliz.com — Computer Delivered IELTS: Pros & Cons]`. **[UNVERIFIED as an
absolute — the book should say "do not rely on a spellchecker; verify in the
official familiarisation test."]**
