## Verified facts

### Structure and mechanics

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| 3 sections / 3 long passages | `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]` |
| 40 questions | `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]` |
| 60 minutes, **including** transfer time | `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]` |
| Total text length **2,150–2,750 words** | `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]` |
| **No extra transfer time** (unlike Listening) — "Unlike the Listening test, no extra transfer time is given" | `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]` |
| Each correct answer = **1 mark**; final score reported as a band 1–9 in whole or half bands | `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]` |
| "Each correct item is awarded one mark. Band scores… are awarded to test takers on the basis of their raw scores." | `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers, p.7]` |
| Answers must be written on the answer sheet within the 60 minutes; markers do **not** read the question paper | `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF, Reading section]` |

### Where the texts come from, and what is in them

- Texts come from **"books, journals, magazines, newspapers and online resources, written
  for a non-specialist audience."** All topics are of **general interest to students at
  undergraduate or postgraduate level.**
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`
- Styles vary: **narrative, descriptive, or discursive/argumentative**, and **"At least one
  text contains detailed logical argument."**
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`
- **"Texts may also contain diagrams, graphs or illustrations."**
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`
- **"If texts use technical vocabulary, then a simple dictionary definition is provided."**
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`
  → i.e. a **glossary**. The official British Council lesson materials train candidates to
  check for one during the 20-second "survey" step: *"if there are specialist or technical
  words that non-specialists are not expected to know, they will be explained in a glossary
  at the end of the text."*
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Developing Reading Skills (lesson PDF)]`
- The Guide for teachers describes Academic Reading as **"Three long reading passages with
  tasks / Texts range from the descriptive and factual to the discursive and analytical /
  Includes non-verbal materials such as diagrams, graphs or illustrations / Texts are
  authentic (e.g. taken from books, journals and newspapers)."**
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers, p.4]`
- Item difficulty: **"The Academic Reading section has more items pitched at bands 5–8,
  whereas the General Training has more items pitched at bands 3–6."**
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers, p.4]`
- Academic and GT Reading scores are **not** interchangeable; GT needs more correct answers
  for the same band. `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers, p.4]`
  `[src: ielts.idp.com — IELTS Reading band scores and what they mean]`

### Does difficulty increase across the three passages?

**NOT STATED by any Tier 1 source I could find.** ielts.org's Reading format page, the
Guide for teachers, the Cambridge FAQs and the official sample-tasks PDF all describe the
three sections without ranking their difficulty.

- The official 2023 sample-tasks PDF labels its extracts "a Part 1 text", "a Part 2 text",
  "a Part 3 text", confirming that passages are ordered/numbered — but says nothing about
  relative difficulty. `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic Reading Sample Tasks (2023) PDF]`
- The only official difficulty statement is at **test level**, not passage level: Academic
  items are pitched at bands 5–8.
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers, p.4]`

`[UNVERIFIED]` — "Passage 3 is the hardest" is universal Tier 2/Tier 3 folklore but is not
an official claim. **Consequence for the book:** do not assert it as fact. Recommend a
timing plan that is *robust either way* (see §Timing strategies), and explicitly tell the
reader to check their own passage-by-passage accuracy in practice rather than assuming.

### Marking, guessing and answer rules

| Rule | Exact official wording / substance | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **No negative marking** | *"it is ok to guess when they are not sure of an answer since test takers are not penalised for wrong answers"* | `[src: ielts.org — News & Insights: How to prepare learners for matching headings task]` |
| **No penalty; blanks score zero** | *"one mark awarded for each correct answer and no penalty for incorrect responses"*; *"a blank answer will get no marks"* | `[src: ielts.idp.com — IELTS Reading band scores and what they mean]` |
| **Word limit is absolute** | *"Answers which are over the word limit will be marked as incorrect."* | `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]` |
| **Word limit (per-task-type wording)** | *"A word limit is given, for example, 'NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER'. You will lose the mark for writing more than the word limit."* | `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]` |
| **Hyphenated words** | *"Hyphenated words such as 'check-in' count as single words."* | `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]` |
| **Contractions** | *"Contracted words such as 'they're' will not be tested."* | `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]` |
| **Numbers** | *"Numbers can be written using figures (1, 2, etc.) or words (one, two, etc.)."* | `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format, Short-answer questions]` |
| **Spelling costs marks** | *"you will lose marks for incorrect spelling and grammar"* | `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]` |
| **Copy exactly** | *"Copy words accurately from the text. Spelling mistakes will mean that you will lose the mark for that question."* | `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]` |
| **Answers must be grammatical** | *"Make sure your answer is grammatically correct where you have to write your answer in words, e.g. short-answer questions, sentence completion, summary completion."* | `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]` |
| **All needed words are in the text** | *"All the words you will need in order to answer the questions will be given in the text."* | `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]` |
| **Never write two answers** | *"Don't write more than one answer when only one is required. Even if one of your answers is correct, you will not receive a mark."* | `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]` |
| **UK and US spellings** | *"Both UK and US spellings are accepted."* (stated in the **Listening** FAQ; for Reading the words are copied from the text so the question rarely arises) | `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF, Listening section]` |

**High-value derived rule for a band-6.5 candidate:** because *"all the words you will need
… will be given in the text"* and *"copy words accurately"*, **every completion answer is a
transcription task, not a writing task.** On computer, copy-paste from the passage removes
spelling risk entirely (see §Computer-delivered specifics). This alone recovers marks for a
candidate whose L1 spelling interference (French doubling, `-tion`/`-sion`) would otherwise
cost them.

## The authoritative question-type inventory: 11 or 14?

Both numbers are official, and the discrepancy is a naming artefact, not a disagreement:

- **11 task types**, because ielts.org's format page groups summary / note / table /
  flow-chart completion as a single "Question Type 9".
  `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format]`
  Same 11-type list at `[src: ielts.idp.com — IELTS Academic Reading test preparation – question types]`
- **14 named tasks**, because the official sample-tasks PDF enumerates the four completion
  formats separately: *"multiple-choice questions, identifying information, identifying the
  writer's views/claims, matching information, matching headings, matching features,
  matching sentence endings, sentence completion, summary completion, note completion,
  table completion, flow-chart completion, diagram label completion and short-answer
  questions."* `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic Reading Sample Tasks (2023) PDF, p.1]`

**Book should present 11 numbered types, flagging that type 9 has four surface forms and
two variations (with / without a word bank).** Official numbering (Type 1 … Type 11) is
worth keeping because Cambridge's own teaching materials are filed under it.

### Master order-following table — the single most strategically useful page

| # | Task type | Do questions/answers follow passage order? | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Multiple choice | **YES** — *"the answer to the first question will be before the answer to the second question, and so on"* | `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` |
| 2 | Identifying information (T/F/NG) | **YES** — *"NB (2) The answers are in the same order as the text."* | `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG (and Y/N/NG) questions, lesson PDF, p.2]` (ielts.org itself is silent) |
| 3 | Identifying writer's views/claims (Y/N/NG) | **YES** — same NB covers both task types; Cambridge's own worksheet tells students *"You do not need to start at the beginning again as the answers are in the text order."* | `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF]` · `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 2 & 3 Activity PDF, Worksheet 1 Ex.2]` |
| 4 | Matching information | **NO** — *"The order of the questions does not match the order of the information in the passage."* ielts.org is silent; structurally it cannot be ordered, since paragraphs may be skipped or reused | `[src: ielts.idp.com — How to Do the Matching Information Question in IELTS Reading]` · structural corroboration `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` |
| 5 | Matching headings | **NO (answers)** — *"NB The answers are NOT in the same order as the text."* See caveat below | `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Matching Headings lesson PDF, p.2]` |
| 6 | Matching features | **NO** — *"NB The answers are NOT in the same order as the text."* | `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Matching Features lesson PDF, p.2]` |
| 7 | Matching sentence endings | **YES** — *"The sentence beginnings are in the same order as the information in the text."* | `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` |
| 8 | Sentence completion | **YES** — *"The questions are in the same order as the information in the text."* | `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` |
| 9 | Summary / note / table / flow-chart completion | **NO (not guaranteed)** — *"The answers may not come in the same order as in the text. However, they will usually come from one part of the text rather than the whole text."* British Council words it more usefully: *"The answers tend to but do not always occur in the same order as in the text."* | `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` · `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Completion questions lesson PDF, p.2]` |
| 10 | Diagram label completion | **NO (not guaranteed)** — same wording as type 9; Cambridge adds *"the words will probably only come from one part of the text but … they do not necessarily occur in the order of the passage"* | `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` · `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 10 Activity PDF, teacher's notes step 4]` |
| 11 | Short-answer questions | **YES** — *"The answers come in the same order as the information in the text."* Cambridge softens this: *"Usually the answers will be in text order, although sometimes they are in slightly, but never totally, different order."* | `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` · `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 11 Activity PDF]` |

**Caveat that must be written carefully in the book — matching headings.** The British
Council NB says *the **answers** are not in the same order as the text*, meaning the Roman
numerals in the heading list are deliberately scrambled relative to the paragraphs. It does
**not** mean the questions jump around: the questions are one per paragraph, in paragraph
order (Q1 = Section A, Q2 = Section B …), as the official samples show.
`[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic Reading Sample Tasks (2023) PDF, Matching Headings]`
Strategically: you work through the paragraphs in order, but you cannot expect heading (i)
to belong to paragraph A.

**Cambridge's operational rule for ordered types:** *"Don't go back to the beginning of the
text for each question when you know from the task type that the answers will come in the
order of the information in the text."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF, Reading "Don't" list]`

### Type 1 — Multiple choice

**Appearance.** Either a question with four options A–D, or the first half of a sentence
with four possible endings. Sometimes a longer option list from which you choose **more
than one** answer (e.g. *"Choose TWO letters, A–G"*).
`[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` · `[src: ielts.org — Academic Reading Sample Tasks 2023 PDF]`

**Exact rules.**
- *"You should read the question carefully to check how many answers you need to choose."*
  `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
- Multi-answer versions carry their own mark per correct letter (the sample shows Questions
  1 and 2 answered by two letters from A–G).
  `[src: ielts.org — Academic Reading Sample Tasks 2023 PDF]`
- Tests *"detailed understanding of specific points or general understanding of the main
  points of the text."* `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`

**The trap.** The distractor options are built out of the passage's own vocabulary.
Cambridge's teacher notes are explicit: you must *"skim and scan for information relating to
the question **stem**, not the answer options, as these are often distractors (options which
are not correct)."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 1 (MCQ) Activity PDF, step 2]`

A second, subtler trap that Cambridge names directly: candidates try to pick the answer that
*sounds* grammatically right. *"Make sure students realise that this is not the case — all
answer options (A–D) are grammatically possible."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 1 (MCQ) Activity PDF, step 8]`

**Technique (Cambridge's own procedure, adapted).**
1. Underline the key words **in the stem only**. Physically cover the options A–D.
2. Skim/scan to locate the relevant part of the text (Cambridge allots ~30 seconds).
3. **Paraphrase/summarise that part of the text in your own words — before you look at the
   options.** This is the whole method.
4. Uncover A–D and choose the one that matches *your* summary.
5. If two options survive, work backwards: prove why the other is wrong (see Type 7 below
   for Cambridge's worked example of this move).
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 1 (MCQ) Activity PDF, steps 3–7]`

**The band-6 mistake.** Reading the four options first, then hunting the passage for the
words in them. This guarantees you find the distractor, because the distractor is the one
built from passage vocabulary. Band 7 reads the stem, answers it from the text in their own
words, and only then meets the options.

**Order:** YES — in passage order.

### Type 2 — Identifying information (True / False / Not Given)

**Appearance.** *"Do the following statements agree with the information given in Reading
Passage 1? … write TRUE if the statement agrees with the information / FALSE if the
statement contradicts the information / NOT GIVEN if there is no information on this."*
`[src: ielts.org — Academic Reading Sample Tasks 2023 PDF, TFNG sample]`

**Exact rules.**
- *"'False' means that the statement contradicts the information in the text. 'Not given'
  means that the statement neither agrees with nor contradicts the information in the text."*
  `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
- *"'False' means that the passage states the opposite of the statement in question."*
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF, p.2]`
- *"You must be careful not to use any information you already know about the topic of the
  text."* `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
- Tests *"your ability to recognise specific information given in the text."*
  `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
- It is about **facts / information**, and is *"used with more factual texts."*
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF, p.2]`

**The trap.** False vs Not Given. See the dedicated §TFNG vs YNNG decision procedure below —
this is the highest-value section in the whole Reading chapter.

**Technique — the official 7-step strategy (British Council, correct order `c d f b e a g`;
`c f d b e a g` also accepted).**
1. **c)** Read the statements; underline/highlight key words.
2. **d)** **Turn the statement into a Yes/No question.** *(This is the move most candidates
   have never been taught, and it is officially sanctioned.)*
3. **f)** Think of synonyms for the key words, or other ways they could be expressed.
4. **b)** From your skimming, identify which paragraph(s) are relevant.
5. **e)** Scan that part of the text for the key words or their synonyms.
6. **a)** Close-read around those words to locate the deciding information.
7. **g)** Convert your Yes/No answer to the required label — *for T/F/NG, Yes = TRUE,
   No = FALSE, and "can't tell" = NOT GIVEN.*
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF, Worksheet 3 Ex.1 + answer key]`

**The band-6 mistake.** Two of them, both fatal at 6.5→7:
1. Answering **FALSE** when the passage simply never addresses the point. British Council's
   deliberately trivial calibration exercise makes it unmissable: passage = *"The Wifi code
   is Dublin."* Statement 3 = *"Dublin is the capital of the Republic of Ireland."* Answer =
   **NOT GIVEN** — *"even if you know Dublin is the capital, based on the information given
   in the TEXT/PASSAGE, the correct answer is NOT GIVEN."*
   `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF, Worksheet 1 answer key]`
2. Spending too long proving a NOT GIVEN. Cambridge's tip list: *"If you're not sure of the
   answer, don't spend too long thinking about it. Choose an answer choice and move on."*
   `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 2 & 3 Activity PDF, Additional information]`

**Order:** YES.

### Type 3 — Identifying writer's views/claims (Yes / No / Not Given)

**Appearance.** *"Do the following statements agree with the views of the writer?"* or
*"Do the following statements agree with the claims of the writer in Sample Passage 8?"* —
answer YES / NO / NOT GIVEN.
`[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` · `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 2 & 3 Activity PDF, Sample Task]`

**Exact rules.**
- *"'No' means that the statement contradicts the writer's view or claim. 'Not given' means
  that the statement neither agrees with nor contradicts the writer's view or claim."*
  `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
- British Council is sharper: *"'No' means that the views or claims of the writer
  **explicitly** disagree with the statement, i.e. the writer somewhere expresses the view or
  makes a claim which is opposite to the one given in the question."*
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG (and Y/N/NG) lesson PDF, p.2]`
- Tests *"your ability to recognise opinions or ideas"*, and is *"often used with discursive
  or argumentative texts."*
  `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` · `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF]`
- Official rubric wording is: **YES** if the statement reflects the claims of the writer;
  **NO** if it contradicts them; **NOT GIVEN** if it is impossible to say what the writer
  thinks about this.
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 2 & 3 Activity PDF, Sample Task]`

**The trap.** The statement is *factually* supported by the passage but is not *the writer's
claim* — or the writer's view exists on a neighbouring but different proposition. Note the
NOT GIVEN wording differs meaningfully from T/F/NG: it is *"impossible to say **what the
writer thinks** about this"*, not "no information on this".

**Technique.** Identical strategy to T/F/NG — officially: *"NB (1) Both task types can be
answered using the SAME strategy."*
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF, p.2]`
Add one filter: hunt for **stance language** — *believed, it is thought, suggests, argues,
maintains, criticize, emphasizes, claims* — since Y/N/NG sits on discursive texts. Cambridge's
matching-features lesson teaches exactly this split: *"Findings of a study are the facts that
have been proved, expressed by language such as **the study shows, reveals, proves**. Opinions
… are the ideas that come up during a study but have no proof, expressed by language such as
**the researchers believe, argue, claim**."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 6 (Matching Features) Activity PDF, step 4]`

**The band-6 mistake.** Treating Y/N/NG as T/F/NG. If the statement is factually true of the
world *and* mentioned in the passage, but the writer never takes a position on it, the answer
is **NOT GIVEN**, not YES.

**Order:** YES.

### Type 4 — Matching information (to paragraphs)

**Appearance.** *"Sample Passage 7 has eight paragraphs labelled A–H. Which paragraph
contains the following information? Write the correct letter A–H … NB You may use any letter
more than once."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 4 Activity PDF, Sample Task]`

**Exact rules.**
- *"Not every paragraph (or section) may be used and some paragraphs (or sections) may be
  used more than once. When the paragraphs (or sections) may be used more than once, the
  instructions will say: 'You may use any letter more than once'."*
  `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
- Tests *"your ability to scan a text in order to find specific information. Unlike Task
  Type 5 (Matching headings), it focuses on **specific information rather than the main
  idea**. You may have to find: specific details, an example, reason, description,
  comparison, summary or explanation."*
  `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`

**The trap.** Word-level matching. Cambridge's own reflection exercise has students mark
this statement **Disagree**: *"If the exact same words appear in the text as in an
information point, then this must be the correct paragraph."* Official reasoning: *"more than
one paragraph may deal with the same general area as an information point so the words may
appear in more than one paragraph. **You need to look for the information not the words.**"*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 4 Activity PDF, Key to Exercise 3]`

Second trap: because paragraphs can be reused and skipped, the "cross it off and move on"
instinct from matching headings is wrong here.

**Technique — Cambridge's officially-endorsed method (note it is *paragraph-led*, not
question-led).**
1. Read the information points first and **paraphrase each one** — Cambridge builds an entire
   exercise around rewriting the six items before touching the passage (e.g. *"the relative
   merits of cars and public transport"* → *"the advantages of both trains and cars"*).
   `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 4 Activity PDF, Key to Exercise 2]`
2. **Read paragraph A, then look through the information points to see if any is in it.**
   Marked **Agree**: *"because you are looking for specific information, it is easier to read
   a paragraph and then look through the information points."*
3. **Do not re-read a paragraph repeatedly.** Marked **Disagree**: *"you will waste time…
   some paragraphs do not contain an information point. It is better to continue and then
   check your answers at the end."*
4. **If nothing matches quickly, move on.** Marked **Agree**.
5. **After every paragraph, re-scan all the information points — even ones you think you've
   matched.** Marked **Agree**: *"keep an open mind about your answers until you have read
   all the paragraphs. This will also help you 'learn' the information points."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 4 Activity PDF, Key to Exercise 3]`

**Tier 2, promoted (2 independent sources + consistent with the official technique above):**
do this task type **last** within its passage, because by then you already know the passage
from having answered the other question sets, and matching information is the slowest type.
`[src: ieltsliz.com — Matching Paragraph Information: IELTS Reading Exercise]` ·
`[src: ieltsadvantage.com — IELTS Reading: Matching Information to Paragraphs]`
Helps-not-hurts check: it costs nothing (no penalty, order-independent) and converts the
slowest type into the best-informed one. Passes.

**The band-6 mistake.** Working question-by-question and re-scanning the whole passage for
each one — 8 paragraphs × 6 questions = 48 scans instead of 8 reads.

**Order:** NO.

### Type 5 — Matching headings

**Appearance.** A list of headings in lower-case Roman numerals (i, ii, iii …) above the
questions; paragraphs/sections lettered A, B, C … One or more may be done for you as an
**Example**.
`[src: ielts.org — Academic Reading Sample Tasks 2023 PDF, Matching Headings]`

**Exact rules.**
- *"There will always be **more headings** than paragraphs or sections, so some headings will
  not be used. It is also possible that some paragraphs or sections may not be included in
  the task. One or more paragraphs or sections may already be matched with a heading as an
  example on the question paper. **No heading may be used more than once.**"*
  `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
- *"This task type is used with texts that contain paragraphs or sections with clearly
  defined themes."* `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Matching Headings lesson PDF, p.2]`
- Tests *"your ability to identify the general topic of a paragraph (or section) and to
  recognise the difference between the **main idea and a supporting idea**."*
  `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
- **It comes first.** ielts.org states: this task type *"will be the first set of questions in
  the section."*
  `[src: ielts.org — News & Insights: How to prepare learners for matching headings task]`
  → So "do matching headings first" is not a strategy choice; it is where the paper puts it.

**The trap.** Choosing a heading that matches **one sentence** inside the paragraph rather
than the paragraph's main idea. Official framing: distinguishing main from supporting ideas
*is* the construct being tested.
Second trap, named by Cambridge: starting from the headings and scanning for their key words.
*"Point out that if they start with the headings and scan for the key words in them, they will
be finding **specific information rather than the main idea**."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 5 Activity PDF, teacher's notes step 13]`

**Technique — the official 10-step strategy (British Council; correct order
`b d e h c g i j a f`).**
1. **b)** Survey the passage: title, sub-headings, diagrams.
2. **d)** Skim the paragraphs — first/topic sentences and final sentences — to get each
   paragraph's gist.
3. **e)** Read the headings, underlining key words.
4. **h)** **Eliminate any heading already used as an example.**
5. **c)** Try to match heading key words to the topic sentences.
6. **g)** If you can't find a match, read the **final** sentence of the paragraph.
7. **i)** If you still can't, read the whole paragraph.
8. **j)** Repeat for each paragraph/section.
9. **a)** Make final choices as you read more of the text.
10. **f)** Be careful transferring answers — write the **Roman numerals**, not the heading text.
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Matching Headings lesson PDF, Worksheet Q7 + answer key]`

Official supporting points:
- *"while topic sentences are very often the first sentence of the paragraph they can be
  elsewhere in the paragraph and sometimes further reading is required."*
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Matching Headings lesson PDF, teacher's notes Ex.4]`
- *"A heading is a short sentence or expression which explains the most important point in a
  text… The main point of a paragraph is usually found in the topic sentence, which may be
  the first or last sentence of the paragraph, or less commonly, in the middle."*
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 5 Activity PDF, Key to Classroom Activity]`
- ielts.org's own three-step public advice: analyse the headings and underline key words →
  identify topic sentences by skimming → match key words to topic sentences, expecting
  **synonyms** rather than identical wording. And: do **not** *"waste time reading the text
  carefully from beginning to end."*
  `[src: ielts.org — News & Insights: How to prepare learners for matching headings task]`

**The band-6 mistake.** Reading each paragraph in full before looking at the headings —
double work — or leaving the answer blank when torn between two headings. There is no
penalty; pick one.
`[src: ielts.org — News & Insights: How to prepare learners for matching headings task]`

**Order:** questions run in paragraph order; **answers (the numerals) do not** — the list is
scrambled. `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Matching Headings lesson PDF, p.2]`

### Type 6 — Matching features

**Appearance.** A set of statements/information to be matched to a lettered list of features
from the text — e.g. *"Look at the following items (Questions 7–10) and the list of groups
below. Match each item with the group which first invented or used them. Write the correct
letter A–E … NB You may use any letter more than once."*
`[src: ielts.org — Academic Reading Sample Tasks 2023 PDF, Matching Features]`

**Exact rules.**
- *"It is possible that some options will not be used, and that others may be used more than
  once. When it is possible to use any option more than once, the instructions will say:
  'You may use any option more than once'."*
  `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
- Tests *"your ability to recognise relationships and connections between facts in the text
  and your ability to recognise opinions and theories. You need to be able to skim and scan
  the text to find the information quickly so that you can then read that part more carefully
  for detail."* `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
- *"It may be used both with factual information, as well as opinion-based discursive texts."*
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Matching Features lesson PDF, p.2]`

**The trap.** Attribution. In a passage that discusses three researchers/studies/theories,
every option is genuinely present in the text; the question is *whose* claim it is. Cambridge
builds a whole worksheet on separating **findings** (*shows, reveals, proves*) from
**opinions** (*believe, argue, claim*).
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 6 Activity PDF, step 4]`

**Technique — the official 5-step strategy (British Council; correct order `b c e d a`).**
1. **b)** From your skimming, **note down which paragraphs/parts of the text are relevant to
   each of the lettered options A–C**. (i.e. build a map of *where each feature lives* before
   you look at the statements.)
2. **c)** Read the questions and underline/highlight key words.
3. **e)** Think of synonyms of these or other ways they may be expressed.
4. **d)** Scan the relevant parts of the text and underline key words or synonyms from the
   questions.
5. **a)** Close-read around those words to find the answer.
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Matching Features lesson PDF, Worksheet Ex.5 + answer key]`

Worked example from the same lesson: statement *"guarding areas of land"* → the paraphrase
in paragraph 1 is *"protecting / territory / ground"* → feature A (reptilian brain).
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Matching Features lesson PDF, Ex.6]`

**The band-6 mistake.** Scanning for the proper noun and taking the first sentence you find
near it. The feature name recurs; the *attribution* of the specific claim does not.

**Order:** NO — *"NB The answers are NOT in the same order as the text."*
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Matching Features lesson PDF, p.2]`

### Type 7 — Matching sentence endings

**Appearance.** *"Complete each sentence with the correct ending, A–F, below. Write the
correct letter, A–F, in boxes 1–3 on your answer sheet."*
`[src: ielts.org — Academic Reading Sample Tasks 2023 PDF, Matching Sentence Endings]`

**Exact rules.**
- *"There will be more sentence endings than beginnings, so you will not use all of them."*
  `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
- Tests *"your ability to understand the main ideas in the text."*
  `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
- Cambridge's variant instruction shows endings can be reusable: *"Complete each sentence
  with the correct ending A–J from the box below … NB You may use any letter more than once."*
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 7 Activity PDF]`

**The trap.** Grammatical fit. Several endings will slot grammatically onto the beginning.
Cambridge: *"there are often distractors which have slightly different meanings"*, and you
must find the ending *"which contains **exactly the same information** as in the text, even if
the words used are different."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 7 Activity PDF, step 5]`

Cambridge's own worked elimination for Q10 is the model to reproduce in the book:
> Options A and B are *"also logical, grammatically correct choices. However with detailed
> reading and understanding they can be disproved as: A — the report includes a **review** not
> **reviews**; B — it is **UCSF** not **AMA** who propose smoke-free public places."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 7 Activity PDF, step 10]`
→ Two classic distractor mechanisms in one example: **number** (singular/plural switched) and
**agent** (the right claim attributed to the wrong body).

**Technique.**
1. Underline key words in the **beginnings only** — cover the endings A–J.
2. Scan the text for those key words; the information *"may be located in more than one part
   of the text."*
3. Read that part and decide what the sentence must say — **before** looking at the endings.
4. Now read the endings and pick the one containing the same information.
5. Highlight the phrase in the text that is the precise paraphrase of your chosen ending, so
   you can prove it. *"Remember, the information is always stated specifically, and is not
   inferred, in the text."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 7 Activity PDF, steps 3–8]`

**The band-6 mistake.** Choosing the ending that reads most naturally. Every wrong ending
reads naturally — that is the design.

**Order:** YES — the sentence **beginnings** are in text order. `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`

### Type 8 — Sentence completion

**Appearance.** *"Complete the sentences below. Choose ONE WORD ONLY from the passage for each
answer."*
`[src: ielts.org — Academic Reading Sample Tasks 2023 PDF, Sentence Completion]`

**Exact rules.**
- *"you have to fill in a gap in each sentence by choosing words from the text."*
- *"You should read the instructions very carefully as the number of words or numbers you may
  use to fill the gaps **can change**."*
- Word limit absolute; contractions not tested; hyphenated words = one word.
- Tests *"your ability to find detail/specific information in a text."*
`[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`

**The trap.** The word limit changes between question sets *within the same paper*
("ONE WORD ONLY" for one set, "NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS" for another). Reading the previous
set's rubric from memory loses marks that you understood perfectly.

**Technique.**
1. Read the rubric. Write the limit at the top of the set.
2. Predict the **grammar** of each gap before scanning — noun? verb? adjective? singular or
   plural? Officially endorsed: *"you will need to think about the type of word(s) that will
   fit into a gap (for example, whether a noun is needed, or a verb, etc.)."*
   `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format, Type 9]`
3. Scan in order (the set is ordered).
4. **Copy the word exactly.** *"Copy words accurately from the text."*
   `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]`
5. Re-read the completed sentence. *"Make sure your answer is grammatically correct."*
   `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]`

**The band-6 mistake — and this is the L1-specific one for this reader.** Adding a word the
gap did not need. French-influenced writers insert an article (*the society*, *the nature*)
or pluralise an uncountable (*informations*, *researches*, *equipments*). In sentence
completion that is not a style error — it is a **zero**: the word wasn't in the text, or the
answer went over the limit. On the official sample, the answers are bare single words:
*theropods, collarbones, reptile, similarities, claw.*
`[src: ielts.org — Academic Reading Sample Tasks 2023 PDF, Sentence Completion answers]`

**Order:** YES.

### Type 9 — Summary / note / table / flow-chart completion

**Appearance.** Four surface forms, named in the rubric:
- **summary** — continuous text;
- **notes** — several notes / bullets;
- **table** — cells left empty;
- **flow chart** — *"a series of boxes or steps linked by arrows to show the order of
  events."*
`[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`

Two **variations**, and the rules differ:
- **Variation 1 — words from the text.** *"Complete the summary below. Choose NO MORE THAN
  TWO WORDS from the passage for each answer."*
- **Variation 2 — from a word bank.** *"Complete the summary using the list of words, A–G,
  below. Write the correct letter, A–G."*
`[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` · `[src: ielts.org — Academic Reading Sample Tasks 2023 PDF]`

**Exact rules.**
- *"the summary is not normally of the whole text"* — it covers **one part**.
- Word limit absolute (variation 1); hyphenated = one word; contractions not tested.
- Tests *"your ability to understand details and/or the main ideas of a part of the text."*
`[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
- British Council adds a fact worth its weight in marks: *"Where a list of answers is
  provided, they most frequently consist of a **single word**."*
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Completion questions lesson PDF, p.2]`

**Rules that differ between the two variations — write this explicitly in the book:**

| | Variation 1 (from the passage) | Variation 2 (word bank A–G) |
|---|---|---|
| What you write | The word(s), spelled exactly as in the text | A **letter** |
| Word limit | Applies, absolutely | Does not apply — you write one letter |
| Spelling risk | High (mark lost for misspelling) | **Zero** |
| Words appear in the passage? | Yes, verbatim | **No — the bank words are paraphrases** and often do not appear in the text at all |
| Main skill | Scan + transcribe | Paraphrase recognition + grammatical fit |

Evidence for the paraphrase point: the official word-bank sample's answers are
*material / fundamental / complex / easy* against a passage that uses *transformed our
material existence*, *pride of place*, *tool of extraordinary sophistication*, *ingenious
simplicity* — none of the four option words is lifted from the passage.
`[src: ielts.org — Academic Reading Sample Tasks 2023 PDF, Summary Completion: selecting from a list of words or phrases]`

**The trap.** Two:
1. **Variation 2 grammar decoys.** The bank contains adjectives that all fit the slot
   grammatically (*difficult, complex, original, admired, material, easy, fundamental*).
   Grammar cannot narrow it; only meaning can.
2. **Variation 1 over-writing.** Writing three words where the rubric says ONE WORD ONLY.

**Technique — the official British Council pre-reading routine (do this BEFORE reading the
text).**
For each gap, answer:
- a) What type of completion question is this?
- b) What guidance do the instructions give? (write the word limit down)
- c) What clues / key words show what's missing?
- d) What would you look for in the text?
- e) **Predict possible answers for each gap.**
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Completion questions lesson PDF, Worksheets 1–2a]`

Then check every answer three ways: *"contextually, grammatically and adherence to
instructions."*
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Completion questions lesson PDF, teacher's notes]`

For **tables specifically**, Cambridge's model is: *"The key words to scan for are the
**column headings**."* And predict the answer *class* per gap — e.g. *"question 10 requires a
time of year e.g. a month or a season; question 11 needs a number or numbers (written either
as figures or words)."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 9 Activity PDF, Key to Worksheet 3–4]`

Also from that key, two marking facts worth reproducing: *"Words in brackets are optional —
they are correct, but not necessary"* and *"Alternative answers are separated by a single
slash (/)"* — e.g. `two to five / 2-5`, `South African tunnelling / tunneling`.
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 9 Activity PDF, Key to Worksheet 5]`
→ i.e. **both UK and US spellings are accepted in the mark scheme**, and numerals or words
both count.

**The band-6 mistake.** Treating variation 2 like variation 1 — hunting the passage for the
bank words. They are not there.

**Order:** NO (not guaranteed) — but *"they will usually come from one part of the text."*
That single sentence is the strategy: **find the region first, then work the gaps locally.**
`[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`

### Type 10 — Diagram label completion

**Appearance.** *"Label the diagram below. Choose NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS from the passage
for each answer."* The diagram *"may be a type of machine, part of a building or of other
information in the text that can be shown through pictures."*
`[src: ielts.org — Academic Reading Sample Tasks 2023 PDF, Diagram Label Completion]` ·
`[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`

**Exact rules.** Word limit absolute; hyphenated = one word; contractions not tested. Tests
*"your ability to understand a detailed description in the text, and then relate that
description to information given in a diagram."*
`[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`

**The trap.** Position and orientation language. The label you need is usually distinguished
from a decoy by **where** it sits, not what it is.

**Technique — Cambridge's own procedure.**
1. Study the diagram first: what does it show? What kind of information is missing (a name?
   a material? a process step?)? What are the diagram's own key words?
2. *"the words will probably only come from one part of the text but … they do not
   necessarily occur in the order of the passage."* → find the region, then work locally.
3. *"students should pay particular attention to expressions relating to **location and
   position** as these are likely to be the ones relating to the diagram, along with the key
   words."*
4. Scan for the diagram's key words; skim around each hit to check relevance. In the official
   worked example, the key word *tunnel* appears in paragraph 1 but is irrelevant; it appears
   several times in paragraph 2, which is the right region.
5. *"Detailed reading should only happen when students are sure they have found the relevant
   section of the text."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 10 Activity PDF, teacher's notes steps 1–6 + Key to Worksheet 4]`

**The band-6 mistake.** Reading the passage linearly looking for the labels. The labels are
clustered in one region; find the region, then read densely.

**Order:** NO (not guaranteed).

### Type 11 — Short-answer questions

**Appearance.** Direct questions about factual details: *"Answer the questions below using
NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS for each answer."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 11 Activity PDF, Student's Worksheet]`

**Exact rules.**
- *"Answers must be taken from words in the text."*
- Word limit absolute. *"Numbers can be written using figures (1, 2, etc.) or words
  (one, two, etc.)."* Contractions not tested; hyphenated = one word.
- Tests *"your ability to find and understand specific information in the text."*
`[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`

**The trap.** Over-answering. Cambridge's teacher note for its own sample: *"Ensure students
have only chosen 3 one-word answers out of the possible list of answers in Q2."* The question
asks for *"the three broad areas"* and the answer is exactly `social, economic,
environmental` — three words, no connectors.
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 11 Activity PDF, Additional information + answer key]`

Second trap: order is *usually* but not *always* strict. Cambridge admits it directly:
*"The answer to Q3 appears before the answer to Q2 in this sample. Usually the answers will
be in text order, although **sometimes they are in slightly, but never totally, different
order**."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 11 Activity PDF, Additional information]`
→ Note this contradicts nothing on ielts.org (which says answers *come* in text order); it
refines it. Both should be reported.

**Technique.**
1. Read the rubric; note the word limit.
2. Underline key words in each question.
3. Scan for the corresponding section and write the **paragraph number** next to the question
   before answering anything.
4. Skim only that paragraph to find the answer.
5. *"If they don't find the answer quickly, skim read **around** the relevant section, and
   keep the other questions in mind at all times."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 11 Activity PDF, teacher's notes step 3]`

**The band-6 mistake.** Answering in a full sentence, or adding an article/preposition
("in the year 1946" instead of "1946"). The extra words either break the limit or introduce a
grammar error, and both cost the mark.

**Order:** YES (with the "slightly different" caveat above).

## TFNG vs YNNG decision procedure

This is the highest-value item in the Reading chapter. Everything below is anchored to
Tier 1 wording.

### The precise difference

|  | **True / False / Not Given** (Type 2) | **Yes / No / Not Given** (Type 3) |
|---|---|---|
| Rubric question | *"Do the following statements agree with **the information** in the text?"* | *"Do the following statements agree with **the views/claims of the writer**?"* |
| Object of judgement | **Facts / information stated in the text** | **The writer's opinions, views and claims** |
| Positive label | TRUE = statement agrees with the information | YES = statement reflects/agrees with the writer's claim |
| Negative label | FALSE = statement **contradicts the information**; the passage *"states the opposite"* | NO = the writer **explicitly** expresses an opposite view or claim |
| Third label | NOT GIVEN = the statement *"neither agrees with nor contradicts the information"* — **"there is no information on this"** | NOT GIVEN = *"it is impossible to say **what the writer thinks** about this"* |
| Text type | *"used with more factual texts"* | *"often used with discursive or argumentative texts"* |
| Skill tested | *"recognise specific information given in the text"* | *"recognise opinions or ideas"* |
| Answers in text order? | YES | YES |

Sources for the rows above: `[src: ielts.org — IELTS Academic: Reading test format, Types 2 and 3]` ·
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG (and Y/N/NG) questions lesson PDF, p.2]` ·
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 2 & 3 Activity PDF, Sample Task rubric]`

**The practical difference, stated in one line for the reader:**
> T/F/NG asks *"Is this so, according to the text?"*
> Y/N/NG asks *"Does the writer think this?"*
> A statement can be factually present in the passage and still be **NOT GIVEN** in Y/N/NG,
> because the writer never took a position on it.

**Instruction-reading rule, from Cambridge:** *"Always read the instructions carefully to see
whether you should be answering Yes, No and Not given or True, False and Not given."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 2 & 3 Activity PDF, Worksheet 2]`

### Cambridge's three-question test (works for both task types)

Cambridge frames it as a comparison of statement A (the question) with sentence B (the
relevant text):

1. *"Does sentence B have the same meaning as sentence A?"* → **Yes / True**
2. *"Does sentence B contain some information that **contradicts** sentence A?"* → **No / False**
3. *"Is the main point of the two sentences **completely different**?"* → **Not given**

`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 2 & 3 Activity PDF, Student's Worksheet 2]`

### The operational decision procedure (assembled from Tier 1 sources)

```
STEP 0  Read the rubric. T/F/NG or Y/N/NG? Fact or writer's view?
STEP 1  Underline the key words in the statement.
STEP 2  Turn the statement into a YES/NO QUESTION.            [British Council step d]
STEP 3  Generate synonyms / paraphrases for the key words.    [British Council step f]
STEP 4  From your skim, decide which paragraph is relevant.   [British Council step b]
        (Answers are in text order — start from where the last answer was.)
STEP 5  Scan that region for the key words or their synonyms. [British Council step e]
STEP 6  Close-read the sentence, and the one before and after.[British Council step a]

STEP 7  DECIDE:
        Can I put my finger on a sentence that ANSWERS my yes/no question?
          ├── NO  → NOT GIVEN. Stop. Do not reason further.
          └── YES →
               Does that sentence answer YES to my question?  → TRUE / YES
               Does that sentence answer NO  to my question?  → FALSE / NO

STEP 8  Convert to the required label.                        [British Council step g]
        T/F/NG: Yes = TRUE, No = FALSE, can't tell = NOT GIVEN
        Y/N/NG: Yes = YES,  No = NO,    can't tell = NOT GIVEN
```
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG (and Y/N/NG) questions lesson PDF, Worksheet 3 Ex.1 + answer key (order c d f b e a g)]`

**The single discriminating question at STEP 7** — phrase it to the reader exactly like this:

> **FALSE requires a contradiction you can point at. NOT GIVEN is the absence of a sentence.**
> If you are constructing an argument ("well, it probably isn't, because…"), you are not
> reading — you are inferring. The answer is NOT GIVEN.

Corroborating Tier 1: *"The main difference between 'False' or 'No' and 'Not Given', is that
'False' and 'No' **can be proven** (that is, there is evidence in the article)."*
`[src: ielts.idp.com — IELTS Reading test: Examples of how to use 'not given']`
Corroborating Tier 2 (IELTS Simon): choose false only *"if the information in the passage
directly contradicts the question statement; in other words, you need to be able to show
that a different answer would be true"*; choose not given *"only when there is no
information, or not enough information."*
`[src: ielts-simon.com — IELTS Reading: false or not given? (via search index; page itself now 404s)]`
`[UNVERIFIED — page not directly retrievable at time of research; treat as Tier 2 corroboration only, do not quote in the book]`

### Worked logic — official examples, fully reasoned

**(A) The calibration exercise — British Council's deliberately trivial passage.**
Passage: *"The Wifi code is Dublin."*

| Statement | Answer | Official reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| London is the Wifi code. | **FALSE** | *"the Wifi code is Dublin, NOT London (direct contradiction)"* |
| The Wifi code has the letter 'b' in it. | **TRUE** | *"the third letter of the code is the letter 'b'"* — note: TRUE is derived from the *form* of the word given, not from a restatement |
| Dublin is the capital of the Republic of Ireland. | **NOT GIVEN** | *"the passage does not mention whether Dublin is the capital… **even if you know Dublin is the capital**, based on the information given in the TEXT/PASSAGE, the correct answer is NOT GIVEN"* |

`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF, Worksheet 1 answer key]`

**(B) Qualifier trap — official, and exactly what a band-6.5 candidate gets wrong.**
Text: *"**Numerous** creatures, humans included, are largely diurnal."*
Statement: (about **most** species)
Answer: **NOT GIVEN** — *"the text says 'numerous' which might refer to most species, but
also might not; **we don't have enough information to determine how many species** are being
talked about."*
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF, Worksheet 3 answer key Q3]`

> **Rule extracted:** a vague quantifier in the text (*numerous, many, several, a number of*)
> against a precise quantifier in the statement (*most, the majority, half*) is **NOT GIVEN**,
> not FALSE. The text has not made the claim the statement makes, and it has not denied it
> either.

**(C) Definitional contradiction.**
Text: circadian rhythm = *"the complete cycle our bodies are naturally geared to undergo
within the passage of a **twenty-four hour** day."*
Statement: (a multi-day cycle)
Answer: **FALSE** — *"it's a 24-hour cycle which means ONE day."*
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF, Worksheet 3 answer key Q4]`

**(D) Lexical contradiction via a word you must actually know.**
Text: *"This is a **benign** variation within circadian rhythms known as a chronotype."*
Statement: (that it is unhealthy)
Answer: **TRUE** for the "not unhealthy" reading — *"'benign' means harmless which means that
it's not unhealthy."*
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF, Worksheet 3 answer key Q5]`
> **Rule extracted:** some T/F/NG decisions rest on a single lexical item. Vocabulary is not
> a separate skill from reading here — it *is* the answer.

**(E) The official Marie Curie set (T/F/NG) — the three canonical shapes.**
Passage facts: awarded the 1903 Physics prize *with her husband Pierre and Henri Becquerel*;
*"then **sole winner** of the 1911 Nobel Prize for Chemistry"*; won a gold medal at 16 for a
*"prodigious memory"*; financed her sister Bronia's studies *"on the understanding that Bronia
would, in turn, later help her to get an education"*; *"In 1891 this promise was fulfilled and
Marie went to Paris."*

| Statement | Answer | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| *"Marie Curie's husband was a joint winner of **both** Marie's Nobel Prizes."* | **FALSE** | The text says *sole winner* in 1911 — a direct contradiction of *both*. The word doing the work is the quantifier **both**. |
| *"Marie became interested in **science** when she was a child."* | **NOT GIVEN** | Her childhood is described (prodigious memory, gold medal) but *"no mention of science interest during childhood"*. The topic is present; the proposition is not. |
| *"Marie was able to attend the Sorbonne because of her sister's financial contribution."* | **TRUE** | The reciprocal promise + *"this promise was fulfilled and Marie went to Paris"* — a multi-sentence inference **that the text licenses**. |

`[src: ielts.org — Academic Reading Sample Tasks 2023 PDF, TFNG sample + answers]` ·
`[src: ielts.idp.com — IELTS Reading test: true, false and not given explained]`

> **Rule extracted from the contrast between rows 2 and 3:** connecting two sentences the
> writer has explicitly linked (*promise → promise fulfilled*) is **reading**, and yields
> TRUE. Filling a gap the writer left (*she was clever as a child → she must have liked
> science*) is **inference**, and yields NOT GIVEN. The line is: did the writer supply the
> link, or did you?

**(F) The official Y/N/NG set (writer's claims) — where Y/N/NG differs.**
Passage: a text on smoking risks. Statements and official answers:

| Statement | Answer | Official reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| *"Thirty per cent of deaths in the United States are caused by smoking-related diseases."* | **NO** | *"the text talks about percentages of deaths **from cancer** whereas in the statement it talks about deaths **in general**"* — a scope contradiction, not a numerical one. The number 30 matches; the denominator does not. |
| *"If one partner in a marriage smokes, the other is likely to take up smoking."* | **NOT GIVEN** | *"the text talks about the effects on the partner of **passive smoking** but doesn't mention if the partners tend to start smoking themselves"* — adjacent topic, different proposition. |
| *"Teenagers whose parents smoke are at risk of getting lung cancer at some time during their lives."* | **YES** | *"the text states that lung cancer can be attributed to high levels of exposure to second-hand tobacco smoke during childhood and adolescence — in other words, children and teenagers breathing their parents' smoke."* Pure paraphrase. |
| *"Opponents of smoking financed the UCSF study."* | **NOT GIVEN** | *"the text states that the **American Medical Association** is an opponent of smoking but doesn't say whether **UCSF** is or not."* The passage mentions the AMA (publisher of the journal) and the UCSF (author of the study); it never says who **funded** it. |

`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 2 & 3 Activity PDF, Key to Sample Task]`

> **Three named NG mechanisms extracted from (F), reusable as drill categories:**
> 1. **Adjacent-proposition NG** — the passage discusses the same actors and topic but a
>    different claim (rows 2 and 4).
> 2. **Scope NO/FALSE** — the figure is right but attached to a narrower/wider set
>    (row 1: deaths *from cancer* ≠ deaths *in general*).
> 3. **Legitimate paraphrase YES/TRUE** — different words, identical proposition (row 3).

### Qualifier / quantifier trap mechanics

Not stated as a rule in any single Tier 1 source, but **derivable directly from the official
worked answers above** (B, E-row-1, F-row-1) and therefore promotable:

| Statement says | Text says | Answer | Why (anchored to an official example) |
|---|---|---|---|
| *most / the majority* | *numerous, many, several* | **NOT GIVEN** | B — vague quantifier gives no basis to confirm or deny a precise one |
| *both / all / every* | one instance is explicitly excluded (*sole winner*) | **FALSE / NO** | E row 1 — an explicit exclusion is a contradiction |
| deaths **in general** | deaths **from cancer** | **NO / FALSE** | F row 1 — scope mismatch is a contradiction, not an absence |
| *only a few* (a proportion) | no proportion stated at all | **NOT GIVEN** | *"there is no mention of the proportion of the children"* `[src: ielts.idp.com — Examples of how to use 'not given']` |
| *quickly / rapidly* (a rate) | the event is described but not its speed | **NOT GIVEN** | *"you are unable to find anything about the speed of the uptake"* `[src: ielts.idp.com — Examples of how to use 'not given']` |
| a comparison (*more beneficial than*) | one side described, no comparison made | **NOT GIVEN** | *"unable to determine whether it is now beneficial or harmful"* `[src: ielts.idp.com — Examples of how to use 'not given']` |
| a time claim (*before they attended school*) | the fact stated, the timing not | **NOT GIVEN** | *"the article doesn't mention **when** they had a more positive attitude"* `[src: ielts.idp.com — Examples of how to use 'not given']` |

**The generalisation to teach:** every qualifier in the statement — quantity, proportion,
rate, time, comparison, exclusivity — is a **separate claim that must itself be present in
the text**. Official phrasing of this principle: all statement information must appear in the
passage, *including* qualifiers such as *"only a few"* regarding proportions.
`[src: ielts.idp.com — IELTS Reading test: Examples of how to use 'not given']`

> **Warning about the popular shortcut.** Some Tier 3 sources teach "if the statement contains
> *all/never/only*, mark it False." That is wrong and score-lowering — see MYTHS. The official
> examples show a strong qualifier producing FALSE (E row 1) *and* NOT GIVEN (B) depending on
> whether the text addresses the qualifier at all.

## Timing strategies

### The official position (Tier 1) — settles the debate

> *"You have 60 minutes to read three texts and answer 40 questions. **You should spend about
> 20 minutes on each text.** Make sure that you do not waste time worrying about questions you
> can't answer — use your time sensibly to read what you can and answer as many questions as
> possible."*
> `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF, Reading section]`

Also official:
- *"Keep an eye on the time and take care not to spend too much time on any one text or
  question."*
- *"**Start at the beginning of the test and work through it.** If you cannot do a particular
  question, leave it and go on to the next. You can then return to that question later, if you
  have time. **Put a mark next to this question** on the question paper so that you can find it
  again quickly."*
- *"Don't forget that you must write your answers on your answer sheet. You will NOT be given
  extra time to do this at the end of the test."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF, Reading Do/Don't lists]`
- *"Answer the questions and transfer their answers to the answer sheet **within the time
  allowed**."* `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers, Tips from teachers]`

### Approach 1 — flat 20/20/20 (Tier 1 default; Tier 2 majority)

Officially recommended, and endorsed by at least three independent Tier 2 experts:
- IELTS Liz: *"It is recommended that you spend 20 minutes on each passage."*
  `[src: ieltsliz.com — IELTS Reading FAQ]`
- Magoosh: divide the three sections equally at 20 minutes each, since *"each question is
  worth the same amount of points (1)."* `[src: magoosh.com — 10 IELTS Reading Tips]`
- IELTS Advantage: finish each section in 20 minutes, of which ~16–17 minutes reading and
  answering and 3–4 minutes transferring/checking.
  `[src: ieltsadvantage.com — How to Complete IELTS Reading in Under an Hour]`

**Verdict: this is the default the book should teach.** It is Tier 1, it is arithmetically
honest (1 mark ≈ 1.5 minutes), and it needs no assumption about which passage is hardest.

### Approach 2 — graded allocation (Tier 2, ≥2 sources)

15–17 / 19–20 / 23–25 minutes, banking time for the last passage.
`[src: e2language.com — How to manage your time in IELTS Reading]` ·
`[src: ieltsadvantage.com — How to Complete IELTS Reading in Under an Hour]`

**Rests on the "passage 3 is hardest" assumption, which Tier 1 does not confirm** (see
§Verified facts). Present it as a *personalisation* the reader earns from their own timed
data, not as a given. Concretely: after three timed practice tests, if their Passage 1
accuracy is ≥90% and Passage 3 accuracy is <70%, shift to the graded plan. Otherwise stay
flat.

### Approach 3 — "question-type-first" / cherry-picking across passages

Doing all the T/F/NG sets across the paper first, or skipping to easier types.
**Not recommended, and contradicted by Tier 1**: *"Start at the beginning of the test and
work through it."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]`
Helps-not-hurts filter: on computer-delivered IELTS the passage and its questions are
displayed together, so jumping between passages costs navigation time and destroys the
"you already know this passage" advantage that makes matching-information cheap. **Route to
MYTHS.**

Within a *single* passage, however, reordering the question sets is legitimate — matching
headings comes first by design `[src: ielts.org — News & Insights: matching headings]`, and
doing matching information last within its passage is Tier 2-supported (see Type 4).

### The hard stop, and the last 3 minutes

- Set a per-question ceiling (~90 seconds) and a per-passage hard stop. Official backing:
  *"take care not to spend too much time on any one text or question"* and *"If you're not
  sure of the answer, don't spend too long thinking about it. Choose an answer choice and
  move on."*
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]` ·
  `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 2 & 3 Activity PDF]`
- Reserve the final ~3 minutes to fill **every** blank. No penalty for wrong answers; a blank
  scores zero. `[src: ielts.idp.com — IELTS Reading band scores and what they mean]`

**Recommended budget for this reader (target 30/40):**

| Passage | Target | Cumulative | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 19 min | 19 | flat 20 minus 1 banked |
| 2 | 19 min | 38 | |
| 3 | 19 min | 57 | |
| Sweep for blanks + flagged | 3 min | 60 | never leave a gap |

## Strategies found

### The three official reading modes (learn the names — the official lessons use them)

| Mode | Official definition | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Surveying** | *"Looking at a text very quickly before reading to get an idea of lay-out: title, pictures, graphs, headings, glossary etc."* | 20 seconds, before anything else |
| **Skimming** | *"Reading a text very quickly to get the general understanding and to get an idea of the organisation of the text."* | 1–3 minutes: topic sentence + final sentence of each paragraph |
| **Scanning** | *"Reading a text with the specific purpose of extracting information such as numbers, facts, words etc."* | Per question, after you know where to look |

`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Developing Reading Skills lesson PDF, Worksheet Ex.2 (answers A3, B1, C2)]`

Add a fourth, which the official materials call **close reading**: *"in order to complete the
table, they must read the information around, close-reading the critical key words … to find
the answers."*
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Developing Reading Skills lesson PDF, Ex.10]`

Official timings from the British Council lesson sequence, useful as drill parameters:
survey = **20 seconds**; skim (topic + final sentences) = **60 seconds** for a ¾-page text
(3 minutes for a 2-page text); scan a single paragraph for key words = **45 seconds**.
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Developing Reading Skills lesson PDF]` ·
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF, Ex.2]`

**Survey checklist (official, from the 20-second exercise):** Does the text have a title?
sub-headings? diagrams or pictures? **a glossary?** How long is it?
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Developing Reading Skills lesson PDF, Ex.4]` ·
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Matching Features lesson PDF, Ex.2]`

### The paraphrase principle — officially the construct being tested

> *"Explain that the Academic Reading paper tests their knowledge and understanding of
> **synonyms and paraphrasing** as the language used in the questions is not the same as in
> the text."*
> `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 6 Activity PDF, step 7]`

Official practice design worth reproducing verbatim as an exercise format: take a sentence
from the text and rewrite it *"changing as many words and structures as possible, but keeping
the meaning the same."* Cambridge's own model:
> Text: *"In recent times, scientific research has been providing evidence that years of
> cigarette smoking vastly increases the risk of developing fatal medical conditions."*
> Rewrite: *"Due to recent scientific research, we have now got much more proof that if you
> smoke for a long time, you are much more likely to die from smoking-related health
> problems."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 6 Activity PDF, step 7]`

Cambridge's T/F/NG worksheet runs the same drill in reverse — paraphrase the **statement**
before scanning:
> Statement: *"Thirty per cent of deaths in the United States are caused by smoking-related
> diseases."*
> Paraphrase: *"Smoking is responsible for just under a third of all deaths in the US."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 2 & 3 Activity PDF, Key to Worksheet 1 Step 3]`

### Keyword anchoring: unchangeable vs changeable keywords

Officially framed as: *"Distinguish between unchangeable keywords (names, numbers, dates) and
changeable ones (synonyms, rephrased terms)."*
`[src: ielts.idp.com — How to Do the Matching Information Question in IELTS Reading]`

- **Unchangeable anchors** — proper nouns, dates, figures, capitalised terms, technical terms
  defined in the glossary. These survive paraphrasing and are your fastest route in. Tier 2
  agrees: *"Try to find names, place names and numbers in the questions. These are often
  easier to find in the text."*
  `[src: ieltsadvantage.com — IELTS Reading: Matching Information to Paragraphs]`
- **Changeable keywords** — every content word that can be swapped for a synonym. For these,
  do NOT scan for the word. Generate 2–3 paraphrases first, then scan for the *idea*.
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF, strategy step f]`

**Paraphrase-mapping drill (built from official material, recommended for the book):**
during review of every practice passage, write a two-column log — *question wording* →
*text wording*. Cambridge's own key does exactly this (statement A vs sentence B pairs).
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 2 & 3 Activity PDF, Key to Worksheet 1]`
This feeds directly into `progress/vocab-bank.md`.

### Other official micro-rules worth a box in the chapter

- *"Look carefully at the title of the text and any subtitles and illustrations it may have.
  You can get a quick idea of what the text is about from these."*
- *"Look at the dictionary definition, if one is provided, to help you understand unfamiliar
  words."*
- *"Pay attention to any examples that are provided."* (Matching-headings examples must be
  crossed off the list — see Type 5 step h.)
- *"Don't worry if you don't understand every word. It may not be necessary to understand all

- *"Skim the questions, where appropriate, before reading the text so that you have a purpose
  for reading."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF, Reading Do/Don't lists]`

- The Guide for teachers' one-line summary of what actually matters: students must *"use
  reading skills such as skimming and scanning — they will need to use these skills to answer
  all the questions in 1 hour"* and *"know how best to approach each type of reading task."*
  `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers, Tips from teachers]`

## MYTHS

Advice that is popular, and either wrong, unsupported, or actively score-lowering for a
band 6.5–7.5 candidate.

**1. "Read the whole passage carefully first, then answer the questions."**
Wrong, and the most expensive habit there is. Official: *"Don't waste time reading the whole
text each time for each set of questions… you need to skim quickly through the text rather
than reading it all carefully."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]`
And on matching headings: do not *"waste time reading the text carefully from beginning to
end."* `[src: ielts.org — News & Insights: matching headings]`
IDP calls it *"perhaps the most dangerous myth."*
`[src: idp.com — 8 myths about the IELTS Reading test to debunk]`
**Why it lowers the score:** 2,150–2,750 words read at comprehension speed consumes 25–35 of
your 60 minutes and you still have to re-locate every answer.

**2. "Not Given is the statistically safest blind guess, because candidates over-choose False."**
`[UNVERIFIED — and I could find no official or credible statistic supporting it.]` No Tier 1
source publishes the answer-key distribution for T/F/NG. This claim currently sits in
`knowledge/tricks.md` §2 and **should be removed or explicitly downgraded**.
**Why it can lower the score:** it trains the reader to reach for NOT GIVEN before completing
the decision procedure, which converts winnable FALSE items into wrong answers. The
defensible version is procedural, not statistical: *if you have located the region and cannot
point at a contradicting sentence, the answer is NOT GIVEN* — which is just STEP 7 done
properly.

**3. "If the statement contains an absolute (all / never / only / always), the answer is False."**
Wrong. The official worked examples produce **FALSE** for one absolute (*both* Nobel prizes,
where the text says *sole winner*) and **NOT GIVEN** for another (*only a few* of the
children, where the text gives no proportion at all).
`[src: ielts.org — Academic Reading Sample Tasks 2023 PDF, TFNG answers]` ·
`[src: ielts.idp.com — Examples of how to use 'not given']`
**Correct rule:** an absolute is a claim that must itself be checked against the text. If the
text addresses it and disagrees → FALSE. If the text is silent on that dimension → NOT GIVEN.

**4. "Answers always come in order, so if you find Q17's answer after Q19's you made a mistake."**
Wrong as a blanket rule. Order is **type-specific**. Matching information, matching features,
summary/note/table/flow-chart completion and diagram labelling are not guaranteed to be in
order. See the master table above. Even short-answer questions can be *"in slightly, but never
totally, different order."*
`[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` ·
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 11 Activity PDF]`
**Why the myth lowers the score:** a candidate who believes it will abandon correct answers
during matching information, which is precisely the type where the belief is false.

**5. "Answers never come in order, so always search the whole passage."**
The opposite error, and equally costly. Official: *"Don't go back to the beginning of the text
for each question when you know from the task type that the answers will come in the order of
the information in the text."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]`

**6. "Do all the questions of one type across all three passages first."**
Contradicted by Tier 1: *"Start at the beginning of the test and work through it."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]`
On computer, passages and their questions are bound together on screen; jumping between them
adds navigation cost and throws away the familiarity that makes the later question sets in a
passage cheap.

**7. "Pick the multiple-choice option that fits grammatically."**
Explicitly debunked by Cambridge: *"Make sure students realise that this is not the case —
all answer options (A–D) are grammatically possible."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 1 Activity PDF, step 8]`

**8. "If the same words appear in a paragraph as in the question, that's the answer."**
Explicitly debunked by Cambridge for matching information: *"more than one paragraph may deal
with the same general area… **You need to look for the information not the words.**"*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Academic Reading Task Type 4 Activity PDF, Key to Exercise 3]`
Generalises to every type: word-matching is the mechanism by which distractors are built.

**9. "Leave it blank if you're not sure — a wrong answer might look bad."**
There is no negative marking. *"Test takers are not penalised for wrong answers"*; *"a blank
answer will get no marks."*
`[src: ielts.org — News & Insights: matching headings]` ·
`[src: ielts.idp.com — IELTS Reading band scores and what they mean]`

**10. "Write two possible answers and let the marker pick the right one."**
Actively harmful: *"Don't write more than one answer when only one is required. Even if one of
your answers is correct, you will not receive a mark."*
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]`

**11. "Use your background knowledge — if you know the topic you'll answer faster."**
For T/F/NG and Y/N/NG this is a direct mark-killer. Official: *"You must be careful not to use
any information you already know about the topic of the text"*; and the Dublin worked example
exists solely to demonstrate that knowing the right answer in the real world produces the
wrong answer on the test.
`[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` ·
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF, Worksheet 1 key]`

**12. "You must understand every word; build a huge topic-vocabulary list per subject."**
Official: *"Don't worry if you don't understand every word. It may not be necessary to
understand all the words in order to answer the questions correctly."* And technical terms are
glossed for you.
`[src: cambridgeenglish.org — IELTS Academic FAQs PDF]` ·
`[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]`
Caveat that saves this from being a licence to skip vocabulary: some answers *do* hinge on one
lexical item (*benign* → *harmless* → *not unhealthy*).
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: T/F/NG lesson PDF, Q5]`
The efficient target is **paraphrase/synonym range**, not topic glossaries.

**13. "Passage 3 is always the hardest, so plan 25 minutes for it."**
The timing advice may well be right *for a given individual*, but the premise is
`[UNVERIFIED]` — no Tier 1 source states that difficulty increases across passages. Teach
20/20/20 as the default and let the reader's own timed data justify any shift.

**14. "There's a statistic that X% of band 6–7 candidates get NG wrong / Cambridge item
analysis shows…"**
Encountered on Tier 3 blogs during discovery with a fabricated-looking attribution to
"Cambridge Assessment English item analysis data (2024)". **No such published dataset was
found.** Do not reproduce any such statistic in the book. `[UNVERIFIED — do not use]`

## Band-descriptor implications

**Reading has no band descriptors in the sense that Writing and Speaking do.** It is a
raw-score-converted objective test: *"Each correct item is awarded one mark. Band scores…
are awarded to test takers on the basis of their raw scores."*
`[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — IELTS Guide for teachers, p.7]`
The only published band statements for Reading are the general nine-band ability scale
(Band 7 = *"Good user"* with *"occasional inaccuracies, inappropriacies and misunderstandings
in some situations"*; Band 6 = *"Competent user"* despite *"some inaccuracies… and
misunderstandings"*).
`[src: ielts.idp.com — IELTS Reading band scores and what they mean]`

So "how does this tip raise a band 6.5–7.5 score?" has an arithmetic answer for Reading, and
the chapter should be built around it:

1. **The 6.5 → 7 gap is exactly 3 marks (27 → 30).** Every recommendation must be justified in
   marks, not in feeling.
2. **Marks are not lost where candidates think they are.** For a strong-English L1-French/Arabic
   candidate the losses cluster in:
   - **Mechanical losses** on completion types — exceeding the word limit, misspelling a
     transcribed word, adding an article or plural the text did not have. These are 100%
     preventable (word-limit discipline + copy-paste on computer) and typically worth 2–4
     marks. `[src: ielts.org — Reading test format]` · `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — FAQs PDF]`
   - **NG-decision losses** on T/F/NG and Y/N/NG. Sets are commonly 5–7 questions; a
     systematic FALSE-for-NOT-GIVEN error costs 2–3 marks in one set.
   - **Time-out losses** on the last passage — blanks that score zero when a guess costs
     nothing. `[src: ielts.idp.com — Reading band scores]`
3. **The construct being assessed is paraphrase recognition + information location**, not
   comprehension of the whole text. Official framing: the paper *"tests their knowledge and
   understanding of synonyms and paraphrasing"* `[src: cambridgeenglish.org — Task Type 6 PDF]`,
   and the skills list is *"reading for gist, reading for main ideas, reading for detail,
   skimming, understanding logical argument and recognising writers' opinions, attitudes and
   purpose."* `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Reading: Matching Features lesson PDF, p.1]`
   → Training time should go to paraphrase mapping and located-reading drills, not to reading
   more articles for pleasure.
4. **Cross-skill dividend.** Every hour spent on Reading paraphrase-mapping feeds Writing
   Lexical Resource and Task 1 introduction paraphrasing, and feeds the repo's
   `progress/vocab-bank.md`. Worth stating in the chapter so the reader budgets accordingly.
5. **Academic items are pitched at bands 5–8** `[src: takeielts.britishcouncil.org — Guide for
   teachers, p.4]`, so a band-7 target means the *hard* items are in scope. There is no version
   of "get everything easy right" that reaches 30/40 — roughly 10 of the 40 items will be
   pitched at or above the reader's target.
