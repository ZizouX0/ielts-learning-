## What GRA measures at band 7

### The official definition (Tier 1)

The Writing Key Assessment Criteria PDF (p.4) states that GRA *"refers to the range and
accurate use of the candidate's grammatical resource via the candidate's writing at
**sentence level**"*, and lists four things it assesses:

1. the range and appropriacy of structures used (e.g. simple, compound and complex sentences)
2. the **accuracy** of simple, compound **and complex** sentences
3. the **density** and communicative effect of grammatical errors
4. the accurate and appropriate use of **punctuation**

*(Source: ielts.org Key Assessment Criteria PDF, created 2023-05-03, Tier 1.)*

Four things follow immediately, and each one is worth more to this reader than any
"advanced grammar" list:

- **Punctuation is a quarter of the named criteria.** It is not presentation. It is scored
  inside GRA, at every band from 3 to 9.
- **Accuracy is assessed separately for simple, compound and complex sentences.** The
  descriptors do not average your accuracy; they compare your complex sentences against
  your simple ones (see the band-6 limiter below).
- **"Density" of errors is named**, not just severity. Frequency counts on its own.
- Spelling and word formation are **not** here — the Key Assessment Criteria puts *"the
  density and communicative effect of errors in spelling"* and *"…in word formation"* under
  **Lexical Resource** (p.4). A misspelling costs LR, not GRA.

Weighting: the official scoring hub states *"The criteria are weighted equally and the
score on the task is the average"* (ielts.org, Tier 1). IDP's official grammar article puts
the same thing as **"25% of your total score"** in both Writing and Speaking (ielts.idp.com,
Tier 1). Because Task 2 carries twice the weight of Task 1, Writing = (T1 + 2×T2) ÷ 3, so
Task 2 GRA alone is roughly **one sixth** of the Writing band.

### The GRA ladder, bands 5 to 8, in the writer's own words

Identical wording in the Task 1 and Task 2 columns of the May 2023 descriptors. Bolding in
the original marks *"negative features that will limit a rating"*.

| Band | Range | Accuracy | Punctuation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **8** | "A wide range of structures is flexibly and accurately used." | "**The majority of sentences are error-free**"; occasional, non-systematic errors with minimal impact | "punctuation is well managed" |
| **7** | "A variety of complex structures is used with some flexibility and accuracy." | "**error-free sentences are frequent**"; "A few errors in grammar may persist, but these do not impede communication." | "Grammar and punctuation are generally well controlled" |
| **6** | "A mix of simple and complex sentence forms is used but flexibility is limited." | "**Examples of more complex structures are not marked by the same level of accuracy as in simple structures.**" | "Errors in grammar and punctuation occur, but rarely impede communication" |
| **5** | "The range of structures is limited and rather repetitive." | "Although complex sentences are attempted, they tend to be faulty, and **the greatest accuracy is achieved on simple sentences**." Grammatical errors "may be frequent and cause some difficulty for the reader." | "**Punctuation may be faulty.**" |

*(All: ielts.org Writing Band Descriptors, Updated May 2023, Tier 1.)*

Speaking, current file (created 2025-09-16), band 7 GRA: *"A range of structures flexibly
used. Error-free sentences are frequent. Both simple and complex sentences are used
effectively despite some errors. A few basic errors persist."* Band 8: *"Wide range of
structures, flexibly used. The majority of sentences are error free."* Band 6: *"Produces a
mix of short and complex sentence forms and a variety of structures with limited
flexibility. Though errors frequently occur in complex structures, these rarely impede
communication."* *(ielts.org Speaking Band Descriptors, Tier 1.)*

### Decoding: what actually separates 6 from 7

**Decode 1 — the 6→7 gate is not more complexity. It is closing the accuracy gap.**

This is the most important sentence in the chapter. Read the band-6 limiter again:
*"Examples of more complex structures are not marked by the same level of accuracy as in
simple structures."* Band 6 already has *"a mix of simple and complex sentence forms"*. The
complex sentences are already there. What holds the script at 6 is that **they are worse
than the simple ones**. Band 5 says the same thing more bluntly: *"the greatest accuracy is
achieved on simple sentences."*

So the ladder 5 → 6 → 7 is not a complexity ladder at all. It is an **accuracy-parity**
ladder:

- Band 5: complex sentences attempted, mostly faulty
- Band 6: complex sentences present, less accurate than the simple ones
- Band 7: complex sentences used *with* accuracy — the gap has closed

A reader at 6 who responds by writing *more* complex sentences, at the same accuracy, moves
**down**, not up: they increase the number of faulty complex structures and make the band-6
limiter more visible. This is the whole "helps-not-hurts" filter, and it is written into the
official descriptor rather than inferred.

**Decode 2 — "a variety of complex structures", not a wide range, and not exotic ones.**

Band 7 asks for *"a variety"*; band 8 asks for *"a wide range"*. Variety means more than one
kind, used more than once, correctly. Nothing in any Tier 1 document names a specific
structure as required or impressive. The Speaking Key Assessment Criteria gives the fullest
official statement of what "range" is made of — five indicators:

> ▪ the length of spoken sentences ▪ appropriate use of subordinate clauses within clauses
> and phrases ▪ complexity of the verb phrase (correct use of auxiliaries in
> continuous/perfect aspect, modality and passive voice) ▪ complexity of other phrases (use
> of pre- and post-modification: items before and after the head noun/adjective, etc.)
> ▪ range of sentence structures, especially to move elements around for information focus.

*(ielts.org Speaking Key Assessment Criteria p.3, Tier 1. This is the Speaking document —
the Writing one is thinner, saying only "simple, compound and complex sentences" — but the
five indicators describe grammatical range as a construct, not a skill-specific rubric.)*

Two of those five are almost universally ignored by candidates and both are cheap:

- *"complexity of other phrases (use of pre- and post-modification)"* — i.e. a rich **noun
  phrase** counts as range. "The sharp decline in rural enrolment recorded after 2015" is
  grammatical range, with no subordinate clause in it at all, and almost no error risk.
- *"range of sentence structures, especially to move elements around for information
  focus"* — this is the official basis for **clefts and fronting** counting as range.

**Decode 3 — how "frequent" is "frequent"? The honest answer.**

No official source quantifies it. What exists:

- Band 8 = *"the majority of sentences are error-free"*. Majority = more than half. So
  band 7's *"frequent"* is, by construction, **regularly occurring but short of a
  majority** — the descriptors are ordered, so band 7 must be less than band 8.
- The header rule constrains it from below: *"A script must fully fit the positive features
  of the descriptor at a particular level."* You do not get band 7 for a handful of clean
  sentences among faulty ones; error-free sentences must be a *normal* feature of the script.
- **Empirical anchor (Roothooft & Breeze 2019, Tier 1).** On individual high-frequency
  features the study found a clean cliff: for third person -s, articles, -ing, regular past
  and irregular past, *"these features are below a 10% error rate at bands 7 and 8"*, and the
  authors invoke the standard SLA acquisition criterion (Dulay & Burt 1973: a feature counts
  as acquired at 90% accuracy or higher).

**Working target for this reader, derived not quoted:** on any single grammatical feature,
fewer than **one error per ten opportunities**. Not "write 60% complex sentences" — that
number does not exist anywhere official (see MYTHS #2). Count *opportunities missed*, not
*structures attempted*.

**Decode 4 — "do not impede communication" is a floor, not a target.**

Band 7 tolerates *"a few errors in grammar… but these do not impede communication"*. Band 6
says errors *"rarely impede communication"*. Both bands are above the impede-line. So
intelligibility does **not** separate 6 from 7 — the count of clean sentences does. This
inverts the intuitive priority: at the 6→7 boundary, the expensive errors are the **frequent
ones**, not the severe ones. An article slip in every third sentence does not obscure a
single idea, and it is precisely what caps this reader at 6, because every sentence it lands
in is removed from the error-free count. This is the analytic basis for the prioritised
error list below.

**Decode 5 — punctuation is inside the accuracy count.**

Because *"Grammar and punctuation are generally well controlled"* sits in the same band-7
cell as *"error-free sentences are frequent"*, a punctuation error makes a sentence not
error-free. A comma splice is worse still: it damages **two** independent clauses at once.
IDP's official article names this exact error — the common mistake of *"using commas instead
of full stops"* — and gives a worked comma-splice example (ielts.idp.com, Tier 1).

## The band-7 structures

Seven structures. For each: the form, an **original** example, when to use it, and — the
part most prep material omits — when it costs you more than it earns.

Where Roothooft & Breeze (2019) measured a structure, its numbers are given, because they
change the advice. Reminder: Speaking data, bands 4–8, 73 interviews.

### 1. Relative clauses — the safest complexity you own

**Form.** *Defining* (restrictive): identifies which one; no commas; `that` allowed;
the pronoun can be dropped when it is the object. *Non-defining*: adds information about an
already-identified thing; commas both sides; `that` **not** allowed.

**Original examples.**

- Defining: *Countries that invest early in vocational training tend to report lower youth
  unemployment.*
- Defining, pronoun dropped: *The measures the council introduced in 2019 have had almost no
  effect on congestion.*
- Non-defining: *Vocational training, which costs a fraction of a university degree to
  deliver, reaches students that academic routes never see.*

**When to use.** Constantly. Relative clauses are the best-value complex structure in the
evidence base. Roothooft & Breeze counted attempts per band: **19** at band 4, 61 at band 5,
86 at band 6, **185 at band 7**, 189 at band 8 — attempts more than double from band 6 to
band 7 — while the error rate fell to **2.2% at band 7**, the lowest of any complex
structure they measured. Band-7 speakers use relative clauses twice as often *and* get them
right. No other structure in the study shows that combination.

**When it backfires.**
- **Resumptive pronoun** — the Arabic transfer error, treated in full below.
  ✗ *the policy which the government introduced **it***
- **Comma changing your meaning.** *Students who live far from campus miss more classes*
  (some students) vs *Students, who live far from campus, miss more classes* (all students
  live far from campus — usually not what you meant). A misplaced comma here is a punctuation
  error **and** a content error.
- **`which` pointing at a whole clause.** ✗ *Enrolment fell sharply, which the government
  did not expect it to happen.* Keep `which` pointing at a noun, or rewrite with *and this*.
- **Stacking.** Two relative clauses inside one sentence usually produces a sentence you
  cannot finish accurately under time pressure.

### 2. Conditionals — high status, and the measured trap

**Form.** 1st: `If + present simple, will + base`. 2nd: `If + past simple, would + base`
(hypothetical present/future). 3rd: `If + past perfect, would have + past participle`
(unreal past). Mixed: `If + past perfect, would + base` (past condition, present result).

**Original examples.**

- 2nd: *If governments taxed sugary drinks at the same rate as tobacco, consumption would
  fall within a decade.*
- 3rd: *If the line had been extended in 2010, the city would have avoided a decade of
  congestion.*
- Mixed: *If the scheme had been funded properly at the outset, it would not be facing
  closure now.*

**When to use.** The second conditional is genuinely useful in Task 2, because Task 2 asks
you to argue about policies that do not exist yet. One well-formed second conditional in a
body paragraph does real work.

**When it backfires — and the data is blunt about this.** In Roothooft & Breeze, **band 7 had
the highest conditional error rate in the entire study: 27.8%**, against 10.7% at band 6 and
2.7% at band 8. The cause is specific: of the **eight** second conditionals produced by
band-7 speakers, **all eight contained an error**. And across all 73 interviews at every
band, *"There were no third conditional structures in the data."*

Read that properly. The third conditional is not a band marker — it is a structure that
IELTS candidates from band 4 to band 8 essentially never produce. Reaching for it to impress
is reaching for something no band-8 speaker in the sample bothered with, using a form with
three moving parts under exam time pressure.

The recurring wrong forms:
- ✗ *If governments **would tax** sugary drinks, consumption would fall.* → ✓ *If governments
  **taxed** sugary drinks…* (no `would` in the if-clause)
- ✗ *If the fee **will increase**, fewer students will apply.* → ✓ *If the fee **increases**,
  fewer students will apply.*
- ✗ *If the scheme **would have been** funded, it would have succeeded.* → ✓ *If the scheme
  **had been** funded, it would have succeeded.*

Note deliberately **not** marked wrong: *If public transport **was** free, more people would
use it.* `were` is the formal subjunctive and the safer choice in an academic register, but
`was` is standard in modern English and is not an error a defensible examiner would penalise.
Prefer `were`; do not fear `was`.

**Verdict for this reader:** own the second conditional cold, in one memorised *pattern*
(not a memorised sentence). Leave the third alone unless the essay genuinely turns on unreal
past. Skip mixed entirely.

### 3. Concession clauses — the highest-frequency error in this whole chapter

**Form.** Three grammatically different things that all mean "contrast", and they take
different complements:

| Word | Takes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| although / though / even though | a **clause** (subject + verb) | *Although fees have risen, applications continue to grow.* |
| while / whereas | a **clause** | *Whereas rural clinics are understaffed, urban hospitals turn patients away.* |
| despite / in spite of | a **noun phrase** or **-ing** | *Despite the rise in fees, applications continue to grow.* |
| however / nevertheless / nonetheless | **nothing** — they are adverbs, not conjunctions | *Fees have risen. However, applications continue to grow.* |

**When to use.** In Task 2 this is the counter-argument machine, and in Task 1 it handles
exceptions to a trend. It is also the cheapest way to signal a *"variety of complex
structures"*, because a concession clause is unambiguously subordination.

**When it backfires.** Three failures, all of them documented for this reader's L1s.

- **`Although … but`** — one conjunction too many. IDP's official grammar article lists
  *"Double conjunctions ('Although…but')"* as a named common error (ielts.idp.com, Tier 1).
  ✗ *Although the scheme is expensive, but it has reduced congestion.*
  ✓ *Although the scheme is expensive, it has reduced congestion.*
  **Why:** English allows exactly one conjunction to join two clauses. Arabic and French both
  tolerate a resumptive contrastive marker in the main clause; English does not.
- **`Despite` + clause** — the single most predictable error for an Arabic/French bilingual.
  ✗ *Despite the scheme is expensive, it has reduced congestion.*
  ✓ *Despite **the expense of the scheme**, it has reduced congestion.*
  ✓ *Despite **being expensive**, the scheme has reduced congestion.*
  ✓ *Despite **the fact that** the scheme is expensive, it has reduced congestion.*
  **Why:** French *bien que* and Arabic *raghma anna* (رغم أن) both take a full clause, and
  both are the natural translation of *despite*. English splits the job: *although* takes the
  clause, *despite* takes the noun. `despite the fact that` is the escape hatch when you
  cannot nominalise fast enough — it is clumsy but correct, and correct beats elegant.
- **`However` used as a conjunction** — a comma splice. See the punctuation section; this is
  the highest-yield single fix in the chapter.

### 4. Passives — required in Task 1, optional in Task 2, dangerous everywhere

**Form.** `be` + past participle. Add the agent with `by` only when the agent matters.

**Original examples.**

- Task 1 process (present simple passive, the default): *The harvested cane is washed,
  crushed and then heated until the juice separates.*
- Task 2, agent genuinely irrelevant: *Tuition fees were frozen for three consecutive years.*
- Perfect/modal passive: *The scheme should have been evaluated before it was extended.*

**When to use.** Task 1 process diagrams effectively require it: the agent is unknown or
irrelevant, and the passive is the natural register (IDP's official Task 1 language guide
specifies present simple + passive for processes — already logged in R4's ledger, Tier 1).
The Speaking Key Assessment Criteria also names *"complexity of the verb phrase (correct use
of auxiliaries in continuous/perfect aspect, modality and **passive voice**)"* as a range
indicator, so passives count as range in their own right (Tier 1).

**When it backfires.**

- **The intransitive passive.** This is a genuine error, documented in Francophone learner
  corpora (Sokeng 2014, Tier 2, example 51: *"The concert **was happened** the week of
  Christmas"*).
  ✗ *The accident **was happened** last year.* → ✓ *The accident **happened** last year.*
  ✗ *The population **was risen** sharply.* → ✓ *The population **rose** sharply.*
  **Why:** *happen, occur, arise, appear, exist, rise, fall, emerge, consist, remain* have no
  object, so there is nothing to promote to subject. No object → no passive. Ever.
- **Vagueness in Task 2.** Task Response wants *"a clear and developed position"*. *It is
  believed that…* / *It is often argued that…* hides who believes it, and if you never say,
  the examiner cannot find your position. IDP's official guidance is explicit: *"Passive voice
  is common in academic-style writing, but that doesn't mean you should use it in every
  sentence"* (Tier 1).
- **Roothooft's warning.** Passives barely appear below band 7 (1, 4 and 7 attempts at bands
  4, 5, 6), then jump to **74 attempts at band 7 with an 8.1% error rate**, against 69
  attempts at band 8 with **1.4%**. The report's own summary: *"band 7 can be characterised by
  attempts at using passive structure, with some errors, while in band 8 passives are
  relatively frequent and are generally produced without errors."* Attempting passives is what
  band-7 candidates do; **getting them right** is what band-8 candidates do.

### 5. Participle clauses — the highest-value under-used signal

**Form.** Three types, all of which delete a subject and a finite verb:

| Type | From | To |
|---|---|---|
| **-ing** (active) | *Because they lack reliable transport, many rural students miss classes.* | ***Lacking** reliable transport, many rural students miss classes.* |
| **-ed** (passive) | *The measures **which were introduced** in 2020 have had little effect.* | *The measures **introduced** in 2020 have had little effect.* |
| **having + p.p.** (perfect) | *After the council had introduced a charge, it saw traffic fall.* | ***Having introduced** a charge, the council saw traffic fall.* |

There is a fourth use that is worth more to this reader than the other three combined,
because it is tailor-made for Task 1 — the **trailing -ing clause** that comments on the
main clause:

- *Enrolment doubled between 2010 and 2020, **rising** fastest in the final three years.*
- *Solar generation reached 40 terawatt-hours, **accounting** for roughly a quarter of total
  supply.*
- *The two figures converged after 2015, **remaining** within two percentage points of each
  other thereafter.*

**When to use.** Everywhere you would otherwise write a second short sentence with the same
subject. It buys subordination, drops five or six words, and — for a writer under a 20- and
40-minute clock — that word economy is real. It is also the clearest available signal of
*"a variety of complex structures"* precisely because most band-6 scripts contain none.

**When it backfires — one failure mode, and it is severe.**

**The dangling participle.** A participle clause takes its subject from the subject of the
main clause. If they do not match, the sentence says something absurd.

✗ *Having introduced a congestion charge, traffic in the city centre fell by a fifth.*
 — the traffic introduced the charge.
✓ *Having introduced a congestion charge, **the council** watched traffic in the city centre
fall by a fifth.*
✓ *After the council introduced a congestion charge, traffic in the city centre fell by a
fifth.*

✗ *Lacking qualifications, employers rejected most of the applicants.*
 — the employers lack qualifications.
✓ *Lacking qualifications, **most of the applicants** were rejected by employers.*

**The one-second self-check:** after writing a participle clause, put your finger on the
word immediately after the comma. That word must be the thing doing the participle. If it
is not, rewrite as a full clause with *Because* / *After* / *Since*.

⚠️ Sourcing note: participle clauses are **not named** in any Tier 1 IELTS document. Their
value here is derived from the descriptor requirement for a *variety of complex structures*
plus the Speaking KAC's *"appropriate use of subordinate clauses within clauses and phrases"*
and *"complexity of other phrases"*. Flagged in open questions.

### 6. Cleft sentences — one per essay, no more

**Form.** *It*-cleft: `It is/was + X + that/who + …`. *Wh*-cleft (pseudo-cleft):
`What … is/was + X`.

**Original examples.**

- *It is the shortage of qualified teachers, rather than the shortage of buildings, that
  limits access to education in rural areas.*
- *What these figures do not show is how the cost is distributed across income groups.*
- *What matters is not how much is spent, but who benefits from the spending.*

**When to use.** Exactly where the Speaking Key Assessment Criteria points: *"range of
sentence structures, especially **to move elements around for information focus**"* (Tier 1).
That is the official warrant for clefts. In practice: use one to state your thesis, or one to
land the point you most want the reader to take from a body paragraph. It is a rhetorical
instrument, not a decoration.

**When it backfires.**
- **It reads as a memorised frame if repeated.** The band-4 LR descriptor penalises
  *"inappropriate use of lexical chunks (e.g. memorised phrases, formulaic language)"*, and
  the Key Assessment Criteria warns that scripts *"may be penalised if they are a) partly or
  wholly plagiarised"*, with band 0 reserved in part for wholly memorised answers (Tier 1).
  Three clefts in one essay stops looking like control and starts looking like a template.
- **Agreement slips.** *What matters **are** the outcomes* — the subject is *What matters*
  (singular). ✓ *What matters **is** the outcomes* is awkward; better: ✓ *What matters is the
  outcome*, or ✓ *The outcomes are what matter.*
- **It lengthens the sentence.** If you are already fighting run-ons (you are), a cleft adds
  a clause boundary you have to punctuate correctly.

### 7. Nominalisation — the academic-register marker, with a tax

**Form.** Turn the verb or adjective into a noun and make it the subject.

| Verbal | Nominalised |
|---|---|
| *Governments regulate emissions strictly, and this has cut air pollution.* | ***Strict regulation** of emissions has cut air pollution.* |
| *The population grew rapidly, which meant housing became scarce.* | ***Rapid population growth** produced a housing shortage.* |
| *Fewer people are willing to commute, so firms have had to offer hybrid contracts.* | ***A decline in willingness to commute** has forced firms to offer hybrid contracts.* |

**When to use.** Three payoffs, all real:
1. **Register.** Nominalisation is the marker of academic prose, and the descriptors require
   an *"academic or semi-formal/neutral style"* (ielts.org format page, Tier 1).
2. **Cohesion, which is a *different criterion*.** Once you have a noun, you can refer back
   to it: *This decline…*, *Such regulation…*. The Key Assessment Criteria names *"the
   flexible use of reference and substitution"* as a **Coherence & Cohesion** sub-skill
   (Tier 1). So nominalising pays you in CC as well as LR.
3. **Density under time pressure.** It removes a clause, and with it a chance to make a
   verb-agreement error.

**When it backfires — and this warning is specific to this reader.**

- **Every nominalisation creates a new article decision.** *Governments regulate* has no
  article problem. *Strict regulation* / *The strict regulation of emissions introduced in
  2019* does. Since articles are this reader's single most expensive error (see the
  prioritised list), nominalisation trades a verb-agreement risk for an article risk — and
  the article risk is the more expensive one **until the article procedure below is
  automatic**. Sequence matters: fix articles first, then nominalise.
- **Noun strings become unreadable.** *Government transport infrastructure investment
  policy reform* is four modifiers deep and impedes communication, which is a band-4/5 event.
  Cap at two premodifiers.
- **The agent disappears.** Task Response wants a position. *A reduction in subsidies would
  be advisable* does not say who should reduce them. Nominalise the process, keep the agent.

## Punctuation and the comma splice

### Why this section is not optional

Punctuation is inside the GRA criterion (Key Assessment Criteria p.4: *"the accurate and
appropriate use of punctuation"*), and it is inside the same band-7 cell as the error-free
sentence count. IDP's official grammar article names the exact failure: *"Punctuation errors
can also affect communication, so make sure it's clear where your sentences begin and end"*,
and separately warns against *"using commas instead of full stops"*, illustrating it with a
comma-splice example of its own (ielts.idp.com, Tier 1). Band 5's bolded limiter is
*"Punctuation may be faulty."*

For this reader the exposure is doubled: **Arabic rhetoric favours coordination over
subordination**, and **French academic style tolerates long comma-linked periods**. Both
push toward the same error.

### The definition, and the four repairs

Purdue OWL: *"run-ons, comma splices, and fused sentences are all names given to compound
sentences that are not punctuated correctly"*; a comma splice is a comma placed between two
independent clauses; a fused sentence has *"no connecting word or punctuation to separate the
clauses"* (Purdue OWL, Tier 2 — university writing centre).

An **independent clause** = subject + finite verb + complete thought. Two of them, joined by
a bare comma, is a splice.

Take one faulty original and repair it four ways:

> ✗ *Online courses are convenient, they allow students to study at their own pace.*

| Repair | Result | When to choose it |
|---|---|---|
| **Full stop** | *Online courses are convenient. They allow students to study at their own pace.* | Always available. Zero risk. Use it if you are unsure. |
| **Semicolon** | *Online courses are convenient; they allow students to study at their own pace.* | Only when the two clauses are closely linked. Counts as range, but a wrong semicolon is a punctuation error. |
| **Comma + FANBOYS** (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) | *Online courses are convenient, **so** they allow students to study at their own pace.* — weak here; better: *Online courses are convenient, **and** they let students work at their own pace.* | Compound sentence. Safe. Note the comma goes **before** the conjunction. |
| **Subordinate one clause** | *Online courses are convenient **because** they allow students to study at their own pace.* | **Best for GRA.** This is the only repair that converts a punctuation error into a complex structure. |

That last row is the whole strategy: **every comma splice in your draft is a complex sentence
you failed to build.** Repairing splices with subordinators raises range and accuracy at the
same time.

### The `however` rule — the single highest-yield punctuation fix

*However, therefore, moreover, furthermore, nevertheless, thus, consequently, in addition,
for example, on the other hand* are **adverbs**, not conjunctions. They cannot join two
clauses. Every one of them placed after a comma between two clauses is a splice.

✗ *Fees have risen sharply, however enrolment has continued to grow.*
✗ *Fees have risen sharply, however, enrolment has continued to grow.* ← still a splice; the
commas around *however* do not change what is joining the clauses
✓ *Fees have risen sharply. However, enrolment has continued to grow.*
✓ *Fees have risen sharply; however, enrolment has continued to grow.*
✓ *Although fees have risen sharply, enrolment has continued to grow.* ← best; buys a complex
structure

**Why it matters so much for this reader:** a band-6 script typically contains five to eight
of these connectors. If each one sits in a splice, the script has five to eight sentences
that are not error-free — which is exactly the mechanism that holds *"error-free sentences
are frequent"* out of reach. And there is a second cost: the band-6 CC descriptor says
cohesion *"may be faulty or mechanical due to misuse, **overuse** or omission"*, so
over-deploying connectors damages Coherence & Cohesion at the same time. One habit, two
criteria.

**Contrast that with `but`, `and`, `so`, `because`, `although`, `while`, `since`, `whereas`
— these are real conjunctions and *can* join two clauses.** Learn the two lists as two lists.

### Four more punctuation rules that cost bands

1. **No comma between subject and verb**, however long the subject.
   ✗ *The rapid expansion of low-cost airlines across southern Europe, has transformed
   regional tourism.* → ✓ delete the comma.
2. **Defining vs non-defining relative clause** — covered above; the comma changes the
   meaning, so it is scored twice.
3. **Introductory element takes a comma.** *In the first decade of the century, enrolment
   doubled.* / *Although fees rose, applications grew.* Missing it is a minor error but it
   is still an error.
4. **The comma before `and` in a compound sentence, but not in a compound predicate.**
   ✓ *Fees rose, and enrolment fell.* (two clauses)
   ✓ *Fees rose and then stabilised.* (one subject, two verbs — no comma)

### 45-second splice drill

Ignore the words. Read only the commas. For each comma, cover everything to its left and ask:
*is what remains on the right a complete sentence?* Then cover the right and ask the same of
the left. **Two complete sentences = a splice.** Fix it with a subordinator if you can, a
full stop if you cannot. Then specifically hunt every *however / therefore / moreover / thus*
and check the punctuation mark in front of it: it must be a full stop or a semicolon, never a
comma.

## Arabic L1 interference

Arabic is a Semitic language with a definite article and **no indefinite article**, a
verbless present-tense sentence type, obligatory resumptive pronouns in most relative
clauses, and a written rhetoric that prefers coordination to subordination. Each of those
four facts predicts a specific, recurring English error.

Evidence base for this section: Junina (2025), *Arabic-to-English Transfer in EFL Writing*,
Frontiers in English Language and Linguistics (60 essays, 30 Palestinian students — Tier 2,
peer-reviewed); Abu Rass (2015), *Challenges Face Arab Students in Writing Well-Developed
Paragraphs in English*, English Language Teaching 8(10) (205 students over 14 years — Tier 2);
Alroudhan (2016), *The Acquisition of English Restrictive Relative Clauses by Arab Adult EFL
Learners*, Advances in Language and Literary Studies 7(1) (100 participants — Tier 2);
Roothooft & Breeze (2019) for the band-level error rates (Tier 1).

### A1. The article system — mechanism

Arabic has the definite article **الـ (al-)**, prefixed to the noun: *al-kitāb* = "the book".
There is **no indefinite article**; indefiniteness is the *absence* of al-, optionally marked
by **tanwīn** (nunation), a diacritic on the noun's ending: *kitābun* = "a book" (Junina 2025,
citing Ryding 2005; Tier 2). In writing, tanwīn is usually not even printed.

Two consequences, pulling in **opposite** directions:

- **Omission of `a/an`.** There is no word to translate, and the marker that does the job in
  Arabic is a diacritic that vanishes in ordinary text. So the slot simply stays empty.
- **Overuse of `the`.** Arabic uses al- with **generic** nouns — "technology in general" is
  *al-tiknulūjiyā*. English drops the article for generics. Junina's corpus contains exactly
  this: *"The technology is important plays an important role in our life."*

And the crucial compounding fact for this particular reader: **French pushes the same way on
generics.** *La société*, *la technologie*, *la nature* all carry the definite article in
generic use. So both of this reader's languages agree with each other and disagree with
English on the same construction. The generic *the* is therefore not a coincidental slip — it
is the single most over-determined error in this reader's profile.

**How much it costs (Tier 1, Roothooft & Breeze 2019, Speaking).** Article error rates by
band: **28.9%** (band 4), 21% (5), **18.4% (band 6)**, **7.4% (band 7)**, 5.2% (8). The chi-
square between bands 6 and 7 is **80.1178** — by a wide margin the largest discontinuity for
any feature in the study, and significant at p<0.01. Bands 5 and 6 were **not** significantly
different from each other. The report's own conclusion, offered as a proposed refinement to
the descriptors:

> - bands 4, 5, 6: candidates can be expected to make a large number of errors against the
>   use of articles and third person -s
> - bands 7, 8: although some errors against articles and third person -s still occur, such
>   errors are infrequent.

Articles do not separate band 5 from band 6. They separate **band 6 from band 7**. That is
this reader's exact boundary.

### A2. Articles — wrong → right

| ✗ | ✓ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *The education is the key to reducing poverty.* | *Education is the key to reducing poverty.* | Generic uncountable → zero article. Arabic *al-taʿlīm* and French *l'éducation* both force the article; English drops it. |
| *The technology plays an important role in the modern life.* | *Technology plays an important role in modern life.* | Two generics, two deletions. *Modern life* is generic even with an adjective in front — an adjective does not make a noun specific. |
| *Physical activity is important for health of the body.* | *Physical activity is important for **the** health of the body.* | The reverse error. `the + noun + of + noun` is specific by definition — the *of*-phrase identifies which health. |
| *Social media has great impact on young people.* | *Social media has **a** great impact on young people.* | Singular countable (*impact*), first mention → *a*. No Arabic equivalent to insert, so the slot stays empty. |
| *He works as engineer in a construction firm.* | *He works as **an** engineer in a construction firm.* | Singular countable after *as*. Same mechanism. |

### A3. Copula omission — mechanism and pairs

Arabic present-tense sentences can be **verbless** (the *jumla ismiyya*, nominal sentence):
*al-madīnatu kabīra* = literally "the-city big" = "the city **is** big". There is no present
tense of *to be*. Documented as a classic negative-transfer case (Abu Rass 2015, citing
Benmamoun 2000; Tier 2).

| ✗ | ✓ |
|---|---|
| *The main problem in many cities the cost of housing.* | *The main problem in many cities **is** the cost of housing.* |
| *These measures very expensive for small businesses.* | *These measures **are** very expensive for small businesses.* |
| *Although the policy popular, it has not reduced emissions.* | *Although the policy **is** popular, it has not reduced emissions.* |

This is a *severe* error — it can impede communication — but it is low-frequency at this
reader's level. Ranked accordingly below.

### A4. Third person -s and subject–verb agreement

Junina's corpus: *"Technology play an important role in education because it help students
learn better. It give access to many resources and make learning more interactive."* — four
omissions in two sentences (Tier 2).

**Cost (Tier 1, Roothooft & Breeze).** Error rate for third person -s: 76.6% (band 4), 58.6%
(5), **70.6% (band 6)**, **7.6% (band 7)**, 5.2% (8). No significant difference between bands
4, 5 and 6; a hard cut-off between 6 and 7. The report notes third person -s occupies the
final position in the acquisition order at nearly every band — it is the *last* morpheme
acquired, and it is the one that most cleanly marks the 6/7 boundary.

| ✗ | ✓ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *Technology play an important role in education.* | *Technology play**s** an important role in education.* | Singular subject, present simple → -s. |
| *A student who study abroad often become more independent.* | *A student who stud**ies** abroad often become**s** more independent.* | The relative clause verb agrees with the head noun too — two slots, both missed. |
| *Peoples are depend on their smartphones.* | *People depend on their smartphones.* | Three errors: *people* is already plural (no *-s*); *are* + bare verb stacks two verbs; and the preposition (see below). |
| *Each of the schools have its own budget.* | *Each of the schools **has** its own budget.* | *Each* is singular; the plural noun in the *of*-phrase is a distractor. |

**Note on collectives:** *The government have decided…* is standard **British** English and
should **not** be marked wrong. Do not overcorrect here.

### A5. Relative clauses — the resumptive pronoun

**Mechanism.** In Modern Standard Arabic, a relative clause leaves a pronoun in the gap. The
Longman Dictionary of Language Teaching and Applied Linguistics, quoted in Alroudhan (2016),
puts it exactly: resumptive pronouns *"are ungrammatical in Standard English, but they are
acceptable or **obligatory** in many languages (e.g. Arabic)"*. In MSA the resumptive pronoun
is **optional** in subject and direct-object position and **obligatory** in indirect-object
and oblique (prepositional) position (Alroudhan 2016; Tier 2).

There is a **second**, less obvious MSA rule with a mirror-image consequence: the relative
marker (*allathī* etc.) appears **only after a definite head noun**. After an indefinite head
there is no marker at all. English requires the relative pronoun regardless of definiteness.
So Arabic predicts **both** an extra pronoun *and* a missing one, depending on the head.

| ✗ | ✓ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *The policy which the government introduced **it** last year has failed.* | *The policy which the government introduced last year has failed.* | Direct object already relativised by *which*; the resumptive *it* is optional in Arabic, ungrammatical in English. |
| *The students who I spoke to **them** were all satisfied.* | *The students who I spoke to were all satisfied.* / *The students **to whom** I spoke were all satisfied.* | Oblique position — this is where Arabic makes the pronoun **obligatory**, so it is the hardest one to suppress. |
| *This is the school where I studied **in it**.* | *This is the school where I studied.* / *This is the school **in which** I studied.* | *Where* already carries the preposition. Never use both. |
| *There are many families live below the poverty line.* | *There are many families **who** live below the poverty line.* / *There are many families **living** below the poverty line.* | The mirror error: indefinite head (*many families*), so MSA drops the marker. English does not. |

That last row is worth its own note. The reduced-relative repair — *families **living** below
the poverty line* — kills the error **and** produces a participle clause, i.e. it converts a
transfer error directly into a band-7 structure.

### A6. Parataxis, and-chains and run-ons

**Mechanism.** Arabic prose builds by **coordination** (*wa-*, *fa-*) rather than
subordination. Junina (2025): *"Arabic often uses paratactic structures (clauses joined by
conjunctions) rather than hypotactic structures (clauses linked by subordination), leading to
sentences that feel overly long and less focused in English"*, and reports that *"Arabic's
preference for long, complex sentences often results in run-on English sentences"* (Tier 2,
citing Ezza & Almudibry 2014). Abu Rass (2015) records the same across 205 students over
fourteen years, with the *and*/*as* pair explicitly identified as the transfer of Arabic
*wa* and *fa* (Tier 2, citing Al-Khatib 2001, Koch 1983).

**Original example.**

✗ *Public transport in the capital is cheap and it is used by many workers and it reduces
traffic and it also reduces pollution and the government wants to expand it.*

✓ *Public transport in the capital is cheap and heavily used by workers. Because it takes
cars off the road, it reduces both congestion and pollution — which is why the government
intends to expand it.*

Count what the repair bought: one subordinator (*Because*), one non-defining relative
(*which is why*), one compound predicate collapsed (*cheap and heavily used*), 33 words down
from 34 but **five** clauses down to three. Range up, error surface down.

**The related cost that is not GRA.** Abu Rass and Junina both document **repetition and
parallelism** as an Arabic persuasive device transferred into English (*"Education plays a
vital role in the development of education systems worldwide"*). In IELTS that inflates the
word count without extending an idea, which is a **Task Response** problem — band 7 TR warns
of *"a tendency to over-generalise"* and *"a lack of focus and precision in supporting
ideas"* — and an **LR** problem, since band 7 LR requires *"the resource is sufficient to
allow some flexibility and precision"*. Repetition is expensive on three criteria at once.

### A7. Prepositions from Arabic

Junina (2025) documents the mechanism directly: *"She is responsible **about** organizing the
school event"* arises because the Arabic preposition عن (*ʿan*), which translates as *about*,
is the one used with expressions of responsibility (Tier 2).

| ✗ | ✓ |
|---|---|
| *The company is responsible **about** training its staff.* | *The company is responsible **for** training its staff.* |
| *Technology has contributed massively **on** the way schools operate.* | *Technology has contributed massively **to** the way schools operate.* |
| *The report focuses **in** rural healthcare.* | *The report focuses **on** rural healthcare.* |

### A8. Uncountables pluralised — and why this one never goes away on its own

Roothooft & Breeze report, in their own conclusion: *"overuse errors against the plural (errors
such as 'peoples' or '**informations**') were found to occur at **all band levels**"* (Tier 1,
emphasis added). This is the only error type in the entire study explicitly stated **not** to
resolve as proficiency rises. It survives to band 8. It will not fix itself; it has to be
memorised as a closed list. Treated in full in the French section below, since French drives
it hardest — but note that Arabic pluralises several of the same nouns (*maʿlūmāt* =
"informations"), so this reader gets it from both sides.

### A9. Arabic self-check drill — the al- sweep (60 seconds)

Run it in three passes, in this order.

1. **Every `the` (25 s).** For each one, ask a single yes/no question: *is the noun after it
   an abstract idea talked about in general, with no `of`-phrase, relative clause or
   superlative after it?* If yes → **delete `the`**. (society, education, technology, poverty,
   life, nature, health, crime, knowledge, pollution, unemployment.)
2. **Every article-less singular noun (25 s).** Point at each singular countable noun with no
   article, no possessive and no quantifier in front of it. It needs **a/an** (first mention)
   or **the** (identifiable). Proper nouns are exempt.
3. **Every `which` / `who` / `where` (10 s).** Is there a stray pronoun after the verb
   (*which … it*, *who … them*, *where … in it*)? Delete it. Is there a clause with no
   relative pronoun after an indefinite head (*many families live…*)? Insert *who*, or reduce
   to *-ing*.

**Target:** fewer than one article error per ten article slots — the band 7/8 threshold in
Roothooft & Breeze (7.4% and 5.2%), and the standard 90%-accuracy acquisition criterion.

## French L1 interference

French is close enough to English to be dangerous. The errors it produces are not gross
breakdowns; they are high-frequency, low-severity slips of exactly the kind that, per Decode
4 above, cap a script at band 6 without ever impeding a single sentence.

Evidence base: Sokeng (2014), *Grammatical Errors of Bilingual 1 Francophone Learners of
English in the University of Yaoundé I*, Theory and Practice in Language Studies 4(9),
pp.1778–1785 — 250 students, 908 errors classified (Tier 2). ⚠️ This corpus is Cameroonian
Francophone, sociolinguistically distant from a Tunisian French/Arabic bilingual; its
*proportions* should be treated as indicative only, and its *mechanisms* as the transferable
part. See open question 5.

Sokeng's distribution, for orientation: verb tense and form 23%, subject–verb agreement 22%,
prepositions 18%, word order 13%, plurality 9%, articles 5%, passive voice 5%, auxiliaries
3%, double negation 2%.

### F1. The generic definite article — mechanism

French requires a determiner on almost every noun. Critically, it uses **le / la / les with
generic reference**: *La société évolue* = "Society is changing"; *L'éducation est
essentielle* = "Education is essential". English marks generic reference with the **zero
article** on plural countables and on uncountables.

This is the same target as the Arabic al- error (A1), reached by a different route — which is
why it is this reader's number-one error and why the fix is worth automating.

**The rule that stops overcorrection.** The error is not "the". The error is "**the** with a
**generic**". As soon as the noun is made specific — by an *of*-phrase, a relative clause, a
superlative, an ordinal, a date, or previous mention — English wants *the* back.

| ✗ | ✓ | And the contrast that proves the rule |
|---|---|---|
| *The society expects graduates to be job-ready.* | *Society expects graduates to be job-ready.* | ✓ ***The** society **in which we live** rewards specialists.* — relative clause makes it specific |
| *The technology has transformed how children learn.* | *Technology has transformed how children learn.* | ✓ ***The** technology **used in these classrooms** was donated.* |
| *The pollution in the cities is caused mainly by the traffic.* | *Pollution in cities is caused mainly by traffic.* | ✓ ***The** pollution **recorded in 2019** exceeded safe limits.* |
| *The people are reluctant to give up the private cars.* | *People are reluctant to give up private cars.* | ✓ ***The** people **surveyed** were mostly commuters.* |
| *The unemployment among the young people remains high.* | *Unemployment among young people remains high.* | ✓ ***The** unemployment **rate** rose to 14%.* |

### F2. The counter-list — nouns that DO take `the` in generic use

This is the part that no article rule handles, and it is arbitrary. English takes the definite
article generically with a specific, lexicalised set. Overcorrecting these is as costly as the
original error.

| Take **the** | Take **zero** |
|---|---|
| the environment, the government, the media, the police, the public, the internet, the economy, the climate, the countryside, the authorities, the state, the workplace, the past, the future, the elderly, the young, the world | society, nature, technology, education, health, poverty, crime, unemployment, inflation, pollution, globalisation, tourism, agriculture, industry, science, history, art, music, life, knowledge, research, transport |

*Learn this as a list, not a rule — there is no principle that predicts why "the environment"
takes the article and "nature" does not.* ⚠️ This table is assembled from standard English
usage, not from a cited reference grammar; flagged in open questions for a grammar-authority
check.

### F3. Uncountables pluralised — mechanism and pairs

French pluralises freely nouns that English treats as mass nouns: *des informations*, *des
conseils*, *des recherches*, *des équipements*, *des connaissances*, *des meubles*, *des
logiciels*, *des preuves*. British Council LearnEnglish states the rule directly — *"We do
**not** use uncount nouns in the plural and we do **not** use them with the indefinite article
*a/an*"* — and lists, under the heading of uncount nouns **"which have plurals in other
languages"**: advice, accommodation, baggage, equipment, furniture, homework, **knowledge**,
luggage, machinery, money, news, traffic (Tier 2 — British Council ELT, not the IELTS site).
The partitive repair is *a piece of / a bit of / an item of*.

| ✗ | ✓ | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *The report provides several informations about rural transport.* | *The report provides **a good deal of information** about rural transport.* / *…several **pieces of information**…* | |
| *Teachers give students many advices about revision.* | *Teachers give students **a great deal of advice** about revision.* | *much advice*, *some advice*, *a piece of advice* |
| *Recent researches show that sleep affects memory.* | *Recent **research shows** that sleep affects memory.* / *Recent **studies show**…* | The verb changes too — *research* is singular. *Studies* is often the better rewrite. |
| *Hospitals need modern equipments.* | *Hospitals need modern **equipment**.* | |
| *Students value the feedbacks they receive.* | *Students value the **feedback** they receive.* | |
| *Employers look for candidates with technical knowledges.* | *Employers look for candidates with technical **knowledge**.* | ⚠️ But ✓ *a working **knowledge of** statistics* — *knowledge* takes *a* in the premodified `a … knowledge of X` frame. |
| *There are many evidences that air quality has worsened.* | *There is **considerable evidence** that air quality has worsened.* | Also ✓ *several **indications** / **findings** / **studies**.* |
| *Schools buy expensive softwares.* | *Schools buy expensive **software**.* | |
| *The office was furnished with second-hand furnitures.* | *…with second-hand **furniture**.* | |

**The nine-word list to memorise:** information · advice · research · equipment · feedback ·
knowledge · software · furniture · evidence.

### F4. Prepositions from French

French governs its verbs with different prepositions, and the transfer is direct.

| ✗ | ✓ | French source |
|---|---|---|
| *Young people depend **of** their parents for longer.* | *…depend **on** their parents…* | *dépendre **de*** |
| *Few citizens participate **to** local elections.* | *…participate **in** local elections.* | *participer **à*** |
| *This essay will discuss **about** the causes of urban sprawl.* | *This essay will discuss Ø the causes of urban sprawl.* | *discuter **de*** — English *discuss* is transitive, no preposition |
| *She has been married **with** an engineer since 2015.* | *…married **to** an engineer…* | *marié **avec*** |
| *Most tourists arrive **to** the capital by air.* | *…arrive **in** the capital by air.* | *arriver **à*** — *in* a city/country, *at* a building/point |
| *Many workers are interested **by** flexible hours.* | *…interested **in** flexible hours.* | *intéressé **par*** |
| *The company is responsible **of** training its staff.* | *…responsible **for** training…* | *responsable **de*** (Arabic gives *responsible **about***) |
| ***In** the other hand, remote work reduces commuting.* | ***On** the other hand, remote work reduces commuting.* | fixed phrase — memorise |
| *The scheme consists **in** three stages.* | *The scheme consists **of** three stages.* | *consister **en*** |
| *Students suffer **of** exam stress.* | *Students suffer **from** exam stress.* | *souffrir **de*** |

**Also from French: `since` vs `for`.** French *depuis* (and Arabic *mundhu*) cover both.
- **since** + a **point** in time: *since 2015*, *since the reform*, *since March*
- **for** + a **length** of time: *for ten years*, *for a decade*, *for three months*

✗ *The scheme has been running **since** ten years.* → ✓ *…**for** ten years.*
✗ *Enrolment has risen **for** 2015.* → ✓ *…**since** 2015.*

### F5. False friends — the table

Every one of these is a **Lexical Resource** error, not GRA. That matters: band 7 LR requires
*"There are only a few errors in spelling and/or word formation and they do not detract from
overall clarity"*, and false friends are precisely word-choice errors. They cost a different
criterion from the article errors — which means they cannot be traded off against each other.

| The French word | What it actually means | ✗ | ✓ |
|---|---|---|---|
| *actuellement* | currently, at present | *Actually, more than half the population lives in cities.* | ***At present**, more than half the population lives in cities.* |
| *actuel* | current | *The actual situation is worse than in 2010.* | *The **current** situation is worse than in 2010.* |
| *éventuellement* | possibly, if need be | *Eventually, the government could subsidise fares.* | ***Possibly**, the government could subsidise fares.* |
| *assister à* | to attend | *Thousands of students assist to the lecture.* | *Thousands of students **attend** the lecture.* |
| *demander* | to ask | *The teacher demanded the students to explain their answers.* | *The teacher **asked** the students to explain their answers.* |
| *formation* | training | *Employers pay for the formation of new staff.* | *Employers pay for the **training** of new staff.* |
| *sensible* | sensitive | *Children are very sensible to advertising.* | *Children are very **sensitive** to advertising.* |
| *déception* | disappointment | *The results caused a great deception among parents.* | *The results caused great **disappointment** among parents.* |
| *librairie* | bookshop | *She bought the textbook at the university library.* | *…at the university **bookshop**.* |
| *supporter* | to tolerate, put up with | *Commuters cannot support the noise of the motorway.* | *Commuters cannot **tolerate** the noise of the motorway.* |
| *achever* | to complete, finish | *Students must achieve their dissertation before June.* | *Students must **complete** their dissertation before June.* |
| *contrôler* | to check, inspect | *Inspectors control the water quality every month.* | *Inspectors **check** the water quality every month.* |
| *résumer* | to summarise | *The introduction resumes the main findings.* | *The introduction **summarises** the main findings.* |
| *significatif* | significant | *There was a significative rise in energy costs.* | *There was a **significant** rise in energy costs.* (*significative* is not an English word) |
| *inconvénient* | drawback | *The main inconvenient of this policy is its cost.* | *The main **drawback** of this policy is its cost.* |
| *stage* | internship | *He did a six-month stage at a law firm.* | *He did a six-month **internship** at a law firm.* |
| *délai* | deadline, time limit | *Students were given a delay of two weeks.* | *Students were given **two weeks** / **a deadline of two weeks**.* |
| *affaire* | business, matter | *He runs a small affair in the city centre.* | *He runs a small **business** in the city centre.* |
| *hasard* | chance | *The discovery was made by hazard.* | *The discovery was made by **chance**.* |
| *permettre de* | to make it possible to | *Online courses permit to study at any time.* | *Online courses **make it possible to** study at any time.* / *Online courses **allow students to** study at any time.* |

That last row is a grammatical point, not just a lexical one: English *permit* and *allow*
require an **object** before the infinitive. *Permit to do* is ungrammatical in any register.
French *permettre de faire* has no object. If you cannot supply an object, switch verb:
*make it possible to*, *enable X to*, *mean that*.

⚠️ Also worth knowing but not worth a practice sentence: *préservatif* is not *preservative*
(French for the food additive is *conservateur*). Avoid the word entirely in IELTS.

### F6. French-style long sentences and register

French academic prose licenses a longer, more periodic sentence and a higher register than
English academic prose. Combined with Arabic parataxis (A6), this reader is pushed toward long
sentences from two directions at once — one supplying the *length*, the other the *coordination*.

Junina (2025) documents register mixing in Arabic-L1 writers too, and Sokeng records French
word-order transfer (adjective after noun: *my friends old*; adverb placement: *I eat never*).

| ✗ | ✓ |
|---|---|
| *In the name of progress, it is of the utmost significance to recognise that the technology has become a fundamental pillar of the modern educational sector.* | *Technology has become central to modern education.* |
| *I visited my friends old from secondary school.* | *I visited my **old friends** from secondary school.* |
| *I eat never at the canteen.* | *I **never eat** at the canteen.* |
| *During the holidays I have too much eaten.* | *During the holidays I **have eaten too much**.* |

**Register note for band 7:** the descriptors ask for *"an awareness of style and collocation
… though inappropriacies occur"* (band 7 LR). They do not reward grandeur. Precision beats
elevation, and elevation costs sentence length, which costs punctuation accuracy.

### F7. Linker overuse — a CC cost, logged here because the cause is French

*De plus*, *en outre*, *par ailleurs*, *ainsi* are high-frequency in French academic writing.
Transferred, they produce a *Moreover* / *Furthermore* / *In addition* at the head of every
paragraph and often every sentence. This is **not** a GRA error. It is a **Coherence &
Cohesion** error, and the descriptor names it: at band 6, cohesion *"may be faulty or
mechanical due to misuse, **overuse** or omission"*; at band 5, *"There may be limited/overuse
of cohesive devices with some inaccuracy."* Band 7 requires cohesive devices *"used flexibly
but with some inaccuracies or some over/under use"* — flexibly, not mechanically.

Rule of thumb: if a linker could be deleted without the reader losing the thread, delete it.

### F8. French self-check drill — the nine-word sweep (45 seconds)

1. **The nine words (15 s).** information · advice · research · equipment · feedback ·
   knowledge · software · furniture · evidence. Scan for any of them carrying an `-s`. Delete
   the `-s` and fix the verb if it followed.
2. **Verb + preposition (15 s).** Find every verb followed by a preposition. Check the five
   that matter: depend **on** · participate **in** · discuss **Ø** · married **to** · arrive
   **in/at**. Add: responsible **for**, interested **in**, consists **of**, suffer **from**.
3. **The false-friend five (10 s).** Search for *actually*, *eventually*, *assist*, *permit
   to*, *significative*. Each one is wrong more often than right in your writing.
4. **`since` / `for` (5 s).** Point at each one. Point in time → *since*. Length of time →
   *for*.

## The article decision procedure

Arabic has a definite article but no indefinite one; French has both but distributes them
differently. Both interfere. The research literature is consistent that the **indefinite**
article is the harder acquisition problem for Arabic L1 (no L1 form to map onto), while the
**definite** article shows facilitative transfer in specific contexts and negative transfer in
generic ones. Roothooft & Breeze put the band 6→7 discontinuity at χ²=80.1, the largest in
their study.

The procedure below is a decision tree, not a rule list. Run it on the **head noun** of the
phrase.

```
STEP 1 — Is the head noun COUNTABLE?
   NO (uncountable: information, advice, research, equipment, evidence,
       knowledge, feedback, software, furniture, water, money, traffic,
       education, pollution, progress, work…)
       → NEVER "a/an". NEVER plural.
       → Go to STEP 3 to decide "the" vs zero.
   YES → STEP 2

STEP 2 — Singular or plural?
   → Note it. It changes only the ZERO-article branch below.
   → STEP 3

STEP 3 — Is this GENERIC (the class as a whole) or SPECIFIC (this one / these ones)?

   GENERIC:
      plural countable   → ZERO   ("Governments should invest in railways.")
      uncountable        → ZERO   ("Education reduces poverty.")
      singular countable → avoid. If forced, "a" for a typical member
                                  ("A private car emits far more CO2 per
                                   passenger than a train.")
      ⚠ EXCEPT the lexicalised set in F2: the environment, the government,
        the media, the police, the internet, the economy, the public…

   SPECIFIC → STEP 4

STEP 4 — Can the reader identify WHICH one, right now?
   Identifiable if ANY of these is true:
      • already mentioned (second mention)
      • followed by an "of"-phrase   → the number of students, the impact of
                                        technology, the health of the body
      • followed by a defining relative clause → the measures that were
                                        introduced in 2019
      • superlative or ordinal        → the highest figure, the first stage
      • unique in context             → the sun, the government (of this country)
      • ranking/proportion nouns      → the proportion, the percentage, the
                                        majority, the rate, the total
   YES → "the"
   NO  → singular countable: "a/an"   (first mention)
         plural countable:   ZERO or "some"
         uncountable:        ZERO or "some"
```

### The five patterns that fix most of this reader's article errors

1. **`the + NOUN + of + NOUN` is almost always `the`.** *the number of students*, *the
   proportion of graduates*, *the impact of technology*, *the health of the body*, *the cost
   of housing*, *the role of government*. This one pattern covers a large share of Task 1
   and Task 2 openings. It is also the exact slot where Arabic L1 **omits** the article.
2. **Superlatives and ordinals take `the`.** *the highest figure*, *the largest share*, *the
   first stage*, *the second most common cause*. But: *by far the largest*, and no article
   after a possessive (*its highest level*).
3. **Generic plural = zero, no matter how long the modifier.** *Young people in rural areas
   are…* (not *The young people…*). An adjective, a prepositional phrase, even a whole string
   of modifiers does **not** make a generic noun specific.
4. **Percentages and dates take no article.** *in 2010* (not *in the 2010*); *20 per cent of
   students*; but *the percentage of students* and *the 1990s*.
5. **Second mention takes `the`.** *A congestion charge was introduced in 2003. **The** charge
   raised £2bn in its first decade.* This is the easiest article you will ever get right, and
   it is free range: it also serves the CC criterion's *"flexible use of reference"*.

### Article self-test (90 seconds, on any old draft)

Take a paragraph you wrote a week ago. Underline every noun. Above each, write **C** or **U**
(countable/uncountable), then **G** or **S** (generic/specific). Now derive the article from
the tree and compare with what you actually wrote. Count: errors ÷ noun slots. Under 10% is
band 7/8 territory (Roothooft & Breeze). Above 15% and articles are the reason you are at 6.

## Prioritised error list

Ordering principle, derived in Decode 4: at the **6→7** boundary, both bands sit above the
"impedes communication" line. So severity is not what separates them — **density** is, because
band 7 is defined as *"error-free sentences are frequent"*. The cost of an error is therefore
approximately **(how often it occurs) × (how many sentences it contaminates)**, with a
multiplier where the descriptors name it as a bolded limiter, and a further multiplier where
it damages more than one criterion.

| # | Error | Why it is this expensive | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Articles** (generic *the*; missing *a/an*; missing *the* before *N of N*) | Highest-frequency error in the profile; both L1s push the same way; the single largest band 6→7 discontinuity measured anywhere in the IELTS literature (χ²=80.1, p<0.01). Never impedes communication — which is exactly why it is invisible to the writer and lethal to the error-free count. | Roothooft & Breeze 2019 (Tier 1): 18.4% at band 6 → 7.4% at band 7. Junina 2025, Alhaysony via Junina (Tier 2) |

Also drags CC when connectors are the joint. | Key Assessment Criteria p.4 + IDP grammar article's own worked example (Tier 1); Junina 2025, Abu Rass 2015 (Tier 2) |
| **3** | **Third person -s / subject–verb agreement** | 70.6% error at band 6 vs 7.6% at band 7 — the sharpest cliff of any morpheme. ## 3-minute self-editing protocol

Assumptions: computer-delivered, word count visible. Stop writing at **37 minutes** on Task 2
and **18 minutes** on Task 1. IDP's official advice supports this: *"Aim to finish your Writing
tasks a few minutes early so you can check your responses and correct any errors you notice"*
(Tier 1).

Four passes, run in **this order**, because the order is the priority order above. Do not read
for meaning — you will read what you meant, not what you wrote. Read for one thing at a time.

> **Pass 1 — THE FULL STOP PASS (60 seconds).** Highest yield; do it first.
> Read **only the commas**. At each one: cover the left, is the right a complete sentence?
> Cover the right, is the left a complete sentence? Two complete sentences with only a comma
> between them = splice. Repair with a **subordinator** if you can (*because, although, while,
> since, which*) — that converts an error into a complex structure. Full stop if you cannot.
> Then hunt every *however, therefore, moreover, furthermore, thus, nevertheless*. The mark
> before each one must be a full stop or a semicolon. Never a comma.

> **Pass 2 — THE NOUN PASS (60 seconds).** Point at every noun.
> (a) Is it one of the nine uncountables? Kill any `-s`, kill any `a`.
> (b) Does it have `the`? Is it a generic abstract with no *of*-phrase, relative clause or
> superlative after it? Delete `the`. (Check against the F2 counter-list first — *the
> environment*, *the government*, *the media* keep theirs.)
> (c) Is it a bare singular countable? It needs `a/an` or `the`.
> (d) Is it in the frame `___ of ___`? It almost certainly needs `the`.

> **Pass 3 — THE VERB PASS (45 seconds).**
> (a) Every present-simple verb with a singular subject: is the `-s` there? Watch the verbs
> *inside* relative clauses — that is the slot most often missed.
> (b) Every *people / governments / students / children*: plural verb, no `-s`.
> (c) Any two bare verbs stacked (*are depend*, *do not works*, *can to go*)?
> (d) Any passive on an intransitive verb (*was happened*, *was risen*, *was occurred*)?

> **Pass 4 — THE COMPLEX-SENTENCE AUDIT (15 seconds).** Find your two or three **longest**
> sentences — nothing else. For each:
> • *Although … but*? Delete the *but*.
> • *Despite* + subject + verb? Change to *Despite the fact that*, or nominalise.
> • Participle clause? Is the word right after the comma the thing doing the participle?
> • *which … it* / *who … them* / *where … in it*? Delete the stray pronoun.
> • Second conditional with *would* in the *if*-clause? Change to past simple.

**The rule that makes the protocol work:** in these three minutes you may **delete** and
**repair**. You may not **add**. Adding a clever sentence at 39 minutes is how a band-7 script
becomes a band-6 script — an untested complex structure at the end of the clock is the single
likeliest place for a faulty one to appear, and one faulty complex sentence re-instates the
band-6 limiter you spent the whole essay avoiding.

## MYTHS

Grammar advice that lowers scores, and the mechanism by which it does so.

**1. "More complex sentences means a higher GRA band."**
**Why it lowers scores.** Band 6's limiter is not a lack of complex sentences — it is
*"Examples of more complex structures are not marked by the same level of accuracy as in
simple structures."* Adding complex sentences at your current accuracy makes that limiter more
visible, not less. Empirically: in Roothooft & Breeze, **band 7 had the highest conditional
error rate in the study (27.8%)** and a higher passive error rate than any lower band (8.1%),
purely because band-7 candidates attempt more. The report's own framing: *"error rates for
such structures can be higher in the top bands than in the lower bands"*. Complexity attempted
is not complexity achieved, and only the achieved kind is in the descriptor.

**2. "Aim for 60–70% complex sentences" / "you need N complex sentences per essay."**
**Why it lowers scores.** No official IELTS document states any proportion, ratio or count.
The descriptors say *"a variety of complex structures"* and never quantify. Tariffs like this
circulate on prep blogs (Tier 3) and were rejected here. Chasing a quota makes you write
complex sentences you do not need and cannot check, which is myth #1 with a number attached.

**3. "Never write a simple sentence — they look basic."**
**Why it lowers scores.** The **current** Speaking band-7 descriptor states outright: *"Both
simple and complex sentences are used **effectively** despite some errors."* Band 8 Writing
asks for a *"wide range"*, which includes the short declarative. A well-placed short sentence
is a rhetorical instrument and it is error-free by construction.
**But the counterweight is Tier 1 and equally firm** — IDP: *"even if your answer has no
grammar errors at all, but you have written everything in short sentences, you cannot receive
a high band score for Grammatical Range and Accuracy."* The truth is a range requirement, not
a ban: **all-simple is capped; simple-among-complex is rewarded.**

**4. "Use the passive everywhere — it sounds academic."**
**Why it lowers scores.** IDP, Tier 1: *"Passive voice is common in academic-style writing,
but that doesn't mean you should use it in every sentence."* Three costs: it hides the agent,
and Task Response requires *"a clear and developed position"*; it invites the intransitive
passive (*was happened*), a genuine grammatical error; and Roothooft's band 8 is distinguished
from band 7 not by using passives but by using them **accurately**.

**5. "Memorise ten impressive sentence templates and drop them into any essay."**
**Why it lowers scores.** Band-4 LR names *"inappropriate use of lexical chunks (e.g.
memorised phrases, formulaic language and/or language from the input material)"*. The Key
Assessment Criteria states scripts *"may be penalised if they are a) partly or wholly
plagiarised"*, and band 0 covers answers that are *"totally memorised"*. Templates also
misfire: a frame written for one prompt attached to another produces off-topic content, which
is a Task Response penalty. Learn **patterns** (the shape of a second conditional) not
**sentences**.

**6. "Semicolons, dashes and colons show sophistication."**
**Why it lowers scores.** Punctuation is scored for *"accurate and appropriate use"*, not
exoticism. A misused semicolon is a punctuation error inside the GRA count; a correctly used
full stop is not. There is no descriptor credit for the *variety* of punctuation marks —
"range" in the descriptors refers to structures, not to punctuation.

**7. "Grammar mistakes don't matter as long as the meaning is clear."**
**Why it lowers scores.** Half true, and the half that is false is the expensive half. Errors
that do not impede communication keep you out of bands 3–4 — but bands 6 **and** 7 are both
above the impede-line. What separates them is the frequency of clean sentences. So
non-impeding errors are not harmless; they are *precisely* the thing capping this reader at 6.

**8. "Long sentences show grammatical range."**
**Why it lowers scores.** The Speaking Key Assessment Criteria lists sentence length as **one
of five** range indicators, alongside subordination, verb-phrase complexity, phrase complexity
and word-order variation. A 60-word chain of *and* clauses has length and none of the other
four — and it is the exact output of Arabic paratactic transfer. Length without subordination
is the band-5 profile with more words.

**9. "Fix articles last — they're minor details."**
**Why it lowers scores.** Inverted. Articles produce the largest band 6→7 discontinuity
measured in the IELTS research literature (χ²=80.1), and they are the one error this reader's
two L1s **both** push toward. They are also invisible on re-reading, which is why they need a
dedicated pass rather than a general proofread.

**10. "Use the third conditional and mixed conditionals to show off."**
**Why it lowers scores.** Across 73 IELTS Speaking interviews spanning bands 4 to 8,
Roothooft & Breeze found **zero** third conditionals. It is not a band marker; it is a
three-part structure with a high failure rate and no demonstrated presence in high-band
performance. Meanwhile, of the eight second conditionals produced by band-7 speakers, all
eight contained an error.

**11. "Write the introduction and conclusion last / add a flourish at the end."**
**Why it lowers scores.** Not a grammar myth exactly, but it interacts with one: the last
sentence written under time pressure is the least checked, and an untested complex structure
in the final minute re-instates the band-6 limiter. Finish early and **subtract**.

**12. "Contractions (don't, it's) cost you marks in Writing."**
**Status: [UNVERIFIED].** No Tier 1 IELTS document found in this research names contractions.
The relevant official requirement is an *"academic or semi-formal/neutral style"*, which is a
register question under LR, not a GRA rule. Treat avoiding contractions in Writing as
**sensible register practice**, not as a scored rule — and note that *its/it's* confusion **is**
a real, scored error (named in the British Council grammar article, Tier 1).

## Open questions for verifiers

1. **Is "frequent" (band 7 error-free sentences) quantified anywhere official?** Nothing found.
   The chapter derives its position from the band-8 anchor (*"the majority of sentences are
   error-free"*) plus Roothooft & Breeze's <10% per-feature error rate at bands 7/8. If a Tier 1
   source with a number exists, it should replace the derivation.

2. **Do noun-countability errors (*informations*, *advices*) score under GRA or LR?** The Key
   Assessment Criteria assigns *"errors in word formation"* to **LR** and *"grammatical errors"*
   to **GRA**. A wrongly pluralised uncountable is arguably both. This affects the prioritised
   list (item 4 is ranked partly on the assumption it may cost twice). Needs a ruling.

3. **Roothooft & Breeze (2019) is SPEAKING data.** 73 interviews, unreported L1 distribution.
   Every band-level error rate in this chapter comes from it. How far do the article and
   third-person cut-offs transfer to Writing? The authors themselves recommend future work
   controlling for L1. Any claim in this chapter framed as "the band 6→7 gate" should be
   re-checked against Writing evidence if any exists.

4. **The Speaking descriptor edition problem.** The Cambridge webinars PDF named in the brief
   is the 2008/2013 public version; ielts.org serves a rewritten file created 2025-09-16 whose
   band-7 GRA wording differs materially (see the warning box at the top). Verifiers should
   (a) confirm the ielts.org file is the one used in current examiner training, and (b) audit
   the rest of the repo for anything sourced from the Cambridge file. **MYTHS #3 depends on the
   new wording.**

5. **Sokeng (2014) is Cameroonian Francophone data** — French as second *official* language in
   a multilingual setting, not a Tunisian French/Arabic bilingual. Its error *proportions*
   (23% verb form, 18% prepositions, 5% articles) are used here only as orientation. Note that
   its article figure (5%) is *low*, which conflicts with the picture from the Arabic literature
   and from Roothooft — probably because its corpus was a personal narrative rather than
   academic prose. A better-matched Maghrebi corpus would be worth finding.

6. **Participle clauses, cleft sentences and nominalisation are not named in any Tier 1 IELTS
   document.** Their band-7 value is derived from *"a variety of complex structures"* plus the
   Speaking Key Assessment Criteria range indicators (*"subordinate clauses within clauses and
   phrases"*, *"complexity of other phrases"*, *"move elements around for information focus"*).
   Verify the derivation or downgrade the claims.

7. **The F2 counter-list** (*the environment* / *the government* / *the media* vs zero-article
   *society* / *technology* / *nature*) is assembled from standard usage, not from a cited
   reference grammar. It is the highest-value table in the chapter for this reader and needs a
   grammar-authority check (Cambridge Grammar of English, Swan's *Practical English Usage*, or
   equivalent).

8. **The uncountable list needs a second authority.** British Council LearnEnglish's uncount
   list covers advice, equipment, furniture, knowledge, luggage, machinery, money, news, traffic
   — it does **not** include *information*, *research*, *evidence*, *feedback* or *software*,
   all of which the chapter treats as uncountable. Cambridge Dictionary's grammar pages returned
   HTTP 403 from this environment. Needs a second Tier 2 source.

9. **`a knowledge of`.** The chapter flags that *knowledge* takes the indefinite article in the
   premodified frame *a working knowledge of X*, despite being listed as uncount. Confirm this
   is safe to teach without reintroducing *a knowledge* generally.

10. **Is `discuss about` unambiguously wrong?** Treated here as an error (English *discuss* is
    transitive). Note that the Hong Kong English literature treats *discuss about* as a
    documented regional variant. For IELTS it should be marked wrong, but a verifier may want to
    confirm no examiner-facing source treats it as acceptable variation.

11. **`Despite the fact that`.** Taught here as a correct escape hatch. Some style guides
    discourage it as wordy. Confirm it is not penalised as an inappropriacy under LR.

12. **Comma splice tolerance.** Purdue OWL and the IDP article both treat it as an error. No
    Tier 1 IELTS document was found stating how heavily an examiner weights a splice relative to,
    say, a missing article. The chapter's ranking (splices at #2, above third-person -s) rests on
    the argument that a splice damages two clauses at once. That argument is derived, not sourced.

13. **Cambridge Dictionary grammar pages returned HTTP 403** from this environment
    (`dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/uncountable-nouns`). Retry from another
    environment — it is the natural second authority for open questions 8 and 9.
