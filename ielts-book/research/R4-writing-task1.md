### What Task Achievement actually assesses (the checklist behind the band)

The Key Assessment Criteria PDF (p.2) sets out, in list form, what TA measures for
Academic Task 1. Restated:

1. **Choosing** the key features out of the input.
2. Giving **enough detail** to illustrate those features.
3. Reporting the information, figures and trends **accurately**.
4. **Comparing and contrasting** — highlighting the identifiable trends, principal
   changes or differences — *as opposed to* mechanically describing detail.
5. Using an **appropriate format**.

Point 4 is the whole "group, don't list" doctrine written down by IELTS itself.
Point 3 is why a mis-copied figure is not a cosmetic slip: accuracy of figures is
named as a TA sub-skill, not an LR or GRA one.

### The 150-word rule — the actual mechanism

This is the most misreported area in IELTS coaching. Here is what the current
official documents do and do not say.

**What is official (Tier 1):**

- TA is defined as assessing how fully, appropriately, accurately and relevantly
  the response fulfils the task **"using a minimum of 150 words."** (Key Assessment
  Criteria p.1.) The word count is written into the *definition of the criterion*.
  A short response therefore cannot fully satisfy TA — that is the primary
  mechanism, and it is a **TA effect, not a separate deduction**.
- Length appears explicitly in the band table only at the **bottom**: at band 3
  the Lexical Resource wording attributes an inadequate resource partly to a
  response being significantly under length, and band 3 GRA notes that the length
  may be too short to show any control of sentence forms. So severe shortfall
  contaminates **LR and GRA as well as TA**.
- **20 words or fewer = band 1** on all four criteria. (Explicit, all four columns,
  band 1 row.)
- An examiner comment in the official sample pack, on a Task 2 script rated 5.5,
  says the response "does not meet the minimum word count for Task 2 of 250 words,
  so there is room for further development" — note the framing: the deficiency is
  expressed as *undeveloped content*, not as an arithmetic penalty.

**What is NOT official:**

- There is **no published formula** — no "minus one band", no "X words = Y band".
  Any coaching source giving a per-word tariff is inventing it.
- The pre-2018 fixed penalty (automatic band 5 on TA/TR for under-length) is
  reported by ex-examiner sources as real but **never published by IELTS**, and
  reported as **withdrawn around mid-2018**. `[UNVERIFIED against Tier 1]` — Tier 2
  (IELTS Liz) only. Do not state it as fact in the book; state the mechanism above
  instead.

**Practical instruction for this reader:** the useful rule is not "don't go under
150" but "150 is the floor at which TA can even be fully satisfied." Target
**170–190 words**. Above ~200 you are stealing minutes from Task 2, which is worth
double (F7).

### Can you copy the prompt? (No — and here is the cost)

Triangulated across three Tier 1 items:

1. **Band descriptors, band 1 row, Task Achievement column:** any copied rubric
   must be discounted. (It sits in the band-1 row because that is where a response
   made *entirely* of copied rubric lands, but the discounting principle is applied
   at every band — see 2.)
2. **Official examiner comment, Task 2 sample script rated Band 5.5:** "The first
   five lines of this response are directly copied rubric; no credit is given for
   copied rubric." A second sample, rated 6.5, is criticised because the opening is
   "mainly copied from the rubric", and the examiner adds that this "reveals a lack
   of ability to paraphrase" — so copying costs **twice**: discounted words *and*
   an LR judgement.
3. **Official IELTS teacher-facing article on Task 1:** advises avoiding copying
   directly from the prompt "as this doesn't count toward word requirements."

**Consequence to state plainly:** copied prompt wording is struck out before the
word count is taken. A 165-word answer whose first 25 words are lifted from the
prompt is a **140-word answer** — i.e. under length — and simultaneously signals
weak paraphrase to the LR examiner. Copying the *title* of the chart is the
commonest way a candidate silently drops below 150.

Separately, band 4 Lexical Resource explicitly names inappropriate use of lexical
chunks — memorised phrases, formulaic language, **and language from the input
material** — as a band-4 feature. That is the official hook for the anti-template
argument in MYTHS below.

### Time budget inside the 60 minutes

Official guidance is "no more than 20" + "no more than 40", which sums to exactly
60 and leaves zero slack. A workable internal budget for Task 1 (Tier 2 consensus,
derivable from F2):

| Minutes | Action |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:03 | Read prompt. Identify visual type, units, time frame, and **whether there is a time axis at all** (this decides tense). Circle/mentally mark the biggest, the smallest, the biggest *change*, and any crossover or outlier. |
| 0:03–0:05 | Decide the **grouping** — the two body paragraphs — and write the overview sentence in your head before typing anything. |
| 0:05–0:17 | Write. Introduction (1 sentence) → overview (2 sentences) → body 1 → body 2. |
| 0:17–0:20 | Check: word count (F13 gives it free on screen), figures against the visual, tense consistency, subject–verb agreement, articles. |

If you overrun, **stop and move to Task 2**. A Task 1 at 6.0 with a Task 2 at 7.0
gives Writing 6.5 (F7); the reverse gives 6.5 too, but running Task 1 to 28 minutes
usually produces a Task 2 at 5.5, which gives 6.0.

## Visual types

Every subsection: **demands · structure · tense · language · trap.**

The generic four-paragraph shape below holds for all seven types; what changes is
the grouping principle, the tense, and the verb family.

```
¶1  Introduction   — 1 sentence, paraphrase of the prompt. No data.
¶2  Overview       — 2 sentences, "Overall, …". No data (or at most one
                     anchor figure). This is the band-7 gate.
¶3  Body 1         — first group, with supporting figures.
¶4  Body 2         — second group, with supporting figures.
```

No conclusion paragraph. But note the nuance in **Strategies** below: the *overview*
may legitimately sit at the end instead of after the introduction — official IELTS
teacher guidance says a closing paragraph is not required "unless the overview
wasn't included in the introduction." What is never wanted is an opinion,
recommendation, or cause.

### 1. Line graph

**Demands.** Change over time, usually 2–6 series. The task is *trend*, not
*value*. TA sub-skill 4 (compare/contrast rather than mechanically describe) bites
hardest here, because a line graph invites walking along the x-axis.

**Structure.** Group by *trend behaviour*, not by series order in the legend:
paragraph of everything that rose, paragraph of everything that fell/was flat. If
lines cross, the crossover is a key feature and belongs in the overview.

**Tense.** Past simple for a dated past range. Present perfect if the range runs to
"now"/"the present day". Future forms (*is projected to*, *is expected to reach*)
for the projected tail of the line — and you must **switch tense mid-answer** at the
projection boundary, which is itself a GRA-range opportunity. If the graph is
undated ("a typical day"), use present simple.

**Language.** Full trend toolkit below. Line graphs are the one type where
*fluctuate*, *peak*, *bottom out*, *level off*, *plateau*, *rebound* all genuinely
apply.

**Trap.** Describing all four lines separately, in legend order, in four
paragraphs. That is the "mechanical recounting of detail" the band-5 TA descriptor
names, and the official Band-6 Task 1A examiner comment criticises "reporting is
somewhat mechanical".

---

### 2. Bar chart

**Demands.** Two sub-species and they behave differently:
- **Time-series bars** — treat exactly as a line graph (trend verbs, past simple).
- **Categorical bars, no time axis** — treat as a table (comparison language,
  superlatives, present simple; **no trend verbs at all**).

**Structure.** Categorical: group by magnitude band (the dominant few / the middle
/ the negligible), or by a natural category split (male vs female, developed vs
developing). Grouped/stacked bars: decide whether the story is *between groups* or
*within groups* and commit to one as the primary axis of your two body paragraphs.

**Tense.** Past simple if dated in the past; present simple if undated or a
timeless comparison.

**Language.** Comparison and proportion toolkits; superlatives; *outstripped*,
*lagged behind*, *was more than double*, *accounted for*.

**Trap.** Importing rise/fall vocabulary into a chart with no time axis. "*Sales
increased in Germany compared to France*" is a lexical error — the correct verb is
*were higher in Germany than in France*. This is a genuine band-6 LR marker
("a lack of precision in word choice") and it is very common.

---

### 3. Pie chart

**Demands.** Composition of a whole. Almost always ≥2 pies (two years, or two
populations), so the real task is **comparing two compositions**, not describing
one.

**Structure.** Body 1 = the categories that grew / are dominant across both pies.
Body 2 = the categories that shrank / are marginal. Overview = which slice
dominates each pie, and which slice moved most.

**Tense.** Past simple for dated pies; present simple for undated. Comparisons
between two dated pies take past simple throughout.

**Language.** Proportion toolkit is the star: fractions, *made up*, *accounted for*,
*constituted*, *represented*, *the largest share*, *the remainder*. Percentage-point
vs percent distinction (below) is a real precision marker.

**Trap.** Reading off all six slices of both pies in order — twelve figures, no
comparison. Also: if the pies are percentages, you cannot say the *number* rose;
you can only say the *share* rose. Saying "the number of car users increased" from
a pie chart of percentages is a **factual TA error** (sub-skill 3, accuracy).

---

### 4. Table

**Demands.** Tables carry the most raw numbers and the least built-in visual story,
so **selection** is the whole game. Tables may or may not have a time dimension.

**Structure.** Work rows and columns: for each row and each column, note the
biggest and the smallest. Ignore mid-range values entirely — that is Simon's
explicit rule (Tier 2), and it is derivable from the descriptor's demand that
*data are appropriately categorised*.

**Tense.** Whatever the table's time frame dictates. Undated tables → present
simple.

**Language.** Superlatives, comparatives, ratio and multiple language, ranking
language (*ranked first*, *came second*, *at the other end of the scale*).

**Trap.** The table is the easiest place to slide into a 200-word list of numbers
and still be at TA 5, because you have "covered" everything but categorised
nothing. **The overview for a table must say something that is not in any single
cell** — e.g. *the same country led on every measure except one*.

---

### 5. Process diagram — man-made

**Demands.** A sequence of stages with a defined start and end. Count the stages;
name the input and the output. There are no figures, so **the trend/comparison
toolkit is entirely irrelevant** and the language burden shifts to sequencing and
the passive.

**Structure.** Introduction (paraphrase, state the number of stages) → overview
(name the *phases*, not the count) → body 1 (first half of the stages) → body 2
(second half).

**Tense.** **Present simple passive** throughout, unless the diagram is explicitly
historical.

**Language.** Sequencers + passive + specific process verbs (see toolkit). Precision
of verb matters: *filtered*, *compacted*, *moulded*, *fired*, *assembled*,
*transported* score higher than *put in* / *made* / *done*.

**Trap — and this one is documented in official evidence.** The official Band-8.5
process script (Task 1C, Script C) is docked *from 9* for exactly one thing: its
overview said only that there are "seven consecutive steps". The examiner's note is
that "for the highest score, a fuller overview would be needed, to summarise those
key stages, for example; extracting the clay, then shaping, drying and delivering
the bricks." **Counting the stages is not an overview.** Naming the phases is.

Second documented trap from the same pack: Task 1C Script B is language-wise
excellent (the examiner records a wide range of sophisticated lexis, majority of
sentences error-free) but was **held to Band 7 overall** because of a letter-style
opening, personal comments, and no clear overview — "the flaws in format and
organisation limit the rating for this response to Band 7." Format is a TA cap,
independent of your English.

---

### 6. Process diagram — natural (cycle / life cycle)

**Demands.** Same as man-made, but usually **cyclical** — no true end point — and
often **agentless in a different way**: nature does the acting.

**Structure.** Same four paragraphs. The overview must state that the process is
**cyclical** and name where the cycle can be said to begin.

**Tense.** Present simple, but here **active voice is often more accurate than
passive**, because there is a natural agent: *water evaporates from the ocean*,
*the larva hatches*, *the caterpillar spins a cocoon*. Only use the passive where
something genuinely is done to the subject (*the eggs are laid on a leaf*).
Blanket-passivising a natural cycle produces unnatural English and is an LR/GRA
precision cost.

**Language.** Cycle language: *the cycle begins when…*, *this in turn triggers*,
*at which point*, *the process then repeats*, *thereby completing the cycle*.

**Trap.** Treating a cycle as a line and ending the answer with "finally". If it is
a cycle, the last sentence must return to the start.

---

### 7. Map comparison

**Demands.** Two (occasionally three) plans of the same place at different dates.
The task is **change**, plus what stayed the same. Purely spatial — no figures.

**Structure.** Two options, both defensible:
- **By area** (north half / south half) — usually stronger, because it groups.
- **By time** (map 1, then map 2) — safer but risks two separate descriptions with
  no comparison.
Overview: the overall character shift (*rural → residential*, *industrial →
leisure*), plus the single biggest structural change and the one thing untouched.

**Tense.** Set by the dates on the maps — this is the type where tense choice is
most often wrong:

| Map dates | Tense |
|---|---|
| past → past (1950 and 1980) | past simple passive: *a car park **was built*** |
| past → present/"today" | present perfect passive: *the woodland **has been cleared*** |
| present → future/"proposed" | future / modal passive: *a marina **will be constructed***, *is due to be developed* |
| describing what exists in one map | past or present simple, active: *the village **stood** on the coast* |

**Language.** Compass and location toolkit (below) + change verbs
(*was demolished, was replaced by, was converted into, was relocated to, was
extended, gave way to, made way for*) + continuity verbs (*remained unchanged, was
retained, was left intact*).

**Trap.** Not saying what stayed the same. Also: forgetting that if no compass rose
is drawn, **north is the top of the map** — you may and should use compass language
anyway.

---

### 8. Mixed / multiple charts

**Demands.** Two different visuals (e.g. a pie plus a bar chart, or a line graph
plus a table) on a related theme. Officially sanctioned by the format page's
phrasing "one or more graphs, charts or tables" (F12).

**Structure.** The mistake is writing two mini-reports. The band-7 move is **one
integrated overview covering both visuals**, then one body paragraph per visual —
with at least one sentence that **links** them (*the sharp rise in ¶3 was driven
largely by the category that dominates the second chart*). Simon's rule (Tier 2):
try to summarise all the information in a single overview rather than a separate
summary for each chart.

**Tense.** Whatever each visual requires; **switching tense between paragraphs is
correct here**, not a mistake, as long as it is switching for a reason.

**Trap.** Time. Two visuals in 20 minutes means fewer figures per visual, not more
words. Also: inventing a causal link between the two charts. You may *point out*
that two figures correspond; you may not say one **caused** the other (F11).

## Language toolkits

### A. Trend verbs (movement over time)

| Direction | Neutral | Strong | Very strong / abrupt |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Up** | rise, increase, go up, grow, climb | surge, jump, leap, shoot up | rocket, soar, skyrocket |
| **Down** | fall, decrease, decline, drop, go down, dip | plunge, slump, tumble | plummet, collapse, crash |
| **Flat** | remain stable / steady / constant / unchanged, stay the same, hold steady | level off, plateau, flatten out, reach a plateau | — |
| **Irregular** | fluctuate, vary, oscillate, swing | — | — |
| **Turning points** | peak (at), reach a peak/high (of), hit a low, bottom out, trough | — | — |
| **Recovery** | recover, rebound, bounce back, pick up | — | — |

**Grammar notes that decide LR/GRA marks:**
- *rise / fall / increase / decrease / grow / decline* here are **intransitive** —
  no object, no passive. ✗ *The number was increased.* ✓ *The number increased.*
- *peak* takes **at**: *peaked at 40%*. *Reach a peak* takes **of**: *reached a
  peak of 40%*.
- *rise* is irregular: rise / **rose** / **risen**. ✗ *raised*. *Raise* is
  transitive and means "lift something".
- *level off* and *plateau* mean *stop rising*, not *fall*. Using them for a fall
  is an accuracy error.

### B. Adverbs of degree and speed

| Category | Items |
|---|---|
| Large change | dramatically, sharply, steeply, markedly, significantly, considerably, substantially, drastically |
| Moderate change | moderately, noticeably, appreciably |
| Small change | slightly, marginally, minimally, negligibly, a little |
| Fast | rapidly, swiftly, abruptly, suddenly |
| Slow / even | steadily, gradually, progressively, consistently, slowly |
| Irregular | erratically, unevenly, wildly |

**Collocation warnings (these are the ones that read as wrong to an examiner):**
- ✗ *increased hardly* — *hardly* means "almost not". Use *sharply* / *steeply*.
- ✗ *rose importantly* — not a degree adverb in English. Use *significantly*.
- ✗ *decreased strongly* — use *sharply* or *steeply*.
- ✗ *fluctuated dramatically over the ten years* is fine; ✗ *fluctuated steadily*
  is a contradiction.
- *significantly* is a strong word. If the change is 2%, say *marginally*.
  Overreaching on degree adverbs is a **TA accuracy** problem, not just LR.

### C. The two grammatical shapes — alternate them deliberately

This is the single cheapest GRA-range win in Task 1. Every trend can be expressed
two ways, and a band-7 script does both.

| Verb + adverb | there + be + adjective + noun |
|---|---|
| Sales **rose sharply**. | **There was a sharp rise in** sales. |
| Numbers **fell steadily**. | **There was a steady fall in** numbers. |
| The figure **fluctuated wildly**. | **There were wild fluctuations in** the figure. |
| Output **grew gradually**. | Output **saw a gradual growth**. / **A gradual growth in** output **occurred**. |

Third and fourth shapes worth having (these push toward band 7–8 range):

- **Subject-as-change:** *The steepest decline occurred between 1995 and 2000.*
- **Participle clause:** *…, rising to a peak of 40% in 2004 before falling back.*
- **Nominalised comparison:** *The rise in car use was matched by an equivalent fall
  in walking.*
- **Inverted/fronted:** *Of the four categories, only cycling showed a net decline.*

Adjective↔adverb pairs to keep straight: sharp/sharply, steady/steadily,
gradual/gradually, dramatic/dramatically, marked/markedly, slight/slightly,
significant/significantly, marginal/marginally, rapid/rapidly, steep/steeply.

### D. Comparison language

| Function | Items |
|---|---|
| Simple comparative | higher/lower than, greater/smaller than, more/fewer than |
| Degree of difference | slightly higher than, marginally lower than, considerably greater than, far more than, nowhere near as high as |
| Multiples | twice as many as, three times higher than, four times the figure for, double, treble, tenfold |
| Fractional | half as many as, a third of, only a fraction of |
| Superlative | the highest/lowest, the largest/smallest, the greatest, the most/least common, ranked first, came bottom |
| Contrast connectors | whereas, while, by contrast, in contrast, conversely, on the other hand, however |
| Similarity | similarly, likewise, in the same way, comparable to, on a par with, roughly equivalent to |
| Overtake / trail | outstripped, overtook, surpassed, exceeded, lagged behind, trailed, fell short of |
| Gap language | the gap between X and Y widened/narrowed, the disparity, the margin |

**Grammar notes:**
- *twice as many **as***, not *than*. ✓ *twice as many as* · ✓ *twice the number of*
  · ✗ *twice more than*.
- Countable → *more/fewer*, *the number of*. Uncountable → *more/less*, *the amount
  of*. *The number of cars* / *the amount of electricity*.
- *compared with* and *compared to* are both fine; **✗ *comparing to***.
- After *whereas* and *while* you need a **full clause**: ✓ *whereas France
  declined* · ✗ *whereas in France a decline*.
- *the double* is French. English: *double the figure* / *twice the figure*.

### E. Proportion language

| Function | Items |
|---|---|
| Verbs | account for, make up, constitute, represent, comprise, form |
| Fractions | a quarter, a third, two thirds, three quarters, half |
| Vague quantities | the vast majority, a substantial majority, a significant proportion, a sizeable minority, a small minority, a negligible/tiny proportion |
| Rates | one in five, two out of every three, a ratio of 3:1 |
| Residual | the remainder, the rest, the balance |
| Share nouns | share, proportion, percentage, fraction, segment, slice |

**Precision points that separate 6 from 7:**
- **Percentage vs percentage point.** From 20% to 30% is a rise of **10 percentage
  points**, or a rise of **50 per cent**. Saying "rose by 50%" when you mean points
  is a TA accuracy error. If unsure, write *rose from 20% to 30%* and dodge it.
- *account for* takes a percentage/share, not a raw count of the whole:
  ✓ *cars accounted for 40% of trips*.
- *comprise* takes no *of*: ✓ *the group comprises four categories* ·
  ✗ *comprises of*.
- ✓ *the majority of students **are*** (plural agreement after majority + plural).

### F. Approximation language

| Position | Items |
|---|---|
| Before a figure | approximately, around, about, roughly, nearly, almost, just over, just under, slightly more than, a little under, some (some 40,000), in the region of, in the vicinity of, close to, well over, upwards of |
| After a figure | or so, or thereabouts |
| Adjectival | an approximate figure of, an estimated |
| Range | between 20 and 25, in the range of, from … to … |

**Why it earns marks:** approximation lets you report a figure you cannot read
exactly *without* being inaccurate, which protects TA sub-skill 3. It also supplies
LR variety at zero risk. Rotate them — using *approximately* six times is the
"restricted range" the band-6 LR descriptor describes.

**Warning:** *nearly*, *almost* and *just under* mean **below** the figure;
*just over*, *well over*, *upwards of* mean **above**. Using the wrong side is an
accuracy error, not a style choice.

### G. Sequencing and passive — processes

**Sequencers (rotate; do not head every sentence with one):**

| Stage | Items |
|---|---|
| Start | initially, in the first stage, to begin with, the process begins with/when, at the outset |
| Middle | then, next, subsequently, after this, following this, at this point, at the next stage, thereafter, meanwhile, simultaneously, in parallel |
| Conditional/temporal | once X has been completed, after X is/has been -ed, when the mixture has cooled, as soon as |
| End | finally, lastly, in the final stage, the process ends with, at which point the cycle repeats |

**Passive patterns:**

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| present simple passive | The clay **is extracted** from the ground. |
| passive + adverb of manner | The mixture **is then thoroughly heated**. |
| passive + purpose infinitive | The pulp **is pressed to remove** excess water. |
| passive + agent (rare, only if shown) | The sheets **are cut by** a rotating blade. |
| present perfect passive (sequencing) | Once the bricks **have been fired**, they are packed. |
| passive + *before/after* + -ing | The glass **is crushed before being melted**. |
| non-finite reduced clause | The material, **having been dried**, moves to the kiln. |

**Process verbs with precision (LR gold):** extracted, quarried, mined, harvested ·
crushed, ground, pulverised, shredded · mixed, blended, combined · heated, fired,
baked, kilned, smelted · cooled, chilled, left to set · filtered, sieved, strained,
purified · moulded, shaped, cast, pressed · assembled, welded, bonded · dried,
cured, seasoned · packaged, stored, distributed, transported.

**Do not** write *put*, *made*, *done*, *goes in* for these. Verb precision is the
main LR lever a process task offers you, because there is no data vocabulary to use.

### I. Introduction paraphrase kit

Paraphrase by swapping **the reporting verb**, **the noun phrase**, and **the time
expression** — leave numbers and proper nouns alone.

| Prompt word | Replace with |
|---|---|
| shows | illustrates, depicts, presents, gives information on, provides data on, compares, sets out, charts |
| the chart / graph | the bar chart, the line graph, the table, the diagram, the plan (name it exactly) |
| the number of people who… | the figures for… / the proportion of the population who… |
| between 1990 and 2010 | over a twenty-year period · over two decades · in the two decades to 2010 |
| in Britain | in the UK · across Britain |

Do **not** paraphrase a technical term that has no synonym (a country name, a
measure like "carbon dioxide emissions"). Forcing a synonym there is a bigger LR
error than repeating the word.

## Band descriptor decoder

All wording below is **restated in my own words** from the May 2023 official Task 1
descriptors (pages 3–5 of the official PDF). One short official phrase is quoted per
criterion, as permitted.

### Task Achievement

| Band | What the descriptor requires (paraphrased) |
|---|---|
| **8** | Everything the task asks for is covered appropriately, relevantly and in sufficient depth. Key features are chosen with skill, made prominent, and illustrated. Only the odd omission or slip in content. |
| **7** | The task's requirements are covered; content is relevant and accurate with at most a few omissions or slips; the format is right. The features chosen are covered and clearly foregrounded, though they could have been illustrated or extended more fully. And, for Academic: a clear overview is present, **"the data are appropriately categorised"**, and the main trends or differences are identified. |
| **6** | The response is focused on what the task asks and the format is appropriate. The features chosen are covered and given adequate prominence. **An overview is attempted, and is relevant.** Information is selected appropriately and backed with figures. But some detail may be irrelevant, inappropriate or inaccurate, especially when illustrating or extending the main points; and some detail may be missing or, conversely, excessive. |
| **5** | The task is addressed only in a general way and the format may go wrong in places. The features chosen are not covered adequately. Detail is recounted mechanically. There may be no figures at all. There is a tendency to fixate on detail without stepping back to the bigger picture. Irrelevant or inaccurate material in key areas drags the rating down. |

**The 6→7 gate, stated exactly.** Three things separate them, and all three must be
present for 7:

1. The overview must be **clear**, not merely *attempted*.
2. **The data must be categorised** — i.e. grouped. This is the load-bearing 2023
   addition. Band 7 explicitly demands categorisation; band 6 only demands
   selection. A script that selects the right figures but presents them in
   x-axis order is a band-6 script *by the wording of the descriptor*.
3. The **main trends or differences must be identified** — named as trends, not
   left for the reader to infer from a list of numbers.

**Why the overview is the highest-leverage element in the whole task.** Follow the
ladder:

- No overview at all → band 6 cannot be awarded, because band 6 requires that a
  relevant overview be attempted. **TA is capped at 5.**
- Overview attempted but vague, partial, or just a count of items → **TA 6**.
- Overview clear, data grouped, trends named → **TA 7**.

That is a **two-band swing on 25% of the task**, bought with two sentences that take
about ninety seconds. Nothing else in Task 1 has that ratio.

The official examiner comments confirm it operationally. Task 1B Sample Script A was
rated **Band 6**, and the examiner wrote: *"In order to gain a higher mark for
content, the candidate would be expected to summarise the most important features of
the graph in an overview, for example, 'audiences for radio peak in the morning, and
for TV, in the evening'."* That is the whole 6→7 fix, in one sentence, supplied by
an official examiner.

**Two documented caveats — put both in the book, because the naive version is
wrong:**

- *Capped ≠ impossible.* Task 1C Script B was rated **Band 7 overall despite having
  no clear overview**, because CC/LR/GRA were at band 8–9 level. TA was capped and
  the other three carried it. So "no overview = no 7" is **false as stated**; the
  true statement is "no overview caps *Task Achievement* at 5, which for a candidate
  whose other criteria are 6–7 makes an overall 7 arithmetically unreachable."
- *A count is not an overview.* Task 1C Script C, rated **8.5**, was told that
  saying the process had "seven consecutive steps" was not enough — a fuller
  overview naming the actual phases was needed for the top band.

**Before → after, TA (line graph, four series):**

> ✗ **6.** *In 1990 walking was 13 million and car was 4 million. In 2000 walking
> was 9 million and car was 8 million. In 2010 walking was 6 million and car was 11
> million.*
> — Accurate, but this is mechanical recounting in x-axis order: no categorisation,
> no trend named. Band-5/6 territory.

> ✓ **7.** *Overall, the two dominant modes exchanged places over the period: car
> travel rose steadily throughout while walking declined by a comparable margin,
> and by 2010 the ranking of 1990 had been reversed. The remaining two modes
> changed far less.*
> — Same data. Now it is grouped ("the two dominant modes" vs "the remaining two"),
> the trend is named, and the relationship between the two series is stated. Nothing
> rare or hard was used.

### Coherence & Cohesion

| Band | What the descriptor requires (paraphrased) |
|---|---|
| **8** | The reader follows it with no effort. Information and ideas are sequenced logically and every aspect of cohesion is handled well. Occasional lapses only. Paragraphing is sufficient and appropriate. |
| **7** | Information and ideas are organised logically and there is clear progression right through, with at most a few lapses. A range of cohesive devices — **including reference and substitution** — is used flexibly, though with some inaccuracy or some over- or under-use. |
| **6** | Information is generally arranged coherently and the overall progression is clear. Cohesive devices work to some good effect, but cohesion within or between sentences may be **"faulty or mechanical"** through misuse, overuse or omission. Reference and substitution may lack flexibility or clarity, producing repetition or error. |
| **5** | Organisation is visible but not wholly logical and overall progression may be lacking, though an underlying coherence survives. The relationship between ideas can be followed but sentences are not fluently linked. Cohesive devices are limited or overused, with some inaccuracy. Repetition results from weak reference and substitution. |

**The 6→7 gate.** Two moves, and the second is the one candidates never do:

1. Stop bolting a connector onto every sentence. *Moreover / Furthermore / In
   addition* at the head of three consecutive sentences is precisely the
   "mechanical" cohesion band 6 describes. The official Band-7.5 Task 2 sample was
   *criticised* for overusing sequencers, and a Band-6 Task 1 sample was marked down
   for connectives that were "overused or inappropriate" — overuse is penalised at
   every band.
2. **Use reference and substitution.** Band 7 names them explicitly; band 6 says
   they lack flexibility. Reference = *this*, *these*, *the former*, *the latter*,
   *the same period*, *both*, *such a shift*, *doing so*. Substitution = replacing a
   repeated noun phrase rather than restating it. This is the cheapest CC upgrade
   available and almost no candidate trains it.

**Before → after, CC:**

> ✗ **6.** *Firstly, car use increased from 4 to 11 million. Moreover, walking
> decreased from 13 to 6 million. Furthermore, cycling decreased from 4 to 2
> million. In addition, bus use decreased from 7 to 5 million.*
> — Four sentences, four bolted-on connectors, zero reference, zero substitution,
> and the connectors do not signal a real logical relationship (*moreover* does not
> introduce a contrast).

> ✓ **7.** *Car use more than doubled over the period, climbing from 4 to 11 million
> trips. Walking moved in the opposite direction, falling from 13 million to 6
> million — a decline almost exactly matching the growth in car travel. The two
> remaining modes followed the same downward path, though far less steeply: cycling
> halved to 2 million and bus use slipped from 7 to 5 million.*
> — One explicit connector-free contrast (*the opposite direction*), one reference
> (*the two remaining modes*, *the same downward path*), one substitution
> (*a decline* standing in for the whole preceding clause). Cohesion is doing work
> without announcing itself.

### Lexical Resource

| Band | What the descriptor requires (paraphrased) |
|---|---|
| **8** | A wide resource used fluently and flexibly to convey precise meaning inside the task's scope. Uncommon and idiomatic items are handled skilfully, though word choice and collocation are occasionally inaccurate. Spelling and word-formation errors are occasional and barely affect communication. |
| **7** | The resource is sufficient for some flexibility and precision. There is some ability with less common or idiomatic items. **"An awareness of style and collocation is evident"**, though inappropriacies occur. Only a few spelling or word-formation errors, and they do not detract from clarity. |
| **6** | The resource is generally adequate and appropriate. Meaning comes across in spite of a fairly restricted range or imprecise word choice. A risk-taker will show more range but with more inaccuracy. Some spelling and word-formation errors, but they do not impede communication. |
| **5** | The resource is limited but just about adequate. Simple vocabulary may be accurate, but the range does not allow much variation. Frequent lapses in appropriacy of word choice; inflexibility shows up as repeated simplifications and repetitions. Spelling and word-formation errors are noticeable and can cause the reader difficulty. |

**The 6→7 gate.** *Precision and collocation*, not rarity. Band 7 is awarded for
using the **right** word with awareness of what it habitually combines with. Band 6
explicitly covers the candidate who reaches for wider vocabulary and gets it wrong
("if the writer is a risk-taker…") — the descriptor tells you outright that
thesaurus-reaching does not buy band 7.

Note also the **task-scope hedge** the 2023 wording adds at bands 7–9: precision is
judged *within the scope of the task*. Task 1 has a narrow lexical field. You are
not expected to produce essay vocabulary; you are expected to produce **accurate
data vocabulary**. That is a much smaller thing to learn.

**Before → after, LR:**

> ✗ **6.** *The number of car users augmented significatively, whereas the number of
> people who use the walk decreased strongly. In the other hand, the cycling had a
> important diminution.*
> — *augmented* (French *augmenter*), *significatively* (not a word), *use the walk*
> (collocation), *decreased strongly* (collocation), *in the other hand*
> (preposition), *a important* (article), *diminution* (French register). Five
> word-choice/collocation problems in 30 words.

> ✓ **7.** *Car use rose sharply, whereas the number of journeys made on foot fell
> steeply over the same period. Cycling, by contrast, saw only a marginal decline.*
> — Nothing here is a rare word. *Rose sharply*, *journeys made on foot*, *fell
> steeply*, *a marginal decline*, *by contrast* are all common items in **correct
> collocation**. That is what band 7 LR is.

### Grammatical Range & Accuracy

| Band | What the descriptor requires (paraphrased) |
|---|---|
| **8** | A wide range of structures within the task's scope, used flexibly and accurately. Most sentences contain no errors and punctuation is well handled. Errors are occasional, unsystematic, and barely affect communication. |
| **7** | A variety of complex structures used with some flexibility and accuracy. Grammar and punctuation are generally well controlled and **"error-free sentences are frequent"**. A few grammatical errors persist but do not impede communication. |
| **6** | Simple and complex forms are mixed, but flexibility is limited. The complex structures are noticeably less accurate than the simple ones. Errors in grammar and punctuation occur but rarely impede communication. Spelling/word-formation errors occur without impeding communication. |
| **5** | Range is limited and repetitive. Complex sentences are attempted but tend to be faulty; simple sentences are the accurate ones. Grammatical errors may be frequent and can cause the reader difficulty. Punctuation may be faulty. |

**The 6→7 gate.** Two independent things, and you need both:

1. **Frequency of clean sentences.** Band 7 says error-free sentences are
   *frequent*; band 8 says the *majority* are. So the practical target for 7 is
   roughly half your sentences with no error at all. **This is countable.** After
   every drill, count sentences and count clean sentences. If you have 9 sentences
   and 3 are clean, you are at 6 regardless of how ambitious the other 6 were.
2. **The complex sentences must be as accurate as the simple ones.** Band 6 is
   defined by the gap between them. The band-6 candidate writes clean simple
   sentences and broken complex ones. The fix is *not* more complexity — it is
   complexity you can control.

Cheap, controllable complexity for Task 1 (all four are near-formulaic and low-risk):

| Structure | Example |
|---|---|
| Non-defining relative clause | *Car use, **which had been the least common mode in 1990**, became the most common by 2010.* |
| Participle clause after a main clause | *Numbers climbed steadily, **peaking at 11 million in 2010**.* |
| Fronted adverbial | ***Over the same period**, walking fell by more than half.* |
| Concessive subordinator | ***Although** bus use declined, it remained the second most common mode.* |

**Before → after, GRA:**

> ✗ **6.** *The peoples who travelled by car was increase during this period, and
> the number of childrens walking to school it decrease a lot, and this is a big
> change between the two years and we can see it in the chart.*
> — One 40-word run-on, joined by *and* twice; *peoples*, *childrens*, *was
> increase*, *it decrease*, resumptive pronoun *it*, plus commentary
> ("we can see it in the chart") that adds nothing.

> ✓ **7.** *The number of children travelling by car rose over the period, while
> those walking to school fell substantially. This reversal, which took place
> gradually rather than abruptly, was the most striking change in the chart.*
> — Two sentences instead of one; a participial modifier (*travelling by car*),
> substitution (*those*), a subordinator (*while*), and a non-defining relative
> clause. Both sentences are error-free.

| # | Strategy | Tier | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 | Four paragraphs: introduction (1 sentence) → overview → 2 detail paragraphs. "General to specific." | 2 (two independent experts) | IELTS Simon; IELTS Advantage ("pyramid structure") |
| S2 | Put the overview **immediately after the introduction**; a closing overview is also acceptable. | 1 + 2 | Official IELTS teacher article ("Conclusion not required unless the overview wasn't included in the introduction"); Simon ("usually written straight after the introduction, though you can also put it at the end") |
| S3 | **No figures in the overview.** Numbers are detail; the overview is the general picture. | 2 (two independent experts) | IELTS Simon; IELTS Advantage. Derivable from the descriptor: band 7 wants *trends identified* and *data categorised*, which is a level above individual figures. |
| S4 | Pick 3–4 main features for the overview, then take those same features into the body paragraphs and support each with figures. | 2 | IELTS Advantage. Strong: it forces the overview and the body to agree, which is what "clear progression" means in CC. |
| S5 | **Data selection rule for tables/bar charts:** mark the biggest and the smallest in every row and column, plus the biggest *change* over time. **Ignore mid-range values.** | 2 | IELTS Simon. Directly derivable from Tier 1: TA sub-skill 1 is *selecting* key features, and band 7 demands categorisation, not coverage. |
| S6 | **Group, do not list.** Grouping principles: shared trend · similar magnitude · biggest vs smallest · the outlier · natural category split. | 1 | Key Assessment Criteria p.2 names "comparing or contrasting … (rather than mechanical description reporting detail)"; band 7 TA requires the data to be categorised. | S7 | **Why listing lowers the score, precisely.** A complete list (a) fails the categorisation requirement → TA capped at 6; (b) forces repetitive sentence patterns → GRA range down; (c) forces repetition of the same nouns and verbs → LR range down and CC repetition; (d) burns words on mid-range data, leaving none for the notable features. One habit, four criteria. | 1 (derived) | Descriptors, all four columns |
| S8 | Decide tense **from the visual, before writing** — this is a 5-second check that prevents a systematic GRA error running through 180 words. | 1 + 2 | IDP question-types article; IELTS Liz ("always check map dates to determine tenses") |
| S9 | For maps, **group by area, not by map**, so that comparison is built into the structure. | 2 | Multiple map guides; consistent with S6 |
| S10 | For multiple/mixed charts, write **one integrated overview** covering both visuals rather than a summary per visual. | 2 | IELTS Simon |
| S11 | For processes, state the **number of stages** in the introduction and the **names of the phases** in the overview. | 1 | Official examiner comment on the Band-8.5 process script |
| S12 | Say what **did not change** (maps, and any chart with a flat series). Stability is a main feature. | 2 | IELTS Liz ("focus on what changes and what remains constant"); supported by band 7 TA "main trends **or differences**" |

## MYTHS

Advice that is common, and that **lowers** a 6.5–7.5 script. Each with the mechanism.

| Myth | Why it costs marks |
|---|---|
| **"Memorise a Task 1 template and slot the data in."** | Band 4 Lexical Resource explicitly names inappropriate use of lexical chunks — memorised phrases and formulaic language — as a band-4 feature. Band 0 is reserved in part for a totally memorised response. A template also cannot adapt: the same frame applied to a map or a process produces format errors, and format errors cap TA (documented: Task 1C Script B, held to Band 7 partly for inappropriate format). |
| **"Start with 'The given graph illustrates…' / 'It is apparent from the illustration that…'."** | Not fatal alone, but these are the highest-frequency memorised openers in the world; they read as chunk not competence, they consume 8–12 of your 150 words saying nothing, and they add no LR credit because they are not *your* resource. Paraphrase the actual prompt instead. |
| **"Copy the prompt sentence — it saves time and words."** | The exact opposite. Copied rubric is discounted before the word count is taken (official descriptor; confirmed twice in official examiner comments: "no credit is given for copied rubric"). It also demonstrates inability to paraphrase, which is an LR judgement. Net effect: shorter answer *and* lower LR. |
| **"Use as many linking words as possible."** | Band 6 CC is *defined* by cohesion that is faulty or mechanical through **overuse**. Band 8 CC is cohesion that attracts no attention. Linker density is inversely correlated with CC above band 6. An official Band-6 Task 1 comment says connectives were "overused or inappropriate"; an official Band-7.5 Task 2 comment still flags "some overuse of sequencers". |
| **"Use rare/impressive vocabulary — *plummet*, *skyrocket*, *exponentially* — wherever possible."** | Band 7 LR is precision and collocation awareness; band 6 explicitly covers the risk-taker who uses wider vocabulary with more inaccuracy. *Plummeted* for a 3% fall is a **TA accuracy error** as well as an LR one, because the descriptor requires trends to be reported accurately. Match the word to the magnitude. |
| **"Explain why the trend happened — it shows analysis."** | Officially excluded. The task relates "narrowly to the factual content" of the visual, "not to speculative explanations that lie outside the given data" (Key Assessment Criteria p.2). Causal speculation is irrelevant content, which the band-6 TA descriptor treats as a *limiting* feature. It also spends words you needed for figures. |
| **"Give your opinion or a recommendation at the end."** | Same mechanism. Also documented: Task 1C Script B was penalised on format partly for "personal comments". |
| **"Write a conclusion paragraph."** | Redundant with the overview and usually a restatement, i.e. repetition (a named band-5/6 feature). *Unless* you chose to place the overview at the end, which is legitimate. What is never legitimate is a conclusion that adds opinion, cause, or prediction. |
| **"Describe every single data point so you don't miss anything."** | See S7. It fails the band-7 categorisation requirement and simultaneously flattens LR, GRA and CC. Coverage is a band-5 virtue; selection is a band-7 one. |
| **"Write 300 words on Task 1 — longer is better."** | There is no upper limit and no length reward, but there is a hard 60-minute budget, and Task 2 is worth double (F7). Twelve extra minutes on Task 1 typically costs a full band on Task 2, which costs twice as much. Also, more words = more error opportunities, and GRA at band 7 is judged on the *frequency* of clean sentences. |
| **"Use the passive everywhere in a process."** | True for man-made/industrial processes, **wrong for natural cycles**, where there is a genuine agent (*water evaporates*, *the larva hatches*). Blanket passivisation produces unnatural English — a collocation/style problem the band-7 LR descriptor is explicitly watching for. |
| **"Use trend verbs (increase/decrease) for any chart."** | Only valid where there is a time axis. On a categorical bar chart or an undated table, *increase* is factually wrong; you need comparison language. This is a precision error under LR **and** an accuracy error under TA. |
| **"A missing overview means you cannot get band 7."** | Half-true and worth stating correctly. No overview caps **Task Achievement** at 5 (band 6 requires an overview to be attempted). Overall 7 is still arithmetically possible if the other three criteria are 8+ — documented: Task 1C Script B, Band 7 overall with no clear overview. For a candidate whose other criteria sit at 6–7, though, the cap makes 7 unreachable. State the cap, not the absolute. |
| **"An overview is just saying how many categories/stages there are."** | Documented as insufficient by an official examiner at Band 8.5: naming the count was not enough; the phases had to be summarised. A count is a fact about the diagram, not a summary of its content. |
| **"There's a fixed penalty of X bands for being under 150 words."** | No such published rule exists in any current official document. `[UNVERIFIED]` The pre-2018 automatic band-5 penalty is reported only by ex-examiner Tier 2 sources and was reportedly withdrawn. Teach the real mechanism (TA is defined against a 150-word minimum; severe shortfall additionally hits LR and GRA; ≤20 words = band 1). |
| **"Bullet points are fine for a process diagram since it's a list of stages."** | Explicitly penalised: bullet points "in any part of the response" and note form are named as penalisable in the Key Assessment Criteria. |

## L1 alert — Arabic (Tunisian) and French interference, Task 1 specific

These are the general L1 patterns from `CLAUDE.md` narrowed to the constructions
Task 1 actually forces you to produce. Task 1 is unusually dangerous for this
reader because it is dense with **numbers, articles, prepositions and comparatives**
— exactly where French transfer lives.

### 1. Articles with abstract and general nouns

Task 1 constantly needs bare abstract nouns. French keeps the article.

| ✗ | ✓ | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| *the car use increased* | **car use** increased | Compound noun used generally → no article |
| *the tourism grew* | **tourism** grew | Abstract/uncountable in a general sense → no article |
| *the people who travelled by bus* (meaning people in general) | **people** who travelled by bus | Generic plural → no article |
| *the electricity consumption fell* | **electricity consumption** fell | Same family: *energy use, water consumption, public transport, education, employment* |

**But** Task 1 also needs *the* where French might drop it or where candidates
over-correct into dropping it:

| ✗ | ✓ | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| *number of cars rose* | **the** number of cars rose | *the number of* is fixed |
| *proportion of women increased* | **the** proportion of women increased | So are *the percentage of, the amount of, the figure for, the majority of, the rest, the remainder* |
| *in 1990 walking was highest* | walking was **the** highest | Superlatives take *the* |
| *between 1990 and 2010* ✓ | — | No article with bare years — this one is usually right |

Drill sentence: *Overall, **car** use rose while **the** number of children who
walked to school fell to **the** lowest figure recorded.* Three article decisions,
all different.

### 2. Uncountables pluralised

Task 1 vocabulary is full of French-pluralisable uncountables:

**information** (✗ *informations*) · **research** (✗ *researches*) · **data** (see
below) · **evidence** · **equipment** · **transport** · **traffic** ·
**consumption** · **expenditure** · **electricity** · **employment** ·
**pollution** · **waste** · **software** · **furniture** · **feedback** ·
**advice** · **knowledge**.

Say *a piece of information*, *an item of equipment*, *a unit of electricity*.

**Data.** In IELTS Academic prose, treat *data* as **plural** — this matches the
official band-7 descriptor's own wording ("the data **are** appropriately
categorised"). *The data show that…*, not *the data shows*. Singular *data* is
accepted in some registers but plural is unambiguously safe here.

### 3. Prepositions with figures — the highest-value drill in this section

These are near-invisible to you and immediately visible to an examiner. Every one of
these is a French-transfer error.

| Pattern | ✓ | ✗ (French transfer) |
|---|---|---|
| Start and end values | rose **from** 4 million **to** 11 million | *rose of 4 to 11* |
| Size of change | rose **by** 7 million / **by** 40% | *rose of 7 million* (French *augmenter de*) |
| Reached a level | rose **to** 11 million | *rose at 11 million* |
| Peak | peaked **at** 11 million | *peaked to 11 million* |
| Peak (noun) | reached a peak **of** 11 million | *a peak at 11 million* (in noun form) |
| Stability | remained stable **at** 5 million | *stable to 5 million* |
| Time point | **in** 1990 · **in** March · **on** 1 March · **at** 10 a.m. | *at 1990* |
| Time span | **between** 1990 **and** 2010 · **from** 1990 **to** 2010 · **over** the period · **during** the 1990s | *between 1990 to 2010*, *during 20 years* (→ **for/over** 20 years) |
| Proportion | accounted **for** 40% · a rise **in** sales | *accounted 40%*, *a rise of sales* (when you mean *in*) |
| Comparison | compared **with/to** · twice as many **as** | *comparing to*, *twice more than* |
| Difference | the difference **between** X **and** Y | *the difference of X and Y* |
| Dependence | depend **on** | *depend of* (*dépendre de*) |
| Consist | consist **of** | *consist in* |

Note the pair that catches almost everyone: **rise *to* a level** vs **rise *by* an
amount**. *Sales rose to 40%* and *sales rose by 40%* are different facts, and
getting it wrong is a **TA accuracy error**, not just grammar.

### 4. False friends that surface specifically in Task 1

| ✗ | ✓ | Note |
|---|---|---|
| *significative* | **significant** | Not an English word |
| *augmented* | **rose / increased** | *augment* exists but means "supplement", wrong register here |
| *diminution* | **decline / decrease / drop** | French register |
| *evolution* (of a trend) | **change / trend / development** | *The evolution of sales* reads oddly |
| *important* (of a quantity) | **large / substantial / considerable** | French *important* = big. *An important increase* is wrong; *a substantial increase* is right |
| *actually* | **currently / at present** | *actuellement* |
| *eventually* | **possibly / if necessary** | *éventuellement* |
| *sensibly* (meaning noticeably) | **noticeably / appreciably** | *sensiblement* |
| *permit to increase* | **allow/enable X to increase** | *permit/allow* need an object |
| *assist* (meaning attend) | **attend** | *assister à* |
| *control* (meaning check) | **check / verify** | *contrôler* |
| *to pass* (an exam) | **to take / to sit** | *passer un examen* |
| *stage* | **internship** | if it ever arises |

### 5. Verb agreement traps that Task 1 manufactures

Long noun phrases separate subject from verb constantly in this task, which is
exactly when agreement fails.

- *The number of cars* **is/was** singular. *A number of cars* **are/were** plural.
- *The proportion of women* **was**, not *were* — the head noun is *proportion*.
- *The majority of respondents* **were** — here the head takes plural agreement.
- *People* is already plural: ✗ *peoples are*, ✗ *people is*.
- ✗ *was increase*, ✗ *has increase* — never stack a bare auxiliary against a bare
  verb. ✓ *increased* / *was increasing* / *has increased*.
- Third-person *-s*: *the figure **rises***, *the chart **shows***.

### 6. Run-on sentences — French/Arabic chaining

The most expensive GRA habit in this task, because a description of four series
invites one enormous comma-and-*and* chain, and band 7 GRA is measured by the
**frequency of error-free sentences** — one 60-word run-on destroys what four clean
short sentences would have earned.

> ✗ *In 1990 the walking was the most used mode with 13 million and after that it
> decreased a lot until 6 million in 2010, and in the other hand the car increased
> from 4 to 11 million and became the first mode, and this is the biggest change of
> the chart.*

> ✓ *In 1990, walking was the most common mode, at 13 million trips, but it had
> fallen to 6 million by 2010. Car travel moved in the opposite direction, climbing
> from 4 to 11 million to become the leading mode. This exchange of positions was
> the chart's most striking feature.*

Same content, three sentences instead of one, all clean. **Rule of thumb: if a
sentence contains more than one figure-pair and more than one *and*, split it.**

### 7. Register borrowed from French academic writing

*In the other hand* (→ **on the other hand**) · *In a first time* (→ **initially /
in the first stage**) · *We can see that / We remark that* (→ drop it; state the
fact, or **the chart shows that**) · *It is to be noted that* (→ **notably**) ·
*In conclusion* on a Task 1 (→ don't). First person (*I, we*) is out of register for
an Academic Task 1 report.

### 8. Arabic-specific

- **Definite-article over-supply.** Arabic marks definiteness heavily; combined with
  French this doubles the pressure toward *the*. When in doubt on a general noun in
  Task 1, **leave it out**.
- **Coordination preference.** Arabic rhetoric favours chained coordinated clauses;
  see §6. English Task 1 wants subordination (*while*, *whereas*, *although*,
  relative clauses).
- **No *is* in equational sentences.** Arabic drops the copula: watch for ✗ *The
  highest figure the car use.* → ✓ *The highest figure **was** car use.*
- **Number–noun agreement.** Arabic number agreement rules do not map: ✓ *five
  million*, ✗ *five millions*; ✓ *20 per cent*, ✗ *20 per cents*; ✓ *a
  ten-year period*, ✗ *a ten-years period*.
