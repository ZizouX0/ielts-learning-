# Appendix E — Sources

Every factual claim in this book traces to a source. This appendix is the map of
those sources, arranged so you can *use* them: grouped by what you would go to
them for, with a note on what each one settles.

It is not the full research ledger. The full ledger — 216 sources with the exact
sentence each one verified — lives in `research/sources-ledger.md`. What follows
is the working set: the pages worth your time while you prepare, plus the two
files you must never open, and the method for checking any source yourself.

---

## The hierarchy, in plain words

There are only four organisations that can tell you what IELTS *is*: **ielts.org**
(the partnership's own site), the **British Council**, **IDP Education**, and
**Cambridge University Press & Assessment**. Between them they write the test,
train the examiners, publish the band descriptors and decide the rules. When one
of them says something about format, timing, scoring or policy, that is not an
opinion — it is the rule. Everything on those four domains is what this book calls
**Tier 1**.

Below that sit the **strategy sources** — IELTS Liz, IELTS Simon, E2, IELTS
Advantage, Magoosh, and the academic literature on language testing. Several are
written by former examiners and they are genuinely good. But they are **Tier 2**,
and the distinction is not snobbery, it is about what a source can be wrong about.
A prep blog can teach you a superb method for attacking Matching Headings; it
cannot tell you what the band-7 descriptor says, because it does not write the
band-7 descriptor. Use Tier 2 for **technique**. Never for **rules**.

The practical version of that rule: **if a Tier 2 source contradicts a Tier 1
source, the Tier 2 source is wrong.** This is not hypothetical. Building this book
turned up a widely repeated blog claim that a Task 1 answer with no overview
"scores below a 5" — the official 2023 descriptors explicitly describe a band-5
script that has no overview. It turned up a "73% of test-takers" collocation
statistic that traces to nothing at all. It turned up two different superseded
band-descriptor PDFs outranking the current ones in search results. Every one of
those would have cost you real marks if you had trusted it.

Anything that is neither official nor an established strategy expert is **Tier 3**:
useful for finding out what questions people ask, worthless as evidence. Nothing
in this book rests on a Tier 3 source.

## Band descriptors and assessment criteria

**These are the four files that decide your score.** Everything else in this book
is an argument about how to satisfy them. Download all four; check the edition
date on each before you read a word of it (see *Do not cite*, below).

| File | What it is |
|---|---|
| `ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-band-descriptors.pdf` | **The single most important document you will read.** 9 pages. Task 1 grid pp.3–5, Task 2 grid pp.7–9. Current edition: created **3 May 2023** |
| `ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors.pdf` | Speaking grid, four criteria. Current edition: created **16 September 2025** — a newer revision than the Writing file, which catches people out |
| `ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-writing-key-assessment-criteria.pdf` | The companion prose. Defines each criterion — including the fact that **spelling and word formation are scored under Lexical Resource, not Grammar** |
| `ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-key-assessment-criteria.pdf` | The same for Speaking, including the appropriacy dimension of Lexical Resource |

Two things in the descriptor header do most of the work, and most candidates never
read them: *a script must fully fit the positive features of a band to be rated
there*, and *bolded text marks negative features that will limit a rating*. If
you only half-fit band 7, you are a 6.

Supporting:

- `ielts.org/organisations/ielts-for-organisations/understanding-ielts-scoring` —
  where the current PDFs actually live, plus confirmation that the four criteria
  are equally weighted and Task 2 carries more weight.
- `ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-guide-for-teachers.pdf` — written for teachers,
  which is why it explains the criteria more plainly than anything aimed at candidates.
- `ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-understanding-the-ielts-speaking-band-descriptors` —
  IDP's plain-English walk-through of the Speaking grid.

## Scoring and band conversion

How raw marks become bands — and the one detail almost every prep site gets wrong.

| Source | Use it for |
|---|---|
| `ielts.org/for-test-takers/how-ielts-is-scored` | The 9-band scale, the overall-band rounding rule (.25 rounds up, .75 rounds up), and the **four official anchor points**: Listening 16/23/30/35, Academic Reading 15/23/30/35 |
| `ielts.org/take-a-test/your-results/ielts-scoring-in-detail` | The same anchors with three worked rounding examples, and the caveat that thresholds vary slightly by test version |
| `ielts.idp.com/turkey/results/scores/reading/en-gb` | **The full Academic Reading table, half bands included** — 40–39=9, 38–37=8.5, 36–35=8, 34–33=7.5, 32–30=7, 29–27=6.5, 26–23=6, 22–19=5.5, 18–15=5 |
| `ielts.idp.com/thailand/results/scores/listening` | **The full Listening table, half bands included** — 39–40=9, 37–38=8.5, 35–36=8, 32–34=7.5, 30–31=7, 26–29=6.5, 23–25=6, 18–22=5.5, 16–17=5 |

> **A trap worth knowing about.** IDP publishes different content on different
> country sites. The Listening table above is on the **Thailand** page. The
> canonical `ielts.idp.com/results/scores/listening` loads perfectly and gives
> only one worked example — no table. The Turkey page, which *does* carry the
> Reading table, does *not* carry the Listening one. If you go looking for these
> figures and land on the wrong market's page, you will conclude no official
> half-band table exists. It does. Both tables carry the same official caveat:
> these are average thresholds, and "actual marks may vary slightly between tests".

For Writing, IELTS publishes the 1:2 weighting of Task 1 to Task 2 but **not** the
arithmetic. `(Task 1 + 2 × Task 2) ÷ 3` is a well-grounded estimate, and this book
labels it as one. Do not present the result as the number IELTS would compute.

## Research and evidence

Where this book's claims about *your specific error patterns* come from.

- **`ielts.org/cdn/Research/investigating-development-of-grammatical-range-and-accuracy-at-different-proficiency-levels-roothooft-et-al-2019.pdf`**
  — Roothooft, Breeze et al., *IELTS Research Reports* 2019/1. **Bookmark.** The
  most useful single research artefact for a band-6-to-7 candidate: it measures
  which grammatical errors actually discriminate between bands. Articles fall from
  an 18.4% error rate at band 6 to 7.4% at band 7 — the largest discontinuity in
  the study. Third-person *-s* omission collapses from 70.6% to 7.6%. And overuse
  errors against the plural (*peoples*, *informations*) are the one category that
  does **not** resolve as proficiency rises.
- `ielts.org/researchers/our-research/test-statistics` and the accompanying
  test-taker performance spreadsheet — mean bands by first language, so you can
  see where Arabic and French L1 candidates actually lose marks.
- `files.eric.ed.gov` (TESL-EJ and ELT journal papers), `onlinelibrary.wiley.com`
  (*TESOL Quarterly*, *Language Learning*) — the second-language acquisition
  literature behind the L1-interference chapter.
- `owl.purdue.edu/…/runonsentences.html`, `learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/…/uncount-nouns`,
  `eapfoundation.com/vocab/academic/awllists/` — grammar and academic-vocabulary
  reference. The last one is the Academic Word List, sublist by sublist.

## Do not cite

> ### ⚠️ Two superseded PDFs outrank the current ones in search results
>
> Both of these load, both look official, both are **years out of date**, and both
> are what you will find if you simply search for "IELTS band descriptors". Two
> different researchers on this project walked into them independently.
>
> | File | Edition | How to recognise it |
> |---|---|---|
> | `assets.ctfassets.net/…/writingbanddescriptorstask1and2.pdf` | **Writing, last modified 13 June 2013** | 2 pages, no Title, no Author, Creator `Nitro Pro 8`. Its band-7 Academic Task Achievement line reads *"presents a clear overview of main trends, differences or stages"* — wording the 2023 edition does not use. The current file is **9 pages** |
> | `assets.cambridgeenglish.org/webinars/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors.pdf` | **Speaking, created 29 September 2008, modified 21 May 2013** | 1 page, Title blank, Author `borour`, Creator `Acrobat PDFMaker 7.0.7 for Word`. Its band-6 Pronunciation cell omits chunking, stress-timing and speech rate entirely; its band-7 Grammar cell omits the simple-and-complex-sentences clause. The current file is **4 pages** |
>
> The cost of using one is not academic. The 2008 Speaking file's band-7 Fluency
> wording is materially different from the 2025 edition, and studying the wrong
> one means optimising for a target that no longer exists.
>
> **Always check the edition date before you trust a descriptor PDF.** Three ways,
> cheapest first:
>
> 1. **Count the pages.** Current Writing = 9. Current Speaking = 4. A 1- or
>    2-page "band descriptors" file is old.
> 2. **Open Document Properties** in any PDF reader and read Title, Author and
>    Created. The current files say Author `IELTS` and carry a real title.
> 3. **Read the metadata directly**, which is what this project did:
>
> ```python
> import pdfplumber
> with pdfplumber.open("descriptors.pdf") as pdf:
>     print(pdf.metadata)          # Title, Author, Creator, CreationDate, ModDate
>     print(len(pdf.pages))
> ```
>
> Real output from the current Writing file: `Title: IELTS Writing Band
> Descriptors`, `Author: IELTS`, `CreationDate: D:20230503…`, 9 pages. Real output
> from the 2013 file: no Title, no Author, `Creator: Nitro Pro 8`,
> `ModDate: D:20130613…`, 2 pages. The difference takes ten seconds to see.
>
> **Download the current descriptors only from `ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/`.**
> The British Council mirror at
> `takeielts.britishcouncil.org/sites/default/files/ielts_writing_band_descriptors.pdf`
> is the same May 2023 edition and is safe; the two files above are not.

## How to check a source yourself

Four habits. They are the whole method, and they generalise well beyond IELTS.

**1. Check the PDF's edition date before you read it.** Page count, then Document
Properties, then metadata. A PDF has no expiry notice and no banner telling you
it has been replaced — the date is the only signal, and search engines rank by
popularity, not currency.

**2. Prefer the official domain, and check you are on it.** `ielts.org`,
`takeielts.britishcouncil.org`, `ielts.idp.com`, `cambridgeenglish.org`. Note the
near-misses: `idp.com` is the parent company's corporate blog, not the test site;
`cambridge.org/elt/blog` is a publisher's marketing blog, not the exam board. Both
are fine reading and neither is authoritative.

**3. A 403 is not proof a page is gone.** Several official sites — the British
Council estate especially, and `britishcouncil.tn` — sit behind bot protection
that rejects automated fetchers with a 403 or a broken connection while serving
browsers normally. During this project, the British Council's own copy of the
Writing band descriptors was recorded as "could not be retrieved" for exactly this
reason; re-tested with a different tool, it loaded on the first attempt and turned
out to be the correct current edition. **Open the URL in a browser before you
conclude anything is dead.** A genuine 404 says so plainly; bot protection does not.

**4. Check whether you are on a market variant.** IDP publishes country-specific
sites — `/thailand/`, `/turkey/`, `/canada/`, `/philippines/` — and their content
is *not* identical. The Listening band-conversion table exists on the Thailand
page and not on the canonical one. The British Council does the same across
national sites, and `britishcouncil.tn` currently contradicts the global pages on
whether paper tests are still bookable. When a figure matters, check a second
market's page before deciding the figure does not exist — and when two official
pages disagree, the more specific and more recently updated one usually wins,
with policy pages outranking blog posts.

## Verification statement

**All sources in this appendix were fetched and verified on 31 July 2026.** Every
quotation attributed to an official source was read on the live page or extracted
from the live PDF on that date, not carried over from memory or from a secondary
site. Where a page could not be retrieved, or where the content is delivered as
video with no published transcript, the ledger says so rather than implying
verification.

**Re-check format, fees and policy before your test date.** IELTS changes: the
paper test is being withdrawn from mid-2026 on a timetable that varies by market,
the Speaking descriptors were revised in 2025 while the Writing descriptors date
from 2023, and Tunisian fees and centre locations are set locally. Nothing in this
book is a substitute for the official page on the morning you book. Re-read three
things before you sit the test: the format pages for your delivery mode, the
current band descriptors, and your test centre's own page.
