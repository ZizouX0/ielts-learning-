# WRITING TASK 1 — the moves

**The clock:** 0–5 plan · 5–17 write · stop at 17 · check to 20.
**The shape:** intro (1 sentence) → overview (2) → body 1 → body 2. No conclusion.

**1. Write the overview before you write any detail.**
Two sentences, no figures, naming the biggest pattern. Start it "Overall,".
→ *Without it, Task Achievement is capped. Cheapest mark in Task 1.*

**2. Group the data. Never list it.**
Decide two groups before typing: what rose / what fell, or top / middle / negligible.
→ *Grouping is the 6-to-7 gate. A perfect list is a 6.*

**3. Ask first: is there a time axis?**
No time axis means no *rose*, *fell*, *increased* — comparison language only.
→ *A trend verb on a static chart is a factual error.*

**4. Decide the tense in five seconds.**
Dated past → past simple. "Today" → present perfect. Undated → present simple. Man-made process → present passive.
→ *Stops one wrong decision repeating across 180 words.*

**5. Never copy the prompt or the chart title.**
Rewrite it in one sentence: change the nouns and the verb, keep the meaning.
→ *Copied words are subtracted before counting, and prove you cannot paraphrase.*

**6. Strip "the" off bare general nouns.**
*car use*, *tourism*, *cycling*, *electricity consumption*. Keep it in *the number/proportion/majority of*.
→ *Your highest-frequency error, and it is marked as grammar.*

**7. Drill the figure prepositions.**
rose **from** 4 **to** 11 · rose **by** 7 · peaked **at** 11 · a peak **of** 11 · stable **at** 5.
→ *"Rose to" and "rose by" are different facts. French leaks here.*

**8. Match the word to the size of the change.**
*Plummeted* for a 3% fall is wrong. Rotate *sharply · steadily · gradually · marginally*.
→ *Wrong intensity costs twice — accuracy and vocabulary.*

**9. Report only what you can see.**
No causes, no opinions, no predictions, no conclusion paragraph.
→ *Every line of commentary is irrelevant content and spent words.*

**10. Replace repeated nouns with reference words.**
*this shift · the former · the latter · both · doing so · the remaining two categories.*
→ *Fastest cohesion upgrade available. Almost nobody drills it.*

**11. Split any sentence with two figure-pairs and two "and"s.**
Full stop, or subordinate it with *while*, *whereas*, *although*.
→ *One 60-word run-on wipes out four clean sentences.*

**12. Say what did not change.**
Name the flat line, the stable share, the untouched building.
→ *A constant is a main feature. Most candidates skip it.*

**13. Approximate when the axis is unlabelled.**
*just over 40,000 · in the region of 200 · some 15 per cent · upwards of half.*
→ *"Nearly" means below, "well over" means above. Wrong side = error.*

**14. Write 170–190 words and stop at seventeen minutes.**
Check the counter when the overview ends: you should be at 55–70.
→ *Overrunning here costs a full band on the task worth double.*

## Sentence starters that always work

**Intro (any visual)**
> *The chart shows how [X] changed in [three countries] between [1990] and [2010].*

**Overview — trend**
> *Overall, [group A] rose steadily across the period, whereas [group B] moved in the opposite direction.*

**Overview — comparison, no time axis**
> *Overall, the figures fall into two clear groups: [A] and [B] are far higher than the remaining three.*

**Overview — process**
> *Overall, the process falls into three broad phases: [collection], [conversion] and finally [packing].*

**Overview — map**
> *Overall, [Ashcombe] has been transformed from a [farming] settlement into a [residential] one, and only [the church] remains unchanged.*

**Detail — trend, shape 1**
> *[Car use] rose steadily from [4 million] in [1990] to [11 million] in [2010], peaking at [11.4 million] before levelling off.*

**Detail — trend, shape 2 (alternate with shape 1)**
> *There was a sharp rise in [car use] over the same period.*

**Comparison**
> *[Country A] used more than twice as much water per head as [Country C], while [Country E] lagged well behind both.*

**Proportion**
> *[Packaging] accounted for [34] per cent of the total, making it the largest single category.*

**Process step**
> *Once the [glass] has been [sorted], it is [washed] before being [crushed into cullet].*

# WRITING TASK 2 — the moves

**The clock:** 0–5 plan · 5–37 write · stop at 37 · check to 40.
**The shape:** intro (position stated) → body 1 → body 2 → conclusion.

**1. Count the demands in the last line. Answer every one.**
*Discuss both views and give your own opinion* = three demands = three things visibly on the page.
→ *Answering one part of a two-part prompt limits you to band 5.*

**2. Build every body paragraph: claim → because → so what → example.**
Four moves, roughly 100 words. Never leave a claim standing alone.
→ *This is the whole 6-to-7 difference. Nothing else buys more.*

**3. State your position in the last sentence of the introduction.**
One unambiguous sentence. Not "there are arguments on both sides".
→ *A position stated late reads as a position tacked on.*

**4. Two developed ideas, not four asserted ones.**
At 280 words, four ideas get 70 each — that is assertion length.
→ *Underdeveloped main ideas are the named band-6 fault.*

**5. Write four or five paragraphs with a blank line between each.**
Decide the paragraph count before you type the first word.
→ *Weak paragraphing drags you to band 5 on its own.*

**6. Paraphrase the prompt. Never lift a clause.**
Change the nouns and the verb; keep the proposition exactly.
→ *Copied words earn nothing, are not counted, and expose weak vocabulary.*

**7. Put your stance in every topic sentence.**
*"The stronger reason for rejecting this is…"* — not *"Another factor is…"*.
→ *Neutral bodies with an opinion at each end is the classic 6.*

**8. Cap discourse markers at two per paragraph. Never zero.**
Delete *Firstly · Moreover · Furthermore · In addition* stacked at sentence heads.
→ *Linker overuse actively lowers your cohesion band.*

**9. Use reference and substitution instead of connectors.**
*that mismatch · these pressures · this shift · the former · one · so.*
→ *Highest-value cohesion move available to a band-6 writer.*

**10. Hedge every generalisation.**
*tends to · appears to · is likely to · in most cases · broadly · often.*
→ *Raises vocabulary and kills the band-7 over-generalising weakness at once.*

**11. Concede once, then counter.**
*"While X is undeniably true, it is an argument about timing, not about cars."*
→ *Proves a developed position and hands you a free complex structure.*

**12. Name the actor, the instrument and the mechanism.**
Not *"governments should raise awareness"* — say who does what, and why it works.
→ *Vague remedies lose on relevance and on word choice together.*

**13. Make the conclusion give a reason, not a summary.**
Name the criterion the verdict rests on: permanent vs temporary, shared vs narrow.
→ *A restated introduction is repetition, which is a named band-6 fault.*

**14. Write 270–290 words. Floor 260, no ceiling that matters.**
Do not cut a well-developed 320 to hit a number. Do not pad.
→ *Two properly developed ideas will not fit inside 250.*

**15. Never open with a memorised line.**
Kill *"In this contemporary era of rapid globalisation…"* and every relative.
→ *Formulaic openers are listed as evidence of a low band.*

**16. Learn ten topic nouns, not twenty synonyms.**
*curriculum · attainment gap · drop-out rate · vocational training · contact hours.*
→ *Reaching for rare words without collocation control is band-6 behaviour.*

**17. Spend the last three minutes on four separate sweeps.**
Articles → plural uncountables → comma splices → third-person *-s*. One pass each.
→ *These four are systematic errors, and systematic is what caps you.*

**18. In those three minutes, delete and repair. Never add.**
A sentence written at minute 39 is unproofread by definition.
→ *That sentence is the one that carries an error into a clean script.*

## The develop-don't-assert pattern

Every body paragraph, four moves:

| Move | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Claim** | The idea, in one sentence. |
| **Because** | The mechanism — *why* it is true. |
| **So what** | The consequence — who gains, who loses, what follows. |
| **Example** | One concrete instance from your own knowledge. No invented statistics. |

**Before — asserted (band 6):**
> *Remote work is convenient for employees. It saves time and money. Therefore many workers prefer it.*

**After — developed (band 7):**
> *Remote work removes the daily commute* **(claim)**, *which for someone travelling into a capital from a satellite town can absorb two hours a day* **(because)**. *Those hours are not merely saved; they are redirected into rest or into domestic work that would otherwise be crowded into the evening* **(so what)**. *A parent commuting from [a satellite town] therefore recovers close to a working day every week* **(example)**.

Same idea. Three times the marks.

## Sentence starters that always work

**Stating a position**
> *In my view, [restrictions] are justified, but only where [the public alternative already exists].*

**Stating a partial position (for "to what extent")**
> *This holds for [vocational subjects], but not for [the humanities].*

**Answering two questions in one line**
> *The shift is driven mainly by [what has become easy], and on balance it is a [negative] development.*

**Topic sentence that carries the stance**
> *The stronger reason for rejecting this claim is that [X].*

**Giving a reason**
> *This matters because [mechanism], which means that [consequence].*

**Conceding**
> *While [X] is undeniably true, it is an argument about [timing] rather than about [Y].*

**Conceding, short**
> *There is real force in this objection, yet [counter].*

**Introducing an example**
> *A [student] who [situation], for example, will [outcome].*

**Hedging**
> *[Graduates] tend to [X], and in most cases [Y] is likely to follow.*

**Weighing one side against the other**
> *The first cost is temporary and local; the second is permanent and shared.*

**Back-referencing a cause when you propose a remedy**
> *The first of these causes is the easier to attack, because [reason].*

**Concluding with a reason**
> *In conclusion, [verdict], because [the criterion the verdict rests on].*
