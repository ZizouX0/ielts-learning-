# GRAMMAR ON DEMAND

## How to use this

You do not read this. You look things up in it: find the structure you need, copy
the pattern, swap in your own content. Before you write it, read the ✗ → ✓ lines
underneath — those are the errors *you* make in that structure, not generic ones.
One rule governs the whole file: a complex sentence you cannot check is worth less
than a simple sentence you can. Accuracy parity is the target, not more complexity.

## The order to learn them

Articles, agreement and punctuation (§9, §10, §11) are not on this list. They are
not structures you choose — they run underneath every sentence you write, and they
are always on. Everything below is optional, and ranked by what it pays after the
risk is subtracted.

| Structure | What it costs you to get wrong | Learn it when |
|---|---|---|
| **§2 Relative clauses** | Almost nothing — this is the one complex structure you get right at the rate band 7 needs | **Now.** Before anything else here. |
| **§1 Comparatives** | Visible in one glance, and Task 1 is built out of them | **Now.** Every Task 1 needs them. |
| **§5 Concession clauses** | *Although…but* and *despite* + clause are your highest-frequency complex-sentence errors | **Now.** You already use these — you just use them wrong. |
| **§4 The passive** | Required in process tasks; the intransitive passive (*was happened*) is a real error, not a slip | Week 1, or the first time a process diagram appears. |
| **§6 Participle clauses** | A dangling participle makes the sentence say something absurd | After relative clauses are automatic — it is the same move, shortened. |
| **§3 Second conditional** | One pattern, and the *would*-in-the-*if*-clause error is the only way to fail it | After articles are under control. One pattern, drilled cold. |
| **§7 Nominalisation** | Buys register, but every single one creates a new article decision | Only once your article error rate is under 10%. |
| **§8 Cleft sentences** | Used twice, it reads as a memorised template | Last. One per essay, no more. |
| **§3 Third conditional** | Nothing to gain. Candidates at every band, up to the top, essentially never write one | **Never.** Rewrite it in the past simple instead. |

## 1. COMPARATIVES AND SUPERLATIVES

### The patterns

| Adjective | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|
| **Short** (1 syllable, or 2 ending in *-y*) | high**er**, larg**er**, easi**er**, cheap**er** | **the** highest, **the** largest, **the** easiest |
| **Long** (2+ syllables) | **more** expensive, **more** significant, **more** reliable | **the most** expensive, **the most** significant |
| **Irregular** | better · worse · further · less · more | the best · the worst · the furthest · the least · the most |

- **than**, never *that*: *higher **than** in 2010*.
- **the** + superlative, always — unless a possessive is already there:
  *the highest figure*, but *its highest level*.
- Equality: **as** … **as**. *as high as* · *not as high as* · *the same **as***.

> *Rates of car ownership were **higher** in urban areas **than** in rural ones
> throughout the period.*
> *Renewable sources accounted for **the largest** share of generation by 2020.*
> *Distance learning is **more accessible** but **less** effective for practical
> subjects.*

### Intensifying a comparison

Put the intensifier **in front of** the comparative:

**far · significantly · considerably · substantially · markedly · slightly ·
marginally** + comparative

> *Public transport use was **considerably higher** in the capital than in any
> other region.*
> *Female enrolment rose **only marginally faster** than male enrolment.*

And for superlatives: **by far the** largest / the smallest / the most common.

> *Road transport remained **by far the largest** source of urban emissions.*

⚠ *very* cannot intensify a comparative. *Very high* ✓, *very higher* ✗ — use *far*.

### Multiples

- **twice as many** commuters **as** in 2005
- **three times higher than** the national average
- **half as much** electricity **as** the previous decade
- **a tenfold rise in** applications · **a fivefold increase in** demand
- **double the** number of · **ten per cent more than**

> *By 2018 the city was processing **three times as much** waste **as** it had a
> decade earlier, a **twofold increase** on the regional average.*

### Your errors

- ✗ more higher → ✓ higher
- ✗ more bigger → ✓ bigger
- ✗ more cheaper → ✓ cheaper
- ✗ the most highest → ✓ the highest
- ✗ the most largest → ✓ the largest
- ✗ bigger that the others → ✓ bigger **than** the others
- ✗ more good → ✓ better
- ✗ more bad → ✓ worse
- ✗ as high than → ✓ as high **as**
- ✗ the same than in 2010 → ✓ the same **as** in 2010
- ✗ very higher → ✓ far higher
- ✗ twice more than → ✓ twice as many **as**
- ✗ It is the more important factor → ✓ It is **the most** important factor
- ✗ highest figure of all → ✓ **the** highest figure of all

## 2. RELATIVE CLAUSES

**Your best structure.** You get relative clauses right at a rate no other complex
structure in your profile comes close to, and band-7 writers use roughly twice as
many of them as band-6 writers. When you need a complex sentence, reach here first.

### Defining vs non-defining — the comma rule in one line

**Say the sentence without the clause. If the reader would no longer know which
thing you mean, use no commas. If they would still know, use commas both sides.**

| | Defining (no commas) | Non-defining (commas both sides) |
|---|---|---|
| Job | Tells the reader **which one** | Adds extra information about something already identified |
| *that* allowed? | **Yes** | **No** — use *which* or *who* |
| Pronoun droppable? | Yes, when it is the **object** | No |

> *Students **who live** far from campus miss more classes.* — only those students
> *Students, **who live** far from campus, miss more classes.* — **all** students
> live far from campus, which is almost never what you meant

That comma is not decoration. It changes the fact you are asserting.

### The relativisers

| Word | Refers to | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **who** | people | *workers **who** commute more than an hour* |
| **which** | things | *policies **which** target congestion* |
| **that** | people or things — **defining only** | *countries **that** invest early* |
| **whose** | possession (people *and* things) | *families **whose** income falls below the line* |
| **where** | places | *regions **where** rainfall is unreliable* |

### Dropping the pronoun

When the relativiser is the **object** of the clause, you can delete it. Free
concision, no risk.

> *The measures **(that)** the council introduced in 2019 have barely affected
> congestion.*

### "which" referring to a whole clause

Use *which* — with a comma, always — to comment on everything you just said.

> *Tuition fees have risen faster than average incomes, **which** has deterred
> applicants from low-income families.*

⚠ Comma compulsory, and never *that* for this job. ✗ *…incomes that has deterred…*

### Reduced relatives — the repair that is also an upgrade

Delete the relativiser and the *be*-verb. Shorter, and it still counts as range.

| Full | Reduced |
|---|---|
| *students **who live** in rural areas* | *students **living** in rural areas* |
| *the measures **which were introduced** in 2020* | *the measures **introduced** in 2020* |
| *households **that rely** on a single income* | *households **relying** on a single income* |

**-ing** for active, **past participle** for passive. This is the single highest-value
move in this file for you, because it kills your Arabic missing-relativiser error and
produces a band-7 structure in the same stroke.

### 5 model sentences

1. *Countries **that** invest early in vocational training report lower youth
   unemployment.*
2. *Vocational training, **which** costs a fraction of a degree to deliver, reaches
   students **whom** academic routes never see.*
3. *Cities **where** cycling infrastructure is separated from traffic show the
   sharpest rise in bicycle use.*
4. *Families **whose** income falls below the poverty line spend a larger share of it
   on food.*
5. *Employees **working** from home two days a week report the highest job
   satisfaction.*

### Your errors

- ✗ people which live abroad → ✓ people **who** live abroad
- ✗ the students which their parents work → ✓ the students **whose** parents work
- ✗ the reason why that people migrate → ✓ the reason **why** people migrate (never *why* + *that*)
- ✗ The reform which the ministry announced **it** in 2019 → ✓ …the ministry announced in 2019
- ✗ The residents who the survey contacted **them** → ✓ The residents **whom** the survey contacted
- ✗ the district where the tramline was built **in it** → ✓ the district **where** the tramline was built
- ✗ There are many households live below the line → ✓ …households **that live** / **living** below the line
- ✗ Vocational training which costs far less reaches more students → ✓ Vocational training**,** which costs far less**,** reaches more students
- ✗ The internet, **that** has changed research, … → ✓ The internet, **which** has changed research, …
- ✗ A child who spend three hours online → ✓ A child who **spends** three hours online
- ✗ policies who target congestion → ✓ policies **which/that** target congestion
- ⚠ Two relative clauses in one sentence is one more than you can check under the
  clock. One per sentence.

## 3. CONDITIONALS

### FIRST — a real future condition

**If + present simple, … will + base verb.**

> *If governments **invest** in rail, congestion **will fall** within a decade.*
> *Unless fares **are** subsidised, ridership **will continue** to decline.*

Use it freely. It is low-risk and it does real work in Task 2 predictions.

### SECOND — hypothetical, not real

**If + past simple, … would + base verb.**

> *If governments **invested** in rail, congestion **would fall** within a decade.*
> *If public transport **were** free, more commuters **would abandon** their cars.*

*Were* is the better academic choice; *was* is standard modern English and nobody
can defensibly mark it wrong. Prefer *were*, do not fear *was*.

### THIRD — skip it

Do not write one. Candidates at every band, including the top, essentially never
produce a third conditional, so it buys you nothing and it is a three-part structure
to get wrong under the clock. When you find yourself starting *If governments had…,
they would have…*, rewrite it in the past simple with a consequence:

> *Governments failed to act in the 1990s, and emissions rose accordingly.*

**Master the second. Use the first freely. Skip the third and mixed entirely.**

### Your errors

- ✗ If governments **would invest**, congestion would fall → ✓ If governments **invested**, congestion would fall
- ✗ If the subsidy **will end** next year, prices will rise → ✓ If the subsidy **ends** next year, prices will rise
- ✗ If governments invested, congestion **will** fall → ✓ …congestion **would** fall
- ✗ If cities banned cars, air quality **would improved** → ✓ …**would improve**
- ✗ **Unless if** fares are cut → ✓ **Unless** fares are cut (*unless* already means *if not*)
- ✗ If it **would be** cheaper, more people would use it → ✓ If it **were** cheaper, …

⚠ The rule behind all of these: **no modal inside the *if*-clause.** Ever.

## 4. THE PASSIVE

### When it is required

- **Process diagrams** — the doer is a machine or nobody, and the stages are the point.
- **When the doer is unknown, obvious, or irrelevant.**
- **When you want the affected thing to be the topic** of the sentence.

Not everywhere. A passive in every sentence hides who is responsible, and Task
Response wants a clear position.

### The forms

| Form | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Present | **is / are** + past participle | *The harvested cane **is washed** and then crushed.* |
| Past | **was / were** + pp | *Tuition fees **were frozen** for three consecutive years.* |
| Present perfect | **has / have been** + pp | *The scheme **has been extended** twice since 2015.* |
| Modal | **can / should / must be** + pp | *Emissions **can be reduced** without new technology.* |
| Modal perfect | **should have been** + pp | *The policy **should have been evaluated** before it was extended.* |
| Continuous | **is / are being** + pp | *Three new lines **are being built** simultaneously.* |

Add *by* only when the agent genuinely matters.

### Your errors

- ✗ The scheme **is consist of** three stages → ✓ The scheme **consists of** three stages
- ✗ An accident **was happened** at the junction → ✓ An accident **happened** at the junction
- ✗ The number of visitors **was risen** sharply → ✓ The number of visitors **rose** sharply
- ✗ This **is occurred** mainly in coastal regions → ✓ This **occurs** mainly in coastal regions
- ✗ A new problem **was appeared** after 2016 → ✓ A new problem **appeared** after 2016
- ✗ The figure **was remained** stable → ✓ The figure **remained** stable
- ✗ It **is depend on** the region → ✓ It **depends on** the region

⚠ **Intransitive verbs have no passive at all.** *happen · occur · arise · appear ·
exist · rise · fall · emerge · remain · consist* take no object, so there is nothing
to promote to subject. No object, no passive, ever.

## 5. CONCESSION CLAUSES

Three groups. They all mean "contrast" and they take three different things after them.

| Word | Takes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **although · even though · though** | a **clause** (subject + verb) | *Although fees have risen, applications continue to grow.* |
| **while · whereas** | a **clause** | *Whereas rural clinics are understaffed, urban hospitals turn patients away.* |
| **despite · in spite of** | a **noun** or an **-ing** form | *Despite the rise in fees, applications continue to grow.* |
| **however · nevertheless · nonetheless** | **nothing** — they are adverbs, not joiners | *Fees have risen. However, applications continue to grow.* |

Three ways to fix a *despite* + clause, all correct:

> *Despite **its unpopularity**, the tax has cut consumption.* (noun)
> *Despite **being unpopular**, the tax has cut consumption.* (-ing)
> *Despite **the fact that** the tax is unpopular, it has cut consumption.* (clumsy but correct — and correct beats elegant)

### Your errors

- ✗ despite of the cost → ✓ **despite** the cost
- ✗ in spite the cost → ✓ **in spite of** the cost
- ✗ Although the tax is unpopular, **but** it works → ✓ Although the tax is unpopular, it works
- ✗ Even though fees rose, **however** applications grew → ✓ Even though fees rose, applications grew
- ✗ Despite the tax **is** unpopular → ✓ Despite **its unpopularity** / Despite **the fact that** the tax is unpopular
- ✗ Despite **of the fact that** → ✓ Despite the fact that
- ✗ Fees have doubled, **however** applications rise → ✓ Fees have doubled**.** However, applications rise
- ✗ Although of the cost → ✓ **Although** the cost **is high**

⚠ English allows exactly **one** joining word between two clauses. Both Arabic and
French let you echo the contrast in the second clause. English does not.

## 6. PARTICIPLE CLAUSES

### The patterns

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| **-ing**, active | ***Lacking** reliable transport, rural students miss classes.* |
| **Having** + past participle | ***Having introduced** a congestion charge, the city saw traffic fall by a fifth.* |
| **Faced with** X | ***Faced with** rising rents, young workers are delaying home ownership.* |
| **Being unable to** X | ***Being unable to** afford childcare, many mothers leave the workforce.* |
| **Trailing -ing** (comments on the main clause) | *Enrolment doubled between 2010 and 2020, **rising** fastest in the final three years.* |

The trailing *-ing* clause is worth more to you than the other four combined,
because it is tailor-made for Task 1:

> *Solar generation reached 40 terawatt-hours, **accounting** for roughly a quarter
> of supply.*

### ⚠ Dangerous — one failure mode, and it is severe

**The subject immediately after the comma must be the thing doing the participle.**
Mismatch it and the sentence says something absurd.

- ✗ *Having banned diesel cars from the centre, **air quality** improved within a
  year.* — the air quality banned the cars
- ✓ *Having banned diesel cars from the centre, **the city** saw air quality improve
  within a year.*
- ✓ *After **the city** banned diesel cars from the centre, air quality improved
  within a year.*

**One-second check:** put your finger on the word right after the comma. Is that the
thing doing the participle? If not, rewrite with *Because / After / Since*.

### Your errors

- ✗ Lacking formal qualifications, **employers** rarely shortlist these candidates → ✓ Lacking formal qualifications, **these candidates** are rarely shortlisted
- ✗ Being unable to afford housing, **the government** must intervene → ✓ Being unable to afford housing, **young workers** need government help
- ✗ Having completed the training, **a certificate** is issued → ✓ Having completed the training, **participants** receive a certificate

## 7. NOMINALISATION

Take the action out of the verb, pack it into a noun, rebuild the sentence around
the noun. This is what makes English academic prose sound academic.

| Verb | Noun form to reach for |
|---|---|
| decide | **the decision to** |
| introduce | **the introduction of** |
| grow | **growth in** |
| rely | **reliance on** |
| regulate | **the regulation of** / *strict regulation* |
| expand | **the expansion of** |
| fail | **the failure to** |
| lose | **the loss of** |

### Before and after

> **Before:** *Governments regulate emissions strictly, and this has cut air
> pollution.*
> **After:** ***Strict regulation** of emissions has cut air pollution.*

You have gained register, you have gained a noun you can now refer back to (*Such
regulation…*, *This decline…*, which pays you under Coherence and Cohesion), and you
have removed a clause — and with it a chance to make an agreement error.

### Your errors

- ✗ the decision **of building** a new line → ✓ the decision **to build** a new line
- ✗ the reliance **of** rural clinics **of** central funding → ✓ the reliance **of** rural clinics **on** central funding
- ✗ a growth **of** the population → ✓ growth **in** the population
- ✗ The introduction **of the** charge **have** reduced traffic → ✓ The introduction of the charge **has** reduced traffic

⚠ **The tax on this one is specific to you.** Every nominalisation creates a **new
article decision** — *governments regulate* has no article problem, *the strict
regulation of emissions introduced in 2019* has one. Articles are your most expensive
error. **Fix articles first, then nominalise.** Run §9 on every noun you create.

## 8. CLEFT SENTENCES

Split one ordinary sentence in two so that the part you most want noticed sits under
a lamp.

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| ***It is/was*** X ***that/who*** … | *It is the shortage of qualified teachers, rather than of buildings, that limits access to education in rural areas.* |
| ***What*** … ***is/was*** X | *What these figures do not show is how the cost is distributed across income groups.* |
| ***What matters most is*** … | *What matters most is not how much is spent but where it is spent.* |

Use it once — to state a thesis, or to land the point you most want taken from a body
paragraph.

### Your errors

- ✗ What matters **are** the outcomes → ✓ What matters **is** the outcome (*What matters* is singular)
- ✗ It is the teachers who **limits** access → ✓ It is the teachers who **limit** access
- ✗ It is because of the cost **which** applications fell → ✓ It is because of the cost **that** applications fell

⚠ **One per essay, no more.** Three clefts stops reading as control and starts
reading as a memorised template — which is a scored penalty, not a style opinion.

## 9. ARTICLES — the one that decides your band

The full **drop-list** and **keep-list** are on the error card — see
`playbook/04-speaking-language.md` §Articles and §The nine uncountables, and
`book/appendix-C-error-card.md`. Do not learn them twice. What belongs here is the
**procedure**, which you run on the **head noun** — the one noun the phrase is
actually about, once you strip everything piled in front of and behind it.

```
STEP 1 — Is the head noun COUNTABLE?
   NO  (information, advice, research, equipment, evidence, knowledge,
        feedback, software, furniture, traffic, education, pollution)
        → NEVER "a/an". NEVER a plural -s. Go to STEP 2.
   YES → go to STEP 2.

STEP 2 — GENERIC (the class as a whole) or SPECIFIC (this one)?
   GENERIC   plural countable → ZERO      "Governments should invest in railways."
             uncountable      → ZERO      "Education reduces poverty."
             → then go to STEP 4 for the exceptions.
   SPECIFIC  → STEP 3.

STEP 3 — FIRST mention or LATER mention?
   LATER (or identifiable another way) → "the"
      Identifiable if ANY of these is true:
        • second mention          • an "of"-phrase follows    the impact of X
        • a defining relative     • a superlative or ordinal  the highest figure
        • unique in context       • a rate/proportion noun    the proportion of X
   FIRST → singular countable → "a/an"
           plural countable   → ZERO
           uncountable        → ZERO

STEP 4 — Is it on the KEEP-list?
   the government · the environment · the media · the economy · the internet ·
   the public · the police · the climate  (full list on the error card)
      → "the", even when generic. This step overrides STEP 2.
```

The five slots that account for most of what you actually get wrong:

1. `the + NOUN + of + NOUN` almost always takes **the** — *the number of students*,
   *the impact of technology*, *the role of government*. That is most of a Task 1 opening.
2. Superlatives and ordinals take **the** — but not after a possessive: *its highest level*.
3. **Generic plural is zero however long the modifier.** *Young people in rural areas
   are…*, not *The young people…*.
4. Percentages and dates take **no** article — *in 2010*, *20 per cent of students* —
   but *the percentage of students*, *the 1990s*.
5. **Second mention takes the.** *A congestion charge was introduced in 2003. **The**
   charge raised £2bn.* Free accuracy, and it buys you cohesion too.

## 10. SUBJECT–VERB AGREEMENT

The traps that actually catch you:

| Subject | Verb | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **people** | *people **are** · people **depend*** | already plural, never *peoples* |
| **the government** | *the government **has*** | *the government **have*** is fine in British English — leave it alone |
| **the number of** X | ***is*** | *The number of students **is** rising.* |
| **a number of** X | ***are*** | *A number of studies **have** shown…* |
| **each / every** + noun | ***is / has*** | *Each of the three regions **has** its own authority.* |
| **information / advice / research / evidence** | ***is / shows*** | *Recent research **shows**…* |
| **the majority / proportion of** X | agrees with **X** | *The majority of students **live** at home.* |
| a verb **inside a relative clause** | agrees with its own subject | *A child who **spends** three hours online **reads** less.* |

### Your errors

- ✗ **Peoples are depend** on public transport → ✓ **People depend** on public transport
- ✗ Technology **play** an important role → ✓ Technology **plays** an important role
- ✗ He **do not** agree → ✓ He **does not** agree
- ✗ The number of students **have** risen → ✓ The number of students **has** risen
- ✗ Each of the regions **have** its own budget → ✓ Each of the regions **has** its own budget
- ✗ Every student **need** support → ✓ Every student **needs** support
- ✗ Recent **researches show** → ✓ Recent **research shows**
- ✗ **Informations are** widely available → ✓ **Information is** widely available
- ✗ The evidence **suggest** otherwise → ✓ The evidence **suggests** otherwise
- ✗ A child who **spend** three hours online → ✓ A child who **spends** three hours online
- ✗ The government **are decide** to act → ✓ The government **has decided** to act

## 11. PUNCTUATION THAT COSTS MARKS

Punctuation is scored **inside grammar**, not as presentation. A comma splice damages
two clauses at once, so it costs you two sentences, not one.

### The comma splice — and its three fixes

Two complete sentences joined by a bare comma. Both your first languages encourage
it: Arabic builds by chaining, French builds long.

**Fix 1 — full stop.** Safe, free, always available.
**Fix 2 — semicolon.** Only when the two halves are tightly paired.
**Fix 3 — subordinator** (*because · although · while · since · whereas*). **Prefer
this one**: it is the only repair that converts a punctuation error into a complex
structure. Every splice in your draft is a complex sentence you failed to build.

- ✗ *Public transport in the capital is heavily subsidised, this has kept fares low
  for two decades, many commuters have therefore abandoned their cars.*
  ✓ *Because public transport in the capital is heavily subsidised, fares have stayed
  low for two decades, and many commuters have abandoned their cars.*

- ✗ *University fees have doubled since 2010, however the number of applicants has
  continued to rise, this suggests that cost is not the main barrier to entry.*
  ✓ *University fees have doubled since 2010. However, the number of applicants has
  continued to rise, which suggests that cost is not the main barrier to entry.*

⚠ *however · therefore · moreover · furthermore · nevertheless · thus · consequently*
are **adverbs**. They cannot join two clauses. Full stop or semicolon before them,
comma after.

### Two more, both cheap

**No comma before *that*.**
- ✗ *The report shows, that emissions have fallen.* → ✓ *The report shows that emissions have fallen.*

**Comma after a fronted adverbial** — anything you put before the main subject.
- ✗ *In the first decade of the century car ownership doubled.* → ✓ *In the first decade of the century**,** car ownership doubled.*
- ✗ *Although fees have risen applications continue to grow.* → ✓ *Although fees have risen**,** applications continue to grow.*

## 12. SENTENCE VARIETY — the 4 shapes

The same idea, four ways. No new vocabulary appears in any of them. This is what
"range" means, and you already own all of it.

**1. Simple** — one clause. Error-free by construction, and a legitimate instrument.
> *Online learning has widened access to higher education.*

**2. Compound** — two equal clauses joined by *and · but · so*.
> *Online learning has widened access to higher education, but many rural students
> still lack a reliable connection.*

**3. Complex** — one clause made to depend on the other by a subordinator.
> *Although online learning has widened access to higher education, many rural
> students still lack a reliable connection.*

**4. Complex with a fronted element** — the same, with something moved to the front
for focus.
> *In rural areas, where connections remain unreliable, the access promised by online
> learning is largely theoretical.*

Rotate the four. An essay of nothing but shape 3 reads as mechanical; an essay of
nothing but shape 1 is capped however clean it is.

## 13. THE 6-STEP PROOFREAD

Last three minutes. Do **not** read for meaning — you will read what you meant, not
what you wrote. Read for one error type at a time, in this order, because this is the
order of what each one costs you.

1. **Every *the* (50 s).** Abstract noun, talked about in general, nothing attached
   after it? **Delete it** — unless it is on the keep-list.
2. **Every comma (50 s).** Cover the left half: is the right a complete sentence?
   Cover the right: is the left? Two complete sentences with a bare comma between them
   is a splice — repair with a subordinator if you can, a full stop if you cannot.
   Then check every *however / therefore / moreover*: full stop before it.
3. **Every bare singular noun (20 s).** No article, no possessive, no quantifier? It
   needs *a/an* or *the*. And anything in the frame `___ of ___` almost certainly
   takes *the*.
4. **Verbs (30 s).** Singular subject in the present simple — is the *-s* there,
   including inside relative clauses? Two bare verbs stacked (*are depend*, *do not
   works*)? A passive on an intransitive verb (*was happened*, *was risen*)?
5. **The nine uncountables + *people* (10 s).** Any of them carrying an *-s*? Delete
   it, then fix the verb behind it.
6. **Your two longest sentences only (20 s).** *Although…but*? Delete the *but*.
   *Despite* + clause? Rewrite. *which…it* / *who…them*? Delete the pronoun. Participle
   clause — is the word right after the comma the thing doing the participle? *Would*
   in an *if*-clause? Change it to the past simple.

**The rule that makes all six work: in these three minutes you may delete and repair.
You may not add.** The clever sentence written at minute 39 is untested, and one
faulty complex sentence re-instates the band-6 limiter you spent forty minutes
avoiding.
