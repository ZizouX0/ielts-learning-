# TASK 2 — the language bank

**Use this while you write. Every line is a thing you can type as it stands.**
Square brackets `[ ]` are your slots. `⚠` = risky, read the warning.
`✗ → ✓` = an error your French or Arabic will produce if you are not watching.

The 18 moves and the 12 starters live in `03-writing.md`. This is the detail
behind them.

---

## 1. The five question types, and what each demands

| The instruction line you will actually see | What the essay must contain | The trap |
|---|---|---|
| **"To what extent do you agree or disagree?"** · "Do you agree or disagree?" · "What is your opinion?" | One clear position, held from the intro to the conclusion. Two developed reasons for it. One concession. | *To what extent* invites "I partly agree" — then you write one paragraph each way and never adjudicate. Partial is fine, but **state it as a rule**: "This holds for X but not for Y." |
| **"Discuss both these views and give your own opinion."** | Three things: view A developed, view B developed, **your own view**. | Reporting like a journalist — "Some say X, others say Y" — and your opinion arrives as a seven-word clause at the end. Put it in the intro's last sentence. |
| **"What are the causes and what measures could be taken?"** · "Why is this happening and what can be done?" | Named causes, then remedies that **attack those exact causes**, keyed back by name. | Mismatched halves: causes are poverty and weak enforcement, solutions are "raise awareness". Nothing joins. Costs you Task Response *and* Coherence. |
| **"What are the advantages and disadvantages?"** | Both sides described. A verdict is optional but safe. | Treating it as the *outweigh* version and forgetting to describe; or vice versa. |
| **"Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?"** | Both sides **plus an explicit judgement of weight, with a named criterion**. | Listing three of each, then "in conclusion there are both advantages and disadvantages". That is a missing demand — the band-5 cap. |
| **"Why is this happening? Is it a positive or negative development?"** (two-part / direct) | Two different answers to two different questions. Body 2 must **evaluate**, not add more causes. | Answering question 1 twice. Causes are easy, evaluation is hard, so body 2 quietly becomes another cause paragraph. |

**The 30-second triage.** Find the last sentence with a question mark or an
imperative. Count the separate demands in it. That number is how many things
must be *visibly* on your page. Hybrid prompts exist — obey the demand count,
never the family name.

## 2. Paraphrasing the prompt

Change the **nouns** and the **verb**. Keep the proposition identical. Do not
change everything — a paraphrase that shifts the meaning puts you off-topic,
which is worse than copying.

| Prompt word | Swap it for |
|---|---|
| *Some people believe / think* | It is widely held that… · A common view is that… · One school of thought maintains that… · There is a widespread belief that… |
| *Some people argue / claim* | It is often argued that… · Some commentators contend that… · A frequent claim is that… |
| *Many people* | A large number of people · A significant proportion of the public · Many observers |
| *Some / others* | One group holds that… , while another maintains… · Opinion divides between those who… and those who… |
| *should* | ought to · would be better placed to · there is a case for [+ -ing] · it would be preferable for X to… |
| *nowadays / these days / in modern society* | in recent decades · over the past twenty years · increasingly · at present |
| *problem / issue / concern* | difficulty · challenge · a growing concern · a phenomenon (neutral) · a trend (neutral) |
| *a lot of / more and more* | a rising number of · a growing proportion of · an increasing share of |
| *the government* | policymakers · the authorities · the state · public bodies |
| *children / young people* | the young · adolescents · school-age children · the younger generation |

**Eight full patterns you can type over any prompt:**

1. > *It is widely held that [proposition]. [This essay will argue that / In my view] [your position].*
2. > *Opinion is divided over whether [proposition]. [Position].*
3. > *[Trend] has become increasingly common over the past two decades, and there is disagreement about whether [question].*
4. > *A frequent claim is that [X]; others counter that [Y].*
5. > *Whether [X] should [verb] is now a live question in many countries.*
6. > *The rapid spread of [X] has prompted debate about [question].*
7. > *Some argue for [noun phrase], on the grounds that [reason]. I find this [convincing / only partly convincing].*
8. > *There is a strong case for [X], though it is weaker than its supporters suggest.*

⚠ Never lift a whole clause. Copied rubric earns nothing, is discounted from
your word count, and is used as evidence that you cannot paraphrase.
⚠ Do not open with *"In this contemporary era of rapid globalisation…"*. That
is memorised language and it is listed as a marker of a low band.

## 3. Stating your position — at three strengths

Put it in **the last sentence of the introduction**, in one unambiguous
sentence.

**STRONG agreement**

> *I agree entirely with this view, for two reasons.*
> *In my view this is not only true but urgent.*
> *There is little doubt that [X], and the case rests on [reason 1] and [reason 2].*
> *I would go further than the statement suggests: [stronger version].*
> *The argument for [X] seems to me decisive.*

**PARTIAL / qualified — use these most; they are safer and they score better**

> *This holds for [vocational subjects], but not for [the humanities].*
> *I agree with the principle, though not with the way it is usually applied.*
> *The claim is right about [causes] and wrong about [remedies].*
> *[X] is justified, but only where [condition] is already in place.*
> *I accept the diagnosis but not the prescription.*
> *This is largely true of [group A]; for [group B] the opposite applies.*

⚠ A qualified position must still be a **rule**, not a shrug.
✗ *There are arguments on both sides.* → ✓ *This is true where the alternative already exists, and false where it does not.*

**DISAGREEMENT**

> *I disagree with this view, although the concern behind it is legitimate.*
> *This argument mistakes [a symptom] for [a cause].*
> *I find this unpersuasive, chiefly because [reason].*
> *The evidence points the other way: [counter-claim].*
> *The proposal would not achieve what its supporters want, and might make matters worse.*

## 4. Topic sentences that carry a stance

A topic sentence must state the idea **and** signal your view. "Another factor
is…" is the band-6 signature.

> *The stronger reason for rejecting this claim is that [X].*
> *The most compelling argument in favour is [X], and it rests on [mechanism].*
> *What makes this view difficult to sustain is [X].*
> *The first of these causes is the easier to attack, because [reason].*
> *The objection is serious, but it is an argument about [timing] rather than about [cars].*
> *The clearest benefit, and the one that weighs most, is [X].*
> *This is where the argument is at its weakest: [X].*
> *A second and less obvious consequence is that [X].*

✗ *In the other hand, another point is the pollution.* → ✓ *On the other hand, the environmental cost is harder to dismiss.*

## 5. Giving a reason

> *…, because [mechanism].*
> *…, since [mechanism].* (formal, good sentence-initially: *Since costs fall on everyone, …*)
> *…, as [mechanism].* (⚠ *as* can also mean *when* — avoid it if the sentence could be misread)
> *This is largely owing to [noun phrase].*
> *This is due to [noun phrase].*
> *This is due to the fact that [clause].*
> *This stems from [noun phrase].*
> *The primary reason is that [clause].*
> *The explanation lies in [noun phrase].*
> *A large part of the answer is [noun phrase].*
> *[X] is the root of the difficulty.*

⚠ **due to vs because of.** *Due to* attaches to a **noun** (usually after
*is/was*): *The delay was due to congestion.* *Because of* attaches to a
**verb or a whole clause**: *He was late because of congestion.* If you cannot
tell, write **because of** — it is right far more often.
⚠ *Due to the fact that* is heavy. One per essay maximum; usually *because*
is better.

✗ *Because of the pollution is increasing, people are ill.* → ✓ *Because pollution is increasing, people are falling ill.* (*because of* + noun; *because* + clause)

## 6. Cause and effect — the full set

**CAUSE → EFFECT** (subject is the cause)

> *[Overcrowding] **leads to** [higher rents].*
> *[Automation] **results in** [job displacement in routine sectors].*
> *[Rapid urbanisation] **gives rise to** [pressure on services].*
> *[The policy] **brought about** [a measurable fall in traffic].*
> *[Screen time] **contributes to** [declining physical activity].* (partial cause — safe, hedged)
> *[The closure] **triggered** [a wave of departures].* (sudden, sharp events only)
> *[Poor transport] **drives** [car dependency].*
> *[The reform] **paved the way for** [wider access].*

**EFFECT ← CAUSE** (subject is the effect)

> *[The shortage] **stems from** [decades of under-investment].*
> *[This resentment] **arises from** [a sense of unfairness].*
> *[The gap] **is attributable to** [differences in early schooling].*
> *[Rising obesity] **is a consequence of** [cheap processed food and sedentary work].*
> *[The decline] **can be traced back to** [the closure of the local industry].*
> *[This behaviour] **is rooted in** [insecurity rather than preference].*

**Nouns — use these to compress a whole clause into a phrase**

> *a driver of [X]* · *the principal driver of [X]*
> *a root cause* · *the underlying cause*
> *a knock-on effect* · *a ripple effect*
> *the fallout from [X]*
> *a side effect* · *an unintended consequence*
> *a feedback loop* (each departure makes the next more likely)
> *a trade-off*

✗ *Pollution gives bad effects to the environment.* → ✓ *Industrial emissions place sustained strain on air quality.*
✗ *This problem is caused from bad management.* → ✓ *This problem is caused **by** poor management.*

## 7. Developing an idea — the four moves

**claim → because → so what → example.** Roughly 100 words. This is the single
biggest band 6 → 7 lever in the whole paper. Band 6 *states* ideas; band 7
*extends and supports* them.

| Move | What it does | Type this |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | The idea, one sentence. Carries your stance. | *The stronger reason for [X] is that…* |
| **Because** | The mechanism. **Why** is it true? | *This is because… · The reason is that… · …, since… · which means that…* |
| **So what** | The consequence. Who gains, who loses, what follows. | *The consequence is that… · As a result… · What follows is that… · The effect is felt most by…* |
| **Example** | One concrete instance. Your own knowledge. | *For instance… · Take [X]… · A [student] who [situation] will…* |

⚠ You may **invent** your examples. Nobody verifies them. No specialist
knowledge is required and the rubric invites examples from your own experience.
⚠ But **no invented statistics.** *A significant minority* scores the same as
*63.4%* and cannot embarrass you.

### Worked example 1 — Education

> **(claim)** The strongest argument against narrowing university provision is
> that the labour market a degree is designed for does not yet exist.
> **(because)** Curricula built around today's vacancies age badly, because the
> occupations they train for are precisely the ones most exposed to automation
> and offshoring. **(so what)** The consequence is that the most narrowly
> trained graduates are the first to need retraining, while those taught to
> argue, weigh evidence and write can move sideways. **(example)** A translation
> graduate I know found her specific technical skill automated within a decade,
> but the analytical training underneath it transferred without difficulty.

### Worked example 2 — Environment

> **(claim)** Congestion charging is the most defensible of the available
> measures. **(because)** This is because it prices the cost a driver imposes on
> everyone else — the delay, the noise, the particulates — which no fuel tax
> reaches. **(so what)** What follows is that traffic falls without anyone being
> banned, and the revenue can be redirected into the alternative that makes the
> charge tolerable. **(example)** In cities where the charge was introduced only
> after the bus network had been extended, public resistance faded within a few
> years; where it came first, it did not.

### Worked example 3 — Work

> **(claim)** The clearest cost of home working, and the one employers
> underestimate, is the loss of informal training. **(because)** Junior staff
> learn largely by overhearing — a half-finished negotiation, a senior colleague
> repairing a mistake — and that channel does not survive scheduled video calls.
> **(so what)** The effect is cumulative rather than immediate: the damage shows
> up several years later, in a generation of employees who were never
> apprenticed. **(example)** A graduate joining a law firm remotely may complete
> her tasks competently for two years and still not know how a client is
> handled when something goes wrong.

## 8. Giving examples

> *For instance, [example].*
> *For example, [example].*
> *A case in point is [X].*
> *Take [Tunisia], for example: [what it shows].*
> *Consider [the case of a graduate who…].*
> *This can be seen in [countries that deregulated early].*
> *As can be seen in [X], [what it shows].*
> *particularly in countries where [condition holds]*
> *especially among [households with the least slack in their week]*
> *A [student] who [situation], for example, will [outcome].*
> *[X] is a good illustration of this.*
> *One need only look at [X].*

⚠ Write **for example**, never *e.g.* An examiner logged *e.g.* as a lapse in
an 8.5 script. Same for *etc.* → *and so on*.
⚠ Two concrete instances across the essay is enough. An example in every
sentence is padding.

## 9. Adding and extending

> *Moreover, [X].*
> *In addition, [X].*
> *What is more, [X].*
> *Furthermore, [X].*
> *Beyond this, [X].*
> *A further consideration is [X].*

⚠ **Maximum two markers per paragraph — and never zero.** Overuse is a named
fault at band 7 *and* band 6. Four sequencers in one paragraph got an official
7.5 script marked down.

**Extend without a new marker. Do this instead:**

- **Relative clause:** *Rents rise fastest in exactly those districts **where new building is hardest**.*
- **Participle clause:** *Cities absorb people faster than they can house them, **pushing rents beyond local wages**.*
- **Reference:** *That mismatch shows up first in rents.* · *These pressures do not ease on their own.*
- **Substitution:** *the former · the latter · one · so · doing so · the remaining group*
- **Colon:** *The cause is not mysterious: the jobs are clustered and the housing is not.*
- **Semicolon:** *One is reversible; the other is not.*

✗ *Moreover, the pollution is a problem, furthermore the government must act.* → ✓ *Pollution is only half the difficulty; the response to it is the other half.*

## 10. Contrast and concession — the band-7 move

**CONTRAST**

> *However, [X].*
> *By contrast, [X].*
> *Conversely, [X].*
> *On the other hand, [X].*
> *[X], whereas [Y].*
> *[X], while [Y].*
> *Yet [X].*
> *In reality, [X].*

✗ *In the other hand* → ✓ **On the other hand.** ⚠ Check this one every time.
⚠ *However* is not a conjunction. ✗ *He tried, however he failed.* → ✓ *He tried; however, he failed.* / *He tried. However, he failed.*

**CONCESSION**

> *Although [X], [Y].*
> *Even though [X], [Y].*
> *While it is true that [X], [Y].*
> *Admittedly, [X]. But [Y].*
> *Granted, [X] — even so, [Y].*
> *Despite [noun phrase], [Y].*
> *Despite the fact that [clause], [Y].*
> *It would be wrong to deny that [X].*
> *There is real force in this objection.*

✗ *Although many people believe this, **but** it is wrong.* → ✓ *Although many people believe this, it is wrong.* (English allows one, not both)
✗ *Despite of the cost* → ✓ *Despite the cost* / *In spite of the cost*
✗ *Despite the cost is high* → ✓ *Despite the high cost* (*despite* + noun, not clause)

**CONCEDE-THEN-COUNTER — five full patterns. This is what makes an argument look mature.**

1. > *While [the objection] is undeniably true, it is an argument about [timing] rather than about [the measure itself].*
2. > *There is real force in this objection; it applies, however, only where [condition], and that condition is becoming rarer.*
3. > *Admittedly, [concession]. That cost is real, but it is [temporary / narrow / recoverable], whereas [the benefit] is [permanent / shared].*
4. > *Supporters of this view are right that [X]. Where the argument fails is in assuming that [unstated assumption].*
5. > *[Concession] is often raised, and it deserves an answer rather than a dismissal: [answer].*

⚠ Concede **once**, then counter. A concession without a counter is a lost
paragraph.

## 11. Hedging — free marks

An examiner named softeners of exactly this kind as evidence of higher-level
vocabulary in a 7.5 script. It raises Lexical Resource **and** kills band 7's
named weakness (over-generalising) in the same word.

> *tend to* · *tends to be* · *are likely to* · *may well* · *can* (not *will*)
> *in most cases* · *in many countries* · *broadly speaking* · *by and large*
> *often* · *typically* · *generally* · *for the most part*
> *arguably* · *it could be argued that* · *there is a case for saying that*
> *this suggests* · *this points to* · *appears to be* · *seems to*
> *a significant minority* · *a large proportion* · *few, if any*

**Before → after**

| ✗ Overgeneralised | ✓ Hedged |
|---|---|
| *Everyone uses social media all day.* | *Most adults now spend a substantial part of the day online.* |
| *Poor students always fail at university.* | *Students from low-income households tend to be at a disadvantage, and in many cases drop out earlier.* |
| *Technology destroys jobs.* | *Automation is likely to displace routine work faster than it replaces it.* |
| *Living in a city makes people unhappy.* | *City life appears to carry costs to wellbeing that are easy to underestimate, particularly for those on low incomes.* |

⚠ Do not hedge your **position**. Hedge the generalisations inside it. A
position hedged into invisibility is a band-6 position.

## 12. Emphasising

> *Crucially, [X].*
> *Above all, [X].*
> *Most importantly, [X].*
> *particularly in [context]* · *especially where [condition]*
> *What matters most is [X].*
> *It is precisely this that [makes the measure worth taking].*
> *The decisive point is that [X].*
> *This is the heart of the matter.*
> *It is worth stressing that [X].*
> *Indeed, [stronger restatement].*

⚠ Do **not** use *very*, *really*, *absolutely*, *totally*, or capital letters,
or an exclamation mark. Emphasis in academic English is lexical, not loud.

## 13. Weighing and judging (for "outweigh" questions)

A verdict must **name the criterion** it rests on. "Overall I think the
advantages are more important" is asserted, not weighed.

> *On balance, the advantages outweigh the drawbacks.*
> *The benefits outweigh the costs, though not by as wide a margin as is often claimed.*
> *The drawbacks are real, but they are outweighed by [X].*
> *While [X] carries a genuine cost, [Y] is the greater consideration.*
> *The two are not comparable in weight: one is [reversible], the other is not.*
> *The first cost is temporary and local; the second is permanent and shared.*
> *The gains are structural and compounding, while the losses are a training problem firms can deliberately solve.*
> *Taking both sides together, the case for [X] is the stronger.*
> *The balance tips towards [X], chiefly because [criterion].*

**Criteria you can name in one clause** — use one of these and a band-6 verdict
becomes a band-7 one:

*permanent vs temporary* · *shared vs narrow* · *reversible vs irreversible* ·
*affects more people* · *falls on those least able to bear it* ·
*compounding over time* · *solvable by policy vs not*

## 14. Proposing solutions

> *Governments should [verb], because [why it attacks the cause].*
> *One measure would be to [verb].*
> *A more effective approach would be to [verb].*
> *This could be addressed by [+ -ing].*
> *This is best tackled at the level of [X] rather than [Y].*
> *Legislation requiring [employers to X] would [effect].*
> *Subsidising [X] would remove the incentive that produces [Y].*
> *The most practical step is [X], and it is also the cheapest.*
> *Schools could be required to [verb], which would [effect].*
> *A guaranteed [service floor] below which closure is not permitted would break the loop.*
> *Redirecting [revenue from X] into [Y] would make the measure politically survivable.*

⚠ **Every solution must answer a cause you named**, and must say so:
> *The first of these causes is the easier to attack, because [reason].*
> *Neither of these remedies touches the second cause, which is why [X] is also needed.*

⚠ Name **the actor, the instrument and the mechanism** — who does what, and why
it changes the cause.
✗ *The government should raise awareness and people must be more responsible.* → ✓ *Municipal authorities could make separated collection the default, since households recycle at far higher rates when doing so requires no extra effort.*

## 15. Conclusions

> *In conclusion, [verdict], because [the criterion the verdict rests on].*
> *To sum up, the case for [X] rests less on [Y] than on [Z].*
> *On balance, then, [verdict] — provided that [condition].*
> *The argument therefore comes down to [criterion], and on that test [X] wins.*
> *[X] is worth doing, but only alongside [Y]; on its own it will disappoint.*
> *The problem is tractable, but only if the remedies are aimed at the causes named above rather than at the symptoms.*
> *In short, [position restated in new words], and the reason is [criterion].*
> *If [the condition] holds, [verdict]; if it does not, the objection stands.*

⚠ **No new ideas.** A conclusion that introduces an argument is an
undeveloped paragraph.
⚠ **Restate in different words.** A conclusion that repeats the introduction is
"repetitive", which is a named band-6 fault.
⚠ Give a **reason**, not a summary. Band 6 conclusions say *there are advantages
and disadvantages*; band 7 conclusions say *why one side weighed more*.

## 17. Idea banks for the same eight themes

Four **for**, four **against**, one line each, ready for the four-move
treatment. This is what stops you staring at a blank screen.

### Education
**FOR (more state involvement / broad education)**
- An educated population raises productivity for everyone, not just the graduate.
- Broad training survives occupational change; narrow training does not.
- Free or subsidised access breaks the link between a family's income and a child's ceiling.
- Universities produce research that no private firm has an incentive to fund.

**AGAINST**
- Public funding is finite, and every subsidised place is a place not funded elsewhere.
- Degrees increasingly signal ability rather than teach skills employers actually need.
- Expanding numbers has devalued the qualification and lengthened the queue.
- Vocational routes are cheaper, faster and closer to real vacancies.

### Technology
**FOR**
- Automation removes dangerous and repetitive work that nobody wants to do.
- Access to information no longer depends on living near a library or a good school.
- Remote tools widen the hiring pool beyond the commuting distance of a capital city.
- Diagnostic and monitoring technology catches illness earlier and cheaply.

**AGAINST**
- The gains accrue to those who already have connectivity, widening existing gaps.
- Routine jobs disappear faster than displaced workers can be retrained.
- Constant availability erodes the boundary between working and living.
- Recommendation systems narrow what people see rather than broadening it.

### Environment
**FOR (regulation / individual restraint)**
- Environmental damage is an externality: the polluter does not pay the cost.
- Some damage is irreversible, so caution is worth more than it looks.
- Early action is cheaper than adaptation later.
- Green industries create employment as older sectors contract.

**AGAINST / the objection**
- Restrictions fall hardest on low-income households, who spend the largest share on energy.
- Unilateral limits simply relocate production to countries with weaker rules.
- Individual behaviour is trivial beside industrial and state emissions.
- Growth is what funds the technology that eventually solves the problem.

### Health
**FOR (state responsibility / prevention)**
- Prevention costs a fraction of treatment and reaches people earlier.
- Ill health is not evenly distributed; it tracks income and postcode.
- A healthy workforce is an economic asset, not merely a moral good.
- Individuals cannot regulate the food industry, but governments can.

**AGAINST**
- Personal responsibility matters; no policy can make anyone exercise.
- Taxes on food and drink are regressive and hit the poorest hardest.
- Public systems ration by waiting time, which has its own human cost.
- Ageing populations make universal free provision arithmetically harder every year.

### Work
**FOR (flexibility / worker protection)**
- Removing the commute returns hours to precisely the households with least slack.
- Flexible hours keep parents and carers in the labour market instead of out of it.
- Secure contracts make workers willing to train and to speak up.
- Overwork lowers output per hour; rested employees produce more.

**AGAINST**
- Informal mentoring does not survive scheduled calls; juniors lose most.
- Flexibility for the employer often means insecurity for the employee.
- Weak dismissal rules make firms readier to hire in the first place.
- Home working shifts costs — heating, space, equipment — onto the worker.

### Crime
**FOR (rehabilitation / social causes)**
- Certainty of being caught deters far more than severity of punishment.
- Prison without training produces reoffenders at public expense.
- Most acquisitive crime tracks unemployment and inequality, not leniency.
- Early intervention with young offenders is the cheapest point to break the cycle.

**AGAINST (deterrence / punishment)**
- Sentences express public condemnation, and that has value beyond deterrence.
- Incapacitation works: an offender in custody commits no street crime.
- Victims are entitled to proportionate punishment, not only offender welfare.
- Lenient sentencing undermines confidence in the system and encourages private retaliation.

### Globalisation
**FOR**
- Trade lowers prices, and the largest benefit goes to those with the least income.
- Access to foreign markets lets small economies specialise instead of doing everything badly.
- Movement of workers transfers skills in both directions.
- Cultural exchange has enriched food, music and language everywhere it has happened.

**AGAINST**
- Gains are national averages; the losses are concentrated in particular towns.
- Local industries cannot compete with subsidised imports and do not recover.
- Skilled emigration drains exactly the people a developing economy needs most.
- Global brands displace local practice faster than any deliberate policy could.

### Media
**FOR (free press / broad access)**
- Scrutiny of power is impossible without an independent press.
- More sources make it harder for any single account to go unchallenged.
- Citizen reporting covers places professional journalists never reach.
- Public broadcasting sets a quality floor that commercial pressure would remove.

**AGAINST**
- Attention is what sells, so alarming stories crowd out important ones.
- Coverage shapes what people fear far more accurately than it shapes what they know.
- Anonymity online lowers the cost of spreading falsehood to almost nothing.
- Concentrated ownership means "many outlets" can still mean one voice.

## 18. Words to stop using, and what to replace them with

| ✗ Band-6 default | ✓ Replace with |
|---|---|
| *nowadays / in today's world* | in recent decades · over the past twenty years · increasingly (or delete it) |
| *big problem* | a serious difficulty · a persistent challenge · a growing concern |
| *good / bad* | beneficial · damaging · valuable · counterproductive |
| *very important* | crucial · decisive · central to · what matters most is |
| *a lot of / lots of* | a large number of · a substantial proportion of · considerable |
| *thing / stuff* | factor · feature · aspect · consideration · element |
| *people* (repeated) | households · workers · residents · commuters · parents · the public |
| *society* (repeated) | communities · the wider population · public life |
| *get* | obtain · acquire · receive · reach |
| *make better* | improve · strengthen · enhance |
| *make worse* | aggravate · worsen · compound |
| *more and more* | a rising number of · an increasing share of |
| *I think / in my opinion I think* | in my view · I would argue that (**pick one**, never both) |
| *we can say that / it is a fact that* | *(delete — say the thing)* |
| *firstly, secondly, thirdly* at every head | *(keep one; carry the rest with reference and substitution)* |
| *In conclusion I want to say that* | In conclusion, [verdict], because… |
| *the youth are* | young people are |
| *this essay will talk about* | this essay argues that [position] |
| *give bad effects to* | place strain on · damage · undermine |
| *the environment problem* | environmental damage · the environmental cost |
| *develop the country* | raise living standards · build infrastructure · grow the economy |

⚠ **A wrong upgrade scores lower than the plain word.** Band 6 is *defined*
partly by the risk-taker who reaches for wider vocabulary at the cost of
accuracy. If you are not certain of the collocation, use the plain word.

**False friends that will appear in your Task 2 essays**
✗ *significative* → ✓ **significant**
✗ *alimentation* → ✓ **diet / nutrition**
✗ *formation* → ✓ **training / education**
✗ *evolution* (meaning a trend) → ✓ **change / development**
✗ *inconvenient* (as a noun) → ✓ **drawback / disadvantage**
✗ *society* (meaning a firm) → ✓ **company**
✗ *actually* (meaning currently) → ✓ **at present / currently**
✗ *eventually* (meaning possibly) → ✓ **possibly / if necessary**
✗ *assist to a lecture* → ✓ **attend a lecture**
✗ *permit to do* → ✓ **allow people to do / permit people to do**
✗ *sensible* (meaning sensitive) → ✓ **sensitive**
✗ *deception* (meaning disappointment) → ✓ **disappointment**
✗ *support* (meaning tolerate) → ✓ **tolerate / put up with**
✗ *demand* (meaning ask) → ✓ **ask**
✗ *achieve* (meaning finish) → ✓ **complete / finish**

**Prepositions the argument itself will trip you on**
✗ *depend of* → ✓ depend **on** · ✗ *discuss about* → ✓ discuss **Ø** ·
✗ *participate to* → ✓ participate **in** · ✗ *responsible of* → ✓ responsible
**for** · ✗ *access to the internet* (as a verb) → ✓ access **Ø** the internet ·
✗ *prevent somebody to do* → ✓ prevent somebody **from doing** ·
✗ *consist in* → ✓ consist **of** · ✗ *suffer of* → ✓ suffer **from** ·
✗ *interested by* → ✓ interested **in** · ✗ *in the other hand* → ✓ **on the
other hand**

**Articles — the scan that decides 6 from 7**
✗ *the society · the technology · the education · the nature · the pollution ·
the crime · the poverty* → ✓ drop the article when the noun is general.
✓ **Keep** the article in: *the government · the environment · the media ·
the economy · the internet · the public · the police · the countryside ·
the climate*.
✓ **Keep** it whenever something after the noun says *which one*: *the education
system of Tunisia · the crime rate · the poverty that this policy creates*.

**Uncountables — check every noun ending in -s**
✗ informations · researches · advices · knowledges · equipments · furnitures ·
softwares · evidences · feedbacks · peoples (unless you mean ethnic groups) ·
gossips → ✓ information · research · advice · knowledge · equipment ·
furniture · software · evidence · feedback · people · gossip.
Say *a piece of research*, *some advice*, *two pieces of evidence*.

## 19. Register — what not to write

| ✗ Do not write | ✓ Write |
|---|---|
| *don't · can't · won't · it's · doesn't* | do not · cannot · will not · it is · does not |
| *You should exercise more.* | People should exercise more. · Individuals ought to… |
| *kids · guys · a huge amount of · a bit* | children · people · a substantial number of · slightly |
| *e.g.* | for example |
| *etc.* | and so on |
| *&* | and |
| *Isn't it obvious that pollution is bad? Who benefits? Why do we allow this?* | *(rhetorical question chains: delete. A question is not a position.)* |
| *This is terrible!* | This is difficult to defend. *(no exclamation marks — not one)* |
| *Bullet points · sub-headings · numbered lists* | Continuous prose. Note form is penalisable anywhere in the response. |
| *ALL CAPS · bold for emphasis* | Lexical emphasis: *crucially · above all · what matters most* |
| *Let's look at…* · *Now I will discuss…* | *(delete — just write the paragraph)* |
| *In my opinion I think that…* | In my view… *(pick one)* |
| *Stats I invented: "73% of students…"* | A significant minority of students… |

⚠ One contraction will not cap you — an official 7.5 script used *don't* three
times. But they buy nothing and cost a little, so keep avoiding them. **Do not
spend exam minutes hunting contractions while a question part sits
unanswered.**

## The 60-second pre-submit scan

1. Position stated in the intro's **last sentence**?
2. One paragraph per demand — count them against the instruction line.
3. Every topic sentence carries a **stance**, not just a topic?
4. Four moves in each body paragraph — claim, because, so what, example?
5. **Articles**: every *the* before a bare abstract noun. And every abstract
   noun on the counter-list that has lost one.
6. **Plurals**: information · research · advice · knowledge · evidence ·
   equipment · people — any of them wearing an *-s*?
7. **Commas**: any comma with a complete sentence on both sides.
8. **-s**: every present-simple verb with a singular subject.
9. Discourse markers: no paragraph with more than two, none with zero.
10. *On the other hand*, not *in the other hand*.

⚠ In these last three minutes you may **delete and repair. Never add.** A
sentence written at minute 39 is unproofread by definition.
