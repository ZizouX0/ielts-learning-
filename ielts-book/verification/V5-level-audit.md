**Why I can't act on it:** The entire strategic core of this book is Ch1's argument that
I need "exactly two more half-bands", bought in Listening and Reading. That argument only
becomes a study plan if I know what a half-band costs in raw marks. Ch1 tells me nobody

2. Ch2 and Ch3 print **the same** interpolated figures or none. Pick one source.
   If IDP's table is good enough for Ch3 it is good enough for Ch2; if it is not, delete
   both and give me the operating rule instead.
3. Replace both tables with the operating rule the book already believes, stated as a
   target: **"Treat 30/40 as band 7 and aim for 32. Do not plan around a 6.5 threshold

### [MAJOR] — The highest-value pronunciation drill in the book depends on "schwa" and on IPA, neither of which is explained

**Chapter:** 06 §Band descriptor decoder, §Drill 4, §Drill 7

**The book says:**
> "with *the, should, a, in, it* compressed to **schwa** and three clear beats."
>
> "English compresses unstressed syllables to **schwa** so stressed beats stay evenly
> spaced."
>
> "*would have* must be crushed to **/wʊdəv/**, *the* to **/ðə/**."
>
> "English reduces it (*asked* is often **/ɑːst/**) but does not delete it."

**Why I can't act on it:** Ch6 tells me — correctly and convincingly — that sustained
rhythm is "your single most efficient route out of Pronunciation 6", and that band 7 is
band 6 plus one band-8 feature. Then the drill that buys it is written in a notation I
cannot read and turns on a term I have never met. I do not know what a schwa is. I do not
know how to pronounce /wʊdəv/, /ðə/, /ɑːst/, /θ/ or /ð/. French schooling did not teach me
IPA. So the rubber-band drill reduces, for me, to "say these three lines in the same
amount of time", which I can half-do, and the compression instruction — which *is* the
drill — is lost.

**Fix:** Three additions to Ch6, all short:
1. **Define schwa in one line at first use**, in plain terms plus a French anchor: "the
   *schwa* is English's neutral unstressed vowel — the sound at the end of *sofa*, the *a*
   in *about*, the *e* in *the*. It is close to the French *e* in *le* and *je*. English
   crushes almost every unstressed syllable into it; French does not, which is exactly
   why your rhythm sounds even."
2. **Respell every IPA form in ordinary letters alongside the symbols**: */wʊdəv/ — say
   it as "wud-uv"*; */ðə/ — "thuh", not "thee"*; */ɑːst/ — "ahst"*. Keep the IPA for
   anyone who wants it; add the crutch for the reader who does not.
3. **Rename the drill or add the rubber band.** It is called "the rubber-band drill" and
   contains no rubber band — the instruction is to tap three beats on the table. Either
   restore the elastic (stretch a band on each stressed beat) or call it the three-beat
   drill, because a reader hunting for the equipment loses two minutes and confidence.

## The three things I'd actually change first

**1. Put a two-page "How to read this book" in front of Chapter 1, and move all nine
60-second summaries to the top of their chapters.**

This is the highest-leverage change by a wide margin, and it is nearly free. The book
currently contains a 9-minute-a-day theory rule and eight hours of theory, with no
reconciliation and no reading order. Every one of my "could I find the three things that
matter in two minutes?" tests failed for the same reason: the summary is at the end, and
there is no map. Moving the summaries is a cut-and-paste. The front matter needs a day-zero
reading block that is explicitly outside the 9-minute budget, and a table saying which
section feeds which day of the weekly rotation. Without this, the reader most likely to
benefit from 70,000 words is the reader least likely to reach them.

**2. Fix the raw-score contradiction, then collapse the seven duplicate L1 tables into one
canonical home each.**

These are one job because they are both about single-sourcing. The half-band contradiction
across Ch1/Ch2/Ch3 is the only defect in the book that could make me plan badly — it sits
directly under the strategic argument the whole book is organised around, and it makes the
book's own self-test question unanswerable. It needs one policy, applied three times. The
duplicate false-friend and uncountables tables are the same disease in a milder form: eight
printings of the same content, which is both a large share of the overrun *and* the reason
I started skimming the L1 alerts, which is where this book's real advantage lives. Keep the
best table once, replace the other seven with a pointer plus the skill-specific consequence
— which is the only part that differs and the only part I need.

**3. Close the three solo-execution gaps: audio sources, the schwa/IPA barrier, and the
spaced-repetition mechanism.**

Chapter 6 sets the standard here — it names what a book cannot do and hands me a procedure
anyway — and three other places fall short of it. Chapter 2 tells me to train five accents
and to have words dictated back, and names no source and no dictator. Chapter 6's own
highest-value drill is written in IPA and turns on "schwa", a word I do not know. Chapter 7
gives me a five-interval ladder for 200 cards and no way to track it, while this repository
ships an `/anki` command that does exactly that and is never mentioned. Each of these is a
paragraph of work, and each converts a piece of good advice from something I nod at into
something I do on Monday. Fix them in that order — the audio one first, because Chapter 1's
own data says Listening is the Arabic-L1 blind spot, and Chapter 2 is currently the least
executable chapter in the book.
