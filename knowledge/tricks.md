# Exam technique reference

Everything mechanical that earns or loses marks. Format and scoring facts here
are verified — see `knowledge/sources.md`.

**Delivery mode assumption:** computer-delivered. IELTS stops offering paper from
mid-2026. The differences that change technique are flagged 🖥️.

---

# 1. LISTENING

4 parts × 10 questions = 40. ~30 minutes. Parts 1–2 are everyday/social;
Parts 3–4 are educational/training. Part 4 is usually a single academic lecture
with no break.

🖥️ **On computer there is no 10-minute transfer time — you get 2 minutes to
check.** On paper you got 10. This is the biggest practical change: you must be
accurate *as you type*, not tidy up afterwards. Train this way.

## The rules that decide your score

**Answers come in order.** Always. If you are hunting for Q17 and hear something
that answers Q19, you have missed 17 and 18 — abandon them, mark your best guess
later, and lock on to 19. Chasing a missed answer backwards costs you the next
three.

**Predict the answer type before the audio starts.** Use the reading time on
every gap. For each blank decide: is it a *number*, *date*, *name*, *place*,
*noun*, *verb*, *adjective*? Singular or plural? A gap reading
"The library closes at ______" can only take a time. You are now listening for
one thing instead of everything.

**The correction trap.** Speakers change their minds, and the first number you
hear is often wrong. Listen for the reversal signal: *sorry, actually, I mean,
no wait, let me check that again, or rather, in fact*. The answer is what comes
**after** the correction.
> "The deposit is fifty pounds — sorry, that's the *monthly* rent. The deposit's
> **a hundred and twenty**." → 120.

**Rejected-option distractors** (multiple choice). All three options are usually
mentioned. Two get rejected. Listen for rejection language: *we considered X
but*, *X wasn't really an option*, *originally we planned X, however*, *I'd
have preferred X*. Hearing a word is not the same as it being the answer.

**Spelling costs marks.** Misspelled = wrong, even if you heard it perfectly.
Names and unusual words are spelled out — write letters as you hear them.
Watch double letters (*accommodation*, *committee*), and *-tion/-sion*.

**Plurals cost marks.** If you hear "two tickets" and write *ticket*, it is
wrong. Decide singular/plural during prediction, then confirm from the audio.

**Word limits are absolute.** "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER" means
three words scores zero even if the meaning is right. Do not add articles that
were not needed. Hyphenated words count as one word. Contracted forms are not
tested.

**Never leave a blank.** No negative marking. Guess something plausible of the
right type.

## Spelling list — words that recur and get misspelled

accommodation · questionnaire · restaurant · February · Wednesday · necessary ·
separate · definitely · receipt · brochure · itinerary · vehicle · colleague ·
maintenance · equipment · environment · government · beginning · committee ·
recommend · occurrence · library · foreign · schedule · laboratory · temperature ·
psychology · rhythm · queue · aisle

**Numbers:** thirteen/thirty, fourteen/forty, fifteen/fifty — the stress moves
(thir-**TEEN** vs **THIR**-ty). Train this specifically.
**Dates:** accept 3 March / 3rd March / March 3. **Money:** copy the symbol.

## Part-by-part

- **Part 1** — form completion, personal details. Easiest marks in the test. Zero
  errors expected. Phone numbers, addresses, spellings of names.
- **Part 2** — monologue, often a map/plan or a tour description. Orientation
  language matters: *bear left, opposite, adjacent to, at the far end, beyond,
  behind, in the corner of*.
- **Part 3** — 2–4 speakers discussing academic work. Hardest for distractors and
  for tracking *who* thinks what.
- **Part 4** — academic lecture, no pause in the middle. Note-completion. Follow
  the signposting (*firstly, turning now to, the second factor*).

# 2. READING (Academic)

3 passages, 40 questions, **60 minutes, no extra transfer time**. 2,150–2,750
words total. Question types are mixed within each passage.

*(Prep sites routinely say the passages get progressively harder. No official
source states this, so do not plan around it — plan around the clock instead.)*

## Time budget

Cambridge's own guidance is **about 20 minutes on each passage**. That is the
official baseline. The uneven split below is a personal tactic, not an official
rule — it buys time for Passage 3 by compressing Passage 1, which works only if
Passage 1 really is easier for you. Use whichever you can hold to.

| Passage | Even split (official) | Front-loaded (tactic) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 min | 16 min |
| 2 | 20 min | 19 min |
| 3 | 20 min | 21 min |
| Check/guess | — | 4 min |

Set a hard stop. Overrunning Passage 1 by ten minutes to secure two marks costs
you five in Passage 3. If a question resists after 90 seconds, guess and move.

## The core method: scan, don't read

You do not have time to read 2,700 words properly. You have time to read them
*purposefully*.

1. **60 seconds skimming** — title, first and last sentence of each paragraph.
   Build a rough map of what lives where.
2. **Read the questions.** They tell you what to look for.
3. **Anchor on keywords** that are hard to paraphrase: proper nouns, dates,
   numbers, technical terms, capitalised words. These survive paraphrasing and
   are your fastest route into the text.
4. **Read intensively only around the anchor** — the sentence before, the
   sentence, the sentence after.

**The paraphrase principle.** The answer is almost never in the same words as the
question. The test *is* the paraphrase. Question says *reduce costs*; text says
*curtail expenditure*. Train yourself to look for the idea, not the string.

## Playbook per question type

**True / False / Not Given** (about facts in the text)
- **True** — the text states it, in different words.
- **False** — the text states the **opposite**. There must be a contradiction.
- **Not Given** — the text does not say. Absence of information.

The trap is False vs Not Given. Ask: *can I point to the sentence that
contradicts this?* If yes → False. If you are reasoning "well, it probably
isn't, because..." → Not Given. **Never use outside knowledge.**

**Qualifier traps.** Watch *all, always, never, only, the most, exclusively,
majority*. But be careful which way a vague quantifier cuts — this is subtler
than most guides admit, and getting it backwards costs marks:

- **Vague word in the text, precise claim in the statement → NOT GIVEN.** If the
  text says *numerous* or *many* and the statement asserts a specific proportion
  or *all*, the text has not made that claim — it has said something looser. No
  contradiction exists, so it is Not Given, **not** False. This is what an
  official worked example gives.
- **Scope mismatch → FALSE.** The text makes a claim about one thing and the
  statement transfers it to another, or reverses it. Now there *is* a
  contradiction.

The test remains the same: **point to the contradicting sentence.** If you cannot
put your finger on it, the answer is Not Given regardless of how the quantifiers
look.

**Yes / No / Not Given** — identical logic, but about the **writer's opinions and
claims**, not facts. Look for opinion language: *arguably, it seems likely,
critics maintain, the author contends*.

**Matching Headings** — do this **before** detailed questions; it forces you to
map the passage. Read the first and last sentences of the paragraph; the topic
sentence usually carries the heading. Cross off headings as you use them.
Distinguish the paragraph's **main idea** from a detail mentioned in it — the
commonest error is picking a heading that matches one sentence.

**Matching Information to paragraphs** — the slowest type; answers are **not** in
order, and one paragraph may hold two answers or none. Do it **last**.

**Multiple Choice** — read the stem, predict the answer, *then* look at options.
Eliminate: options that are true but don't answer the stem, options that
overstate, options containing a word from the text used differently.

**Sentence completion** — answers **are** in passage order. Predict the grammar
of the gap first. Obey the word limit absolutely.

**Summary / note / table / flow-chart completion** — answers are **NOT**
guaranteed to be in passage order (only *sentence* completion is). Official
wording is that they may not come in the same order as the text. If words come
from a box they may be paraphrased; if taken "from the passage" you must copy
them exactly, spelling included.

**Matching Features** (e.g. researchers to findings) — options may be reused
**only when the instructions say so**. Assume no reuse unless told otherwise.
Scan for the proper nouns first, then read around each.

**Short answer** — obey the word limit, take words from the passage.

## Guessing strategy

No negative marking — never leave a blank. For MCQ with four options, eliminate
two and pick.

**On T/F/NG, ignore the folklore that "Not Given is the safest guess."** No
official data supports it, and believing it actively trains you to reach for Not
Given before you have finished searching — which turns findable Trues and Falses
into misses. Search properly; guess only when the clock forces you to, and then
guess whichever your partial reading actually supports.

# 3. WRITING TASK 1 (Academic)

≥150 words, ~20 minutes. Describe visual information in your own words. Worth
**one third** of the Writing band.

## Structure — 4 paragraphs, no more

1. **Introduction (1 sentence).** Paraphrase the question. Change the nouns and
   the verb, keep the data. Never copy the prompt — copied text is excluded from
   your word count.
2. **Overview (2–3 sentences). MANDATORY.** The biggest patterns, no numbers.
   What is highest, lowest, rising, falling, and what changed most. **Without an
   overview you cannot reach band 7 on Task Achievement.** Signal it: *Overall,*
3. **Body 1 (3–4 sentences).** A logical group with supporting figures.
4. **Body 2 (3–4 sentences).** The rest.

Aim 170–190 words. **No conclusion. No opinions. No causes.** You are not
allowed to explain *why* — the data does not tell you, and speculation costs
Task Achievement marks.

## Group data; never list it

Band-5 writing walks along the x-axis: *In 1990 it was 10. In 1991 it was 12. In
1992 it was 15.* Band-7 writing groups: *The three European countries followed
the same upward path, whereas both Asian markets contracted after 2005.*

Group by: shared trend · similar magnitude · biggest vs smallest · the outlier.

## Language toolkit

**Increase:** rise · climb · grow · surge · soar · rocket · escalate · pick up
**Decrease:** fall · decline · drop · plunge · plummet · dip · tail off · slump
**Steady:** remain stable/constant/unchanged · level off · plateau · hold steady
**Change direction:** peak at · bottom out · fluctuate · recover · rebound

**Degree:** dramatically · sharply · steeply · markedly · significantly ·
moderately · gradually · slightly · marginally
**Speed:** rapidly · swiftly · steadily · slowly

**Both grammatical shapes — alternate them:**
- verb + adverb: *Sales rose sharply.*
- there + be + adjective + noun: *There was a sharp rise in sales.*

**Comparison:** twice as many as · three times higher than · roughly half ·
accounted for X% of · the highest/lowest figure · compared with · whereas ·
by contrast · while · outstripped · lagged behind
**Approximation:** approximately · around · just over/under · nearly · almost ·
in the region of · a little more than

**Proportions:** a quarter · a third · two thirds · the vast majority · a
significant minority · one in five · a negligible proportion

**Process diagrams:** sequencers (*initially, subsequently, following this, at
this stage, once X has been completed, finally*) + **passive voice**
(*the mixture is heated*, *the components are assembled*). Count the stages, name
the start and end points, say whether it is linear or cyclical.

**Maps over time:** past/present perfect + passive (*a car park was constructed*,
*the woodland has been cleared*), plus compass and position language
(*to the north of, adjacent to, on the outskirts, in the south-east corner, was
replaced by, was converted into, remained untouched*).

**Tables with no time dimension:** compare across categories, not over time.

## Checklist before you stop

- [ ] Overview present, and it actually summarises?
- [ ] ≥150 words (count them)?
- [ ] Figures quoted accurately, units and dates right?
- [ ] Data grouped rather than listed?
- [ ] Tense correct for the time frame?
- [ ] No opinion, no cause, no conclusion?
- [ ] Prompt paraphrased, not copied?

# 4. WRITING TASK 2

≥250 words, ~40 minutes. **Worth double Task 1.** If you are short of time,
protect Task 2.

## The five question families

Getting this wrong is the most expensive error in the test, because it wrecks
Task Response — the criterion that is hardest to recover.

> **What these families are and are not.** IELTS publishes **no** official
> taxonomy of Task 2 question types. This five-way split is a recognition
> heuristic, and a good one — but it is not a rule handed down by the test
> makers. The two organisations that run IELTS don't even agree with each other:
> the British Council's teaching pages give five categories, IDP's give six, and
> they do not map onto each other cleanly. Use the families to make sure you have
> spotted every part of the question. Do not treat a prompt that fits none of
> them as broken — read what it actually asks.

**1. Opinion (agree/disagree).** *To what extent do you agree or disagree?*
→ Pick **one** clear position and hold it to the end. A wobbling answer is
band 6. You may concede a point, but your position must be unmistakable.

**2. Discussion (both views).** *Discuss both views and give your own opinion.*
→ Two body paragraphs, one per view, **plus your own opinion, stated clearly.**
The commonest band-6 failure in the whole test: discussing both sides well and
tacking on the opinion as a final clause. Give it real weight.

**3. Problem / cause and solution.** *What are the causes and what measures could
be taken?* → Body 1 = causes, Body 2 = solutions, and the **solutions must
address the causes you named**. Mismatched halves cost Task Response and
Coherence.

**4. Advantages / disadvantages.** *Do the advantages outweigh the
disadvantages?* → If it says *outweigh*, you must **weigh** and deliver a
verdict. Listing three of each and stopping does not answer the question.

**5. Two-part / direct question.** *Why is this happening? Is it a positive or
negative development?* → **Answer both parts, in separate paragraphs, with equal
weight.** Half-answering is an automatic band 6 on Task Response.

**Before writing, spend 5 minutes:** underline the topic, the task word, and
every question part. Write your position in one sentence. List two main ideas
with one example each. Then write — you will not have to think and phrase at the
same time.

## The blueprint

```
Introduction (2–3 sentences, ~45 words)
  Paraphrase the prompt · state your position/roadmap
Body 1 (~90 words)
  Topic sentence · explanation of WHY · example/consequence · link
Body 2 (~90 words)
  Topic sentence · explanation of WHY · example/consequence · link
[Body 3 — only if the question genuinely has a third part]
Conclusion (2 sentences, ~40 words)
  Restate position in new words · no new ideas
```

**Target 260–290 words** — but understand what the limit actually is.

There is a **floor and no ceiling**. An official examiner-marked exemplar scoring
**7.5** runs to roughly **375 words**. So length itself is not the risk; what
costs you is spending words you then have no time to check, since accuracy is
where band 7 is decided. Write as long as you can write *accurately*.

Under 250 words is not an arithmetic penalty either — IELTS publishes none, and
the May 2023 descriptors contain no deduction. What actually happens is that a
short essay cannot have fully addressed the prompt, so **Task Response** drops,
and it shows less range, so **Lexical Resource** and **Grammar** drop too. The
only published hard threshold is that 20 words or fewer scores band 1.

## Develop, don't assert

This is what separates 6 from 7 more than any other single thing. Band 6 states
ideas. Band 7 *extends* them.

> **Asserted (band 6):** Online learning is convenient for students.
> **Developed (band 7):** Online learning removes the commute, which for students
> in rural Tunisia can consume two hours a day. That reclaimed time goes into
> study rather than travel, so the same course delivers more actual learning —
> particularly for students who also work.

The pattern: **claim → because → so what → concrete instance.**

## Band-7 killers

1. **A question part left unanswered.** Fatal for Task Response.
2. **Undeveloped paragraphs** — three claims, no reasoning.
3. **Memorised openers.** *In this contemporary era of rapid globalisation, it is
   an undeniable fact that...* Examiners see it hundreds of times; it counts for
   nothing and signals a template.
4. **Linker spam.** An examiner logged **four sequencers in a single paragraph**
   of a band-7.5 essay as "some overuse". Treat **two discourse markers per
   paragraph** as your ceiling. Note that under-use is also penalised — a band
   6.5 script was docked for too few.
5. **Informal register** — real, but **less fatal than most guides claim**. That
   same official band-7.5 script uses *don't* three times, writes "In my opinion,
   I think", and contains outright errors. It still scored 7.5. Tidy your
   register, but do not believe a contraction has cost you the band; undeveloped
   ideas cost far more. (One thing that *was* docked, on an 8.5 script: writing
   "e.g." instead of "for example".)
6. **Under length** — costs you through Task Response and reduced evidence of
   range, not through a published penalty. See the length note above.
7. **Thesaurus abuse.** *Ameliorate the pecuniary situation* instead of *improve
   finances*. Wrong collocation lowers Lexical Resource.
8. **Overgeneralising.** *All young people are addicted to phones.* Hedge:
   *many, tend to, are likely to, in most cases*.

## Worked skeleton

> *Some people believe university education should be free for all students.
> Others think students should pay. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.*

- **Intro:** Paraphrase. Position: *funding should be means-tested — free for
  those who cannot pay, contributory for those who can.*
- **Body 1 (free education view):** Claim — removes financial barriers →
  because talent is distributed evenly across income but opportunity is not →
  so the state loses capable graduates it could have had → instance: capable
  students entering work at 18 for family income reasons.
- **Body 2 (fee-paying view):** Claim — fees fund quality and shift cost to those
  who benefit → because graduates earn a measurable premium → so free education
  is a transfer from non-graduate taxpayers to future high earners.
- **Conclusion:** Both have force; means-testing captures the strength of each.
  Restate in fresh words.

# 5. SPEAKING

11–14 minutes, three parts, face-to-face with an examiner, recorded.

## Part 1 — Introduction and interview (4–5 min)

Familiar topics: home, family, work, study, hobbies. Warm-up, but it is marked.

**Answer + Reason + Example.** Two to three sentences. Never one word; never a
90-second monologue.

> **Q:** Do you enjoy cooking?
> **✗ Band 5:** Yes, I like it.
> **✓ Band 7:** I do, though mainly at weekends — during the week I rarely get
> home before eight. On Sundays I'll cook properly, usually something Tunisian
> like a couscous, because it's the one meal the whole family sits down for.

## Part 2 — Long turn (3–4 min including prep)

Cue card, **1 minute preparation**, then speak **1–2 minutes** uninterrupted.

**Use the prep minute properly.** Do not write sentences — you will read them
aloud and sound flat. Write **keywords only**, one line per bullet, plus one
detail you can expand on. The bullets are your structure; follow them in order.

**Fill the time.** Stopping at 40 seconds hurts Fluency. If you run dry, extend
with: *how I felt about it · how it compares to X · whether I'd do it again ·
what someone else thought · why it stayed with me.*

**Invent freely.** Nobody verifies your story. A vivid invented answer beats a
true but thin one.

## Part 3 — Discussion (4–5 min)

Abstract questions related to Part 2's topic. This is where band 7 is won or
lost — it demands opinion, analysis, speculation and comparison.

**Toolkit:**
- **Speculating:** *It's likely that... · I'd imagine... · That could well
  lead to... · Presumably... · There's a good chance that...*
- **Conceding then countering:** *That's true up to a point, but... · I can see
  why people say that, although... · Granted, ... even so, ...*
- **Comparing:** *Whereas in the past... · Compared with a generation ago... ·
  Unlike in Europe, in Tunisia... · By contrast...*
- **Generalising carefully:** *Broadly speaking... · As a rule... · In most
  cases... · There are exceptions, but generally...*
- **Buying thinking time:** *That's an interesting question. · I've not thought
  about that before, but I'd say... · It depends largely on...*
  (Use sparingly — one or two per test, not per answer.)

**Expect pushback.** *But couldn't you argue the opposite?* This is not hostility
— it is the examiner testing whether you can defend or refine a position. Do
both: concede what is fair, then hold your ground with a reason.

## Why memorised answers fail

Examiners are trained to spot them, and they are penalised. Memorised speech
gives itself away: register jumps to written-formal, delivery flattens, and the
answer drifts off the actual question. Learn **flexible frameworks and
collocations**, never scripts.

## Pronunciation: intelligibility, not accent

Your Tunisian/French accent costs nothing — the descriptor mentions accent only
at bands 8 and 9, and only to say it has minimal or no effect there.

The 2025 descriptor rewrote this criterion and named the features that matter.
**Work them in this order** — the first two are worth more than the rest
combined:

1. **Speech rate.** Band 6 explicitly names *a rapid speech rate* as something
   that wrecks rhythm. Speaking fast to sound fluent is a band-6 marker in the
   official text. Fluency is *continuity*, not speed.
2. **Stress-timing.** English compresses unstressed syllables between stressed
   beats; Arabic and French do not. This is named in the descriptor and is your
   cheapest route out of Pronunciation 6 — band 8's named feature is *sustained
   rhythm*.
3. **Chunking** — grouping words into sense units and pausing at the boundaries,
   not mid-phrase.
4. **Word stress** placement (*com-FOR-table*; pho-TO-graph / PHO-to-graph /
   photo-GRAPH-ic all differ), and resisting **French final-syllable stress**.
5. **Intonation that is sustained**, not occasionally right. The 6→8 difference
   is consistency, not capability.
6. **Individual sounds**, last — they cost you only when a word becomes
   unrecognisable: /θ/ and /ð/ (*think*, *this*), /h/ dropped or added.

**Two corrections to the standard advice, specific to you:**

- **/p/ vs /b/ is probably not a missing sound for you.** French supplies /p/, so
  you have it. The likely issue is **aspiration** — English /p/ at the start of a
  stressed syllable comes with a puff of air that French /p/ lacks, which is what
  makes *pear* sound like *bear*. Hold a strip of paper in front of your mouth:
  it should move on *pin*, not on *bin*. That is a drill, not a lifelong problem.
- **Ignore the usual "Arabic speakers break up initial consonant clusters"
  advice** (*istudy*, *ispring*). That is built on Modern Standard and Gulf
  Arabic phonotactics. **Tunisian Arabic permits two-consonant onsets**, so this
  is likely not your error at all. Your real difficulty is English **final**
  clusters — *asked*, *texts*, *twelfths*, *strengths*.

**Record yourself on your phone** for every Part 2 you practise, and listen back.
Text-based coaching cannot hear you — this is the one part of the system you must
close yourself.

# 6. SCORING MECHANICS

**Listening & Reading:** 40 questions, 1 mark each, no negative marking.

| Band | Listening | Academic Reading |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 5 | 16 | 15 |
| 6 | 23 | 23 |
| 7 | 30 | 30 |
| 8 | 35 | 35 |

Conversion varies slightly between test versions — targets, not guarantees.

**Writing:** (Task 1 + 2 × Task 2) ÷ 3.

**Speaking:** four criteria, equally weighted.

**Overall:** mean of the four skills, rounded to the nearest half band. .25
rounds up to the next half band; .75 rounds up to the next whole band.
Example: 6.5 / 6.5 / 5.0 / 7.0 → 6.25 → **6.5**.

**Routes to 7.0 overall** — you do not need 7 everywhere:

| L | R | W | S | Avg | Reported |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7.5 | 7.5 | 6.0 | 6.5 | 6.875 | **7.0** |
| 7.0 | 7.0 | 6.5 | 6.5 | 6.75 | **7.0** |
| 8.0 | 7.0 | 6.0 | 6.5 | 6.875 | **7.0** |
| 6.5 | 6.5 | 6.5 | 6.5 | 6.5 | 6.5 ✗ |

The lesson: **Listening and Reading carry you.** They are objective, trainable,
and 30/40 is a technique problem rather than a language problem. Writing moves
slowest — start it first and work it longest.

## Exam-day mechanics

- Listening 🖥️ no transfer time, 2 minutes to check. Type as you go.
- Reading: no transfer time at all. Never leave a blank.
- Writing: do **Task 2 first** if you tend to run out of time — it is worth
  double. Otherwise keep to 20 / 40.
- Word count 🖥️ displayed on screen. Check it; do not eyeball it.
- Speaking may be on a different day from the other three papers.
